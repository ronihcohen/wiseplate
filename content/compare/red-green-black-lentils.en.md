## A Scientific Comparison: Red, Green and Black Lentils

**All lentils are almost equally nutritious — about 9 g of protein per 100 g cooked — and the difference between them is mainly culinary: red lentils cook in 15–20 minutes, fall apart into soup and are the easiest to digest; green and brown lead on fiber and suit stews like mujadara; and black (beluga) lentils hold their shape in a salad and are richest in antioxidants.**

**Names on the package:** red lentils are also sold as **Split red lentils** or **Red split lentils** (they are actually orange); black lentils are often labeled **Beluga lentils**, and French green lentils as **Puy lentils**. In Hebrew, lentils are "עדשים", and red lentils are called both "עדשים כתומות" (orange lentils) and "עדשים אדומות" (red lentils). The [full table of names](#lentil-names) is below.

[Lentils](/en/food/lentil/) (*Lens culinaris*) are among the oldest domesticated foods in the world — their remains have been found at sites about 11,000 years old in the Middle East, and they appear in the Book of Genesis in the story of Jacob and Esau's lentil stew. They are cheap, relatively quick to cook, and provide protein, fiber and [iron](/en/food/iron/) in a single serving. A meta-analysis of controlled trials found that eating pulses — on average about one serving a day — lowered LDL cholesterol modestly but significantly. [Ha et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24710915/).

### Lentils — carbohydrate or protein?

This is the most common question, and the answer: **lentils are a legume, so they're both — but in terms of calories, they're mainly carbohydrate.**

Per 100 g of cooked lentils:

| Component | Amount | Share of calories |
|---|---|---|
| **Carbohydrates** | 20.1 g | **~67%** |
| **Protein** | 9.0 g | **~30%** |
| Fat | 0.4 g | ~3% |
| Of the carbohydrates — fiber | 7.9 g | — |

Two important caveats:

1. **About 40% of the carbohydrates are fiber** (7.9 out of 20.1 g) — fiber isn't absorbed and doesn't raise blood sugar. The carbohydrate actually available is about 12 g per 100 g, much lower than [rice](/en/food/rice/) or potato.
2. **9 g of protein per 100 g cooked** is a high amount for a plant food — more than in [quinoa](/en/food/quinoa/) and similar to [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/).

**Protein quality:** Lentil protein is rich in lysine and low in methionine; grains are the opposite. That's why the traditional pairing of lentils with rice — mujadara, dal with rice, khichdi — creates a complete amino acid profile. The combination doesn't have to be in the same meal, just over the course of the day.

**Iron:** The iron in lentils is non-heme iron, which is absorbed less well than iron from meat. Vitamin C in the same meal — lemon, tomato, bell pepper — improves its absorption. [Source: NIH ODS, Iron](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/).

### 1. Red/Orange Lentils (Red Lentils)

Lentils whose skin has been removed and that have been split in two. The orange color is the inside of the seed.

**Benefits:**
* **Speed:** They cook within 15–20 minutes without soaking.
* **The easiest to digest:** Without the skin they contain less insoluble fiber and usually cause less gas. Good for people who get bloated from legumes, for toddlers and for people recovering from illness.
* **Texture:** They break down completely into a purée — ideal for thick soup, Indian dal and spreads.

**Downsides:**
* **Less fiber:** Removing the skin removes a considerable share of the fiber.
* **A slightly higher glycemic response** than lentils with skin, because they break down and digest faster — still low compared with other starchy foods.
* **They don't hold their shape:** Not suitable for salads.

### 2. Green/Brown Lentils

The classic and most common lentil — "mujadara lentils". The whole seed with its skin.

**Benefits:**
* **The most fiber:** The skin is kept, and with it most of the fiber.
* **The cheapest** in most stores.
* **Versatility:** They hold their shape in most cooking, but release enough starch to thicken a stew.
* **A low glycemic load** thanks to the skin and fiber.

**Downsides:**
* **Long cooking time:** 30–45 minutes, preferably after soaking.
* **More gas:** The skin and the oligosaccharides cause more digestive discomfort. Soaking, changing the cooking water and gradual adaptation reduce this.

**A premium variation:** French *Puy lentils* — small green-gray lentils from the Le Puy region, with an especially firm texture and a mineral flavor. They cost more and behave in the kitchen like black lentils.

### 3. Black Lentils (Black Beluga Lentils)

Called "beluga" because they look like caviar.

**Benefits:**
* **Anthocyanins:** The black color of the skin comes from anthocyanins — the same family of antioxidants found in [blueberries](/en/food/blueberries/) and dark grapes. This is the only notable nutritional difference between lentil types.
* **Texture:** They keep their shape and bite even when fully cooked — the best choice for cold salads and side dishes.
* **A deeper, nuttier flavor** than the other types.

**Downsides:**
* **Price:** Two to three times more expensive than green lentils.
* **Availability** is more limited.
* **They stain** light-colored dishes a purplish hue.

### Which lentil should you choose?

**The nutritional differences between lentil types are small, and they are all excellent food.** If you still want to choose by goal:

* **Soup, dal, spreads and a quick meal:** red.
* **Bloating from legumes, toddlers and people recovering from illness:** red.
* **The most fiber and blood sugar balance:** green/brown.
* **Mujadara and stews, and on a budget:** green/brown.
* **Cold salads, side dishes and good-looking plates:** black or Puy.
* **Antioxidants:** black.
* **A vegetarian diet:** any type, combined with grains over the day and a vitamin C source for iron absorption.

### How to reduce gas from lentils

1. **Soak** green and black lentils for 4–8 hours (red lentils don't need it).
2. **Pour off the soaking water** and cook in fresh water — the oligosaccharides that cause gas are water-soluble.
3. **Skim off the foam** that rises at the start of cooking.
4. **Cook thoroughly** until fully softened.
5. **Start with red lentils** and move gradually to types with skins.
6. **Adaptation:** For many people, symptoms ease after a few weeks of eating legumes regularly.
7. **Cumin or asafoetida:** Traditional spices for legumes. Evidence on their effect on gas is thin, but they are safe and tasty.

### An important cooking tip

**Don't add salt or acidic ingredients** ([tomatoes](/en/food/tomato/), [lemon](/en/food/lemon/), vinegar) at the start of cooking — they toughen the skin and lengthen the cooking time. Add them after the lentils have softened.

### Summary

Lentils provide about 9 g of protein and up to about 8 g of fiber per 100 g cooked, with a low glycemic load and at a low price. In terms of calories they are mainly carbohydrate (about 67%), but about 40% of that carbohydrate is fiber. The difference between the colors is mainly culinary: **red** for soup, dal and easy digestion; **green** for mujadara, stews and the most fiber; **black** for salads, firm texture and antioxidants.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Are lentils a carbohydrate or a protein?**
Answer: Both, but mainly carbohydrate. 100 g of cooked lentils contain 20.1 g of carbohydrates (about 67% of the calories) and 9 g of protein (about 30%). About 8 g of the carbohydrates are fiber that isn't absorbed, so the available carbohydrate is only about 12 g.

**Question: What's the difference between green and red lentils?**
Answer: Red lentils have had their skin removed and been split; green lentils are the whole seed. Red lentils cook in 15–20 minutes, break down into a purée and are easy to digest, but contain less fiber. Green lentils need 30–45 minutes, partly keep their shape and contain almost twice the fiber, but cause more gas.

**Question: Are split red lentils the same as red lentils?**
Answer: In practice, yes. Most red lentils are sold hulled and split, so "red lentils", "split red lentils" and "red split lentils" on a package usually mean the same product. Whole red lentils exist but are less common and take a little longer to cook.

**Question: What are beluga lentils?**
Answer: Small black lentils named for their resemblance to beluga caviar. They hold their shape well, cook in about 20–25 minutes and are rich in anthocyanins.

**Question: Which lentils are the healthiest?**
Answer: The differences are small and they're all excellent. Green/brown lead in fiber, black lead in antioxidants thanks to the anthocyanins, and red lead in ease of digestion.

**Question: Do you need to soak lentils before cooking?**
Answer: Red lentils — no. Green and black lentils — soaking for 4–8 hours is recommended: it shortens cooking, reduces phytates and improves tolerance. Pour off the soaking water and cook in fresh water.

**Question: How long do you cook lentils?**
Answer: Red 15–20 minutes (without soaking), black 20–25 minutes, green/brown 30–45 minutes. Don't add salt, tomatoes or lemon at the start of cooking — they toughen the skin and lengthen the cooking time.
