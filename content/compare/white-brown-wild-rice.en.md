Rice is a staple food for half of the world's population, and the question of which type is better comes up again and again. The short answer: **wild rice leads nutritionally, brown rice is the practical compromise, and there are cases where white rice is actually the right choice** — including one consideration, arsenic, in which brown rice is the worst of the three.

### Nutritional comparison table (per 100 grams cooked)

| Component | [White rice](/en/food/rice/) | Brown rice | Wild rice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calories | 130 | 112 | **101** |
| Protein | 2.7 grams | 2.6 grams | **4.0 grams** |
| Carbohydrates | 28 grams | 24 grams | **21 grams** |
| Dietary fiber | 0.4 grams | **1.8 grams** | **1.8 grams** |
| [Magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) | 12 mg | **43 mg** | 32 mg |
| [Zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/) | 0.5 mg | 0.6 mg | **1.3 mg** |
| Glycemic index | 72-89 | 50-68 | **45-57** |
| Inorganic arsenic | Relatively low | **The highest** | The lowest |
| Cooking time | 15 minutes | 40 minutes | 45-60 minutes |

### 1. [White Rice](/en/food/rice/)

The polished, most common rice — the bran and germ have been removed.

* **Benefits:** Immediately available energy (a real advantage for athletes before and after training); **especially easy to digest** because it lacks fiber and phytic acid, which is why it's recommended for upset stomachs, during IBS flare-ups and in low-FODMAP diets; long shelf life; **lower arsenic** than brown rice.
* **Downsides:** High glycemic index; very low in fiber, magnesium and B vitamins — unless it's fortified.
* **A point that is often missed:** The difference between varieties of white rice is greater than the difference between white and brown when it comes to glycemic index. **Basmati** stands at 50-58 — lower than regular brown rice — while **jasmine** reaches 100 and above. Anyone monitoring blood sugar will gain more from switching to basmati than from switching to brown rice.

### 2. Brown Rice

Rice that hasn't been polished and has kept its bran layer and germ.

* **Benefits:** 4.5 times the fiber of white rice; 3.5 times the [magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/); [vitamin B1](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b1/), B6 and [manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/); longer-lasting fullness and a lower glycemic index.
* **Downsides:** **Phytic acid** in the bran binds [iron](/en/food/iron/), [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/) and [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) and reduces their absorption — soaking for 8-12 hours before cooking reduces it significantly; long cooking time; **arsenic — the most significant downside**, as detailed below.

### 3. Wild Rice

It isn't rice at all, but the seed of an aquatic grass (*Zizania*) that grows in North American lakes.

* **Benefits:** **Almost twice the protein** of white or brown rice, and relatively complete protein in terms of its amino acid profile; the lowest in calories and carbohydrates; 2-3 times the [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/); much higher amounts of anthocyanins and [polyphenols](/en/food/polyphenols/) thanks to its dark color; **the lowest arsenic** of the three, because it isn't in the rice family and doesn't absorb arsenic the same way.
* **Downsides:** Significantly more expensive; a strong earthy flavor and chewy texture that aren't to everyone's taste; long cooking time.
* **A practical solution:** A blend of wild rice with brown or basmati rice — common commercially, it softens the flavor and lowers the price.

### Arsenic — the consideration that turns the picture around

This is the figure that is almost always missing from rice comparisons, and it's the only one in which brown rice comes out worse.

The rice plant grows in flooded fields and absorbs inorganic arsenic from the soil and water far more efficiently than other grains. **The arsenic concentrates in the bran layer** — exactly the part that brown rice keeps. The result: brown rice contains on average **about 80% more inorganic arsenic** than white rice from the same source.

Inorganic arsenic is classified as a definite human carcinogen (IARC Group 1), and chronic exposure has also been linked to diabetes and heart disease.

**What to actually do — three steps that work:**

1. **Cook in plenty of water and drain.** A ratio of 6-10 cups of water per cup of rice, cooking it like pasta and draining the excess, **removes 40%-60% of the inorganic arsenic**. This is by far the most effective step.
2. **Rinse well before cooking** — removes about another 10%-20%, and also gets rid of excess starch.
3. **Choose your source.** Basmati from India and Pakistan and rice from California are significantly lower in arsenic than rice grown in the southern United States. Wild rice is the lowest of all.

**For the right context:** for an adult who eats rice a few times a week, this isn't a risk that justifies avoiding it. The issue is more critical for babies and toddlers — rice cereals for infants have been a major source of exposure — and for those for whom rice is the everyday base of their diet.

### And after cooking — storage safety

Cooked rice left at room temperature is a breeding ground for *Bacillus cereus* spores, whose bacteria secrete a heat-resistant toxin that isn't destroyed by reheating. Cooked rice should be refrigerated within an hour, and reheated no more than once. The full details are in [the dangers of eating reheated rice](/en/food/reheated_rice_dangers/).

**Resistant starch — a bonus of cooling:** Cooling cooked rice overnight in the fridge turns some of the starch into resistant starch, which isn't digested in the small intestine and functions as dietary fiber that feeds gut bacteria. The result is a lower glycemic index, even after reheating. It's a simple way to improve white rice.

### Summary and conclusions

**Which rice is the healthiest?** Wild rice — the most protein, the fewest carbohydrates, the most antioxidants and the least arsenic. Its only problem is the price.

**What's practical for most people:** brown rice or basmati. Brown rice wins on fiber and minerals; basmati wins on glycemic index and low arsenic. A combination of the two is a good compromise.

**And when white rice is the right choice:** before or after training, during digestive distress, on a low-FODMAP diet, and when feeding toddlers — where the lower arsenic actually matters.

And in any case, regardless of type: **cook in plenty of water and drain.** That's the single step with the greatest impact.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Which rice is the healthiest?**
Answer: Wild rice. It provides almost twice the protein (4 grams per 100 grams cooked), the fewest calories and carbohydrates, the most anthocyanins, and the least arsenic. Its only downside is a significantly higher price. For everyday use, brown rice or basmati are the practical compromise.

**Question: Is brown rice really healthier than white rice?**
Answer: On most parameters, yes — 4.5 times the fiber, 3.5 times the magnesium, and a lower glycemic index. But it falls short on two: it contains about 80% more inorganic arsenic, because arsenic concentrates in the bran, and its phytic acid reduces the absorption of iron and zinc.

**Question: How much arsenic is in rice, and how do you reduce it?**
Answer: Rice absorbs inorganic arsenic from the soil very efficiently, and it concentrates in the bran — so brown rice contains on average about 80% more than white rice. Cooking in plenty of water (6-10 cups per cup of rice) and draining the excess removes 40%-60% of the arsenic, and rinsing beforehand removes another 10%-20%.

**Question: What is the glycemic index of rice?**
Answer: White rice 72-89, brown rice 50-68, wild rice 45-57. But the difference between varieties of white rice is bigger: basmati stands at 50-58 — lower than regular brown rice — while jasmine reaches 100 and above.

**Question: Is wild rice really rice?**
Answer: No. It's the seed of an aquatic grass of the genus Zizania that grows in North American lakes, and it isn't botanically close to rice. That's also why it doesn't absorb arsenic the same way and contains more protein.

**Question: How can you lower the glycemic index of white rice?**
Answer: Cool it in the fridge overnight after cooking. Some of the starch turns into resistant starch that isn't digested in the small intestine and functions as dietary fiber, and the effect holds even after reheating. Switching to basmati lowers the index even further.

**Question: Should you soak brown rice before cooking?**
Answer: It's worth it. Soaking for 8-12 hours significantly reduces the phytic acid in the bran, which binds iron, zinc and calcium and reduces their absorption. Soaking also shortens the cooking time and helps reduce arsenic.
