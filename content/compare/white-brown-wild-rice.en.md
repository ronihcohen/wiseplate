## A Scientific Comparison: White, Brown and Wild Rice

**Wild rice leads nutritionally — nearly 1.5 times the protein, fewer calories and as much fiber as brown rice — but it is expensive. Brown rice is the practical everyday choice for its fiber and magnesium, though it carries more arsenic. White rice, especially basmati, is the right choice before training, for digestive trouble and for toddlers. And whichever you choose, cooking it in plenty of water and draining roughly halves the arsenic.**

In Hebrew these are "אורז לבן", "אורז מלא" and "אורז פרא". Rice is a staple for half the world's population. The differences between the types come down to what is left of the grain: brown rice keeps its bran and germ, white rice has had them removed, and "wild rice" is a different plant altogether.

### Nutrition comparison (per 100 g cooked)

| Nutrient | [White rice](/en/food/rice/) | Brown rice | Wild rice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calories | 130 | 112 | **101** |
| Protein | 2.7 g | 2.6 g | **4.0 g** |
| Carbohydrates | 28 g | 24 g | **21 g** |
| Dietary fiber | 0.4 g | **1.8 g** | **1.8 g** |
| [Magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) | 12 mg | **43 mg** | 32 mg |
| [Zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/) | 0.5 mg | 0.6 mg | **1.3 mg** |
| Glycemic index (reported range) | 72–89 | 50–68 | 45–57 |
| Inorganic arsenic | Relatively low | **Highest of the three** | Little data |
| Cooking time | 15 minutes | 40 minutes | 45–60 minutes |

**About the glycemic index:** The international GI tables show that rice comes in high-, medium- and low-GI versions, depending on variety and processing. [Atkinson et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34258626/). As a rule, basmati is relatively low while jasmine and sticky rice are high — so the variety sometimes matters more than white versus brown.

### 1. White Rice

Polished rice, with the bran and germ removed.

**Benefits:**
* **Available energy:** A carbohydrate that digests quickly — an advantage for athletes before and after training.
* **Easy to digest:** Low in fiber and phytic acid, so it suits an upset stomach, an irritable-bowel flare and a low-FODMAP diet.
* **Less arsenic** than brown rice, and a long shelf life.

**Downsides:**
* **High glycemic index** in most varieties.
* **Low in fiber, magnesium and B vitamins,** unless it is enriched.

### 2. Brown Rice

Unpolished rice, with the bran layer and germ intact.

**Benefits:**
* **Fiber and minerals:** 4.5 times the fiber and 3.5 times the magnesium of white rice, plus more vitamin B1, B6 and manganese.
* **Fullness:** A lower glycemic index and a longer feeling of satiety.

**Downsides:**
* **Arsenic:** Inorganic arsenic concentrates in the outer layers of the grain, so brown rice usually contains more of it than white rice from the same source.
* **Phytic acid:** It binds iron, zinc and calcium and slightly reduces their absorption. Soaking before cooking reduces it partly.
* **Long cooking time.**

### 3. Wild Rice

Not rice at all, but the seed of a water grass (*Zizania*) from North America.

**Benefits:**
* **Protein:** About 4 g per 100 g cooked, nearly 1.5 times that of white or brown rice.
* **The lowest in calories and carbohydrates**, with twice the zinc.
* **Dark color:** The dark grain is richer in phenolic compounds than white rice.

**Downsides:**
* **Price:** Significantly more expensive.
* **Taste and texture:** Earthy and chewy, not to everyone's taste.
* **Cooking time:** The longest of the three.
* **A practical fix:** A blend of wild rice with brown or basmati rice softens the flavor and the price.

### Arsenic — and how to reduce it

The rice plant grows in flooded fields and takes up inorganic arsenic from soil and water more efficiently than other grains. The World Health Organization notes that inorganic arsenic is classified as carcinogenic to humans and that long-term exposure is also associated with cardiovascular disease and diabetes. [Source: WHO, arsenic](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/arsenic).

**What actually works:**

1. **Cook in plenty of water and drain — like pasta.** In a controlled study, cooking at 6 parts water to 1 part rice cut inorganic arsenic by about 45%, while cooking in a small amount of water (fully absorbed) removed none. [Raab et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19137137/). An FDA study found average reductions of about 40% in long-grain white rice, 50% in brown rice and 60% in parboiled rice. [Gray et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26515534/).
2. **The trade-off: vitamins in enriched rice.** In the same study, excess-water cooking also removed 50–70% of the iron, folate and B vitamins added to enriched white rice. In brown rice, which isn't enriched, the loss is much smaller.
3. **Rinsing alone barely helps with arsenic** — it removed about 10% in basmati and less in other varieties, and it washes out the enrichment. It does remove excess starch.
4. **Vary your grains:** Swap some of the rice for quinoa, buckwheat, bulgur or oats.

**For context:** For an adult who eats rice a few times a week, this is no reason to avoid it. It matters more for babies and toddlers, and for people whose daily staple is rice. Rice drinks, for example, are not recommended for children under 5 because of their arsenic levels. [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/weaning-and-feeding/drinks-and-cups-for-babies-and-young-children/).

### Storage safety — and a cooling bonus

Cooked rice left at room temperature is a breeding ground for *Bacillus cereus* spores, which release a heat-resistant toxin that reheating does not destroy. Refrigerate within an hour and reheat only once. Details in [the dangers of eating reheated rice](/en/food/reheated_rice_dangers/).

**Resistant starch:** In a small study, chilling cooked white rice for 24 hours in the fridge raised its resistant starch content, and rice that was chilled and then reheated produced a lower blood sugar response than freshly cooked rice. [Sonia et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26693746/). The effect is modest but easy to apply.

### Which rice should you choose?

* **Everyday eating, fiber and fullness:** brown rice, cooked in plenty of water.
* **Blood sugar control (diabetes, prediabetes):** basmati or brown rice, in a moderate portion with protein and vegetables; chilled-and-reheated rice is a bonus.
* **Before and after training:** white rice.
* **Digestive trouble, an irritable-bowel flare or a low-FODMAP diet:** white rice.
* **Babies and toddlers:** white rice cooked in plenty of water, as part of a varied diet — not every day, and no rice drinks.
* **Protein and variety:** wild rice, alone or in a blend.

### Practical use

* **How to cook rice in excess water:** Bring a large pot of water to the boil (about 6–10 cups per cup of rice), add the rice, cook until tender — about 10–15 minutes for white and 25–30 for brown — then drain in a sieve and let it steam for a few minutes back in the pot.
* **Seasoning:** Since the water is drained, salt the cooking water like pasta water or season after draining.
* **Blends:** Cooking wild and brown rice together works well, since their cooking times are similar.

### Summary

**Wild rice** is the most nutritious — more protein, fewer calories — but expensive. **Brown rice** is the practical everyday choice for its fiber and minerals, with more arsenic. **White rice**, especially basmati, suits training, digestive trouble and toddlers. Whichever you pick, the simple step with the biggest effect is to cook it in plenty of water and drain, which roughly halves the arsenic.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Which rice is the healthiest?**
Answer: Wild rice: about 4 g of protein per 100 g cooked, the fewest calories and carbohydrates, and as much fiber as brown rice. Its drawback is the price. For everyday use, brown rice or basmati is the practical compromise.

**Question: Is brown rice really healthier than white rice?**
Answer: On most measures, yes — 4.5 times the fiber, 3.5 times the magnesium and a lower glycemic index. The downsides: more inorganic arsenic, which concentrates in the bran, and phytic acid, which slightly reduces iron and zinc absorption.

**Question: How do you reduce arsenic in rice?**
Answer: Cook it like pasta — in plenty of water (about 6 parts to 1 part rice) and drain the excess. That cuts inorganic arsenic by about 40–60%, depending on the type. Rinsing alone barely helps. In enriched white rice, this method also removes some of the added vitamins.

**Question: What is the glycemic index of rice?**
Answer: It varies a lot by variety: regular white rice about 72–89, brown rice about 50–68 and wild rice about 45–57. Basmati is relatively low and jasmine high, so the choice of variety sometimes matters more than white versus brown.

**Question: Is wild rice actually rice?**
Answer: No. It is the seed of a water grass of the genus *Zizania* that grows in North American lakes, and it is not closely related to ordinary rice. That is also why it is richer in protein.

**Question: Can babies eat rice?**
Answer: Yes, as part of a varied diet. White rice cooked in plenty of water is best, and rice shouldn't be the only grain. Rice drinks are not recommended for children under 5 because of arsenic.

**Question: How can you lower the glycemic index of white rice?**
Answer: Chill it in the fridge for about a day after cooking and reheat it before eating — some of the starch turns into resistant starch. Choosing basmati and serving it with protein, fat and vegetables lowers the blood sugar response further.
