## A Scientific Comparison: Spinach, Kale and Chard

**No single leafy green is "the healthiest": kale wins on vitamin C and on calcium your body actually absorbs, spinach leads on folate and iron and disappears into smoothies, and Swiss chard is especially rich in vitamin K and magnesium. For plant calcium, pick kale; if you are prone to kidney stones, go easy on raw spinach and chard.**

In Hebrew these are "תרד" (spinach), "קייל" (kale) and "מנגולד" (chard). All three are very low in calories and rich in vitamin K, folate and antioxidants. The differences come down to three things: how much oxalate they carry (which decides how much of their calcium is absorbed), how much vitamin C they have, and how they behave in the kitchen — raw, cooked or blended.

Values are rounded and based on standard food-composition data (USDA). They vary with variety, season and growing conditions, so read them as "about".

Keep in mind that 100 g of raw leaves is a large volume — several cups. Cooking shrinks greens dramatically, so a cooked serving delivers several times more of everything than the same cup of raw leaves.

### 1. Spinach

The soft, familiar leaf, with a mild taste that all but vanishes in a smoothie or an omelet.

**Benefits:**
* **Folate and iron:** The leader of the three for both. The iron in leaves is non-heme iron, which is absorbed less well than iron from meat; pairing it with a vitamin C source such as lemon or bell pepper improves absorption.
* **Convenience:** Available fresh and frozen all year, and it cooks in a minute.
* **Vitamin A and carotenoids:** Rich in beta-carotene and lutein.

**Downsides:**
* **Oxalates:** Spinach is one of the richest vegetables in oxalic acid. It binds the calcium in the leaf, so only a small share of it is absorbed — spinach is not a good calcium source despite the number in the table. [Source: NIH ODS, Calcium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/).
* **Kidney stones:** For people who form calcium-oxalate stones, large amounts of raw spinach (a daily green smoothie, for example) are a poor idea. Boiling removes much of the soluble oxalate: in one study, boiling cut soluble oxalate in vegetables by 30–87%, with the oxalate ending up in the cooking water that was poured off. [Chai & Liebman, 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15826055/).

### 2. Kale

A leafy cabbage from the crucifer family — tough and slightly bitter.

**Benefits:**
* **Calcium that gets absorbed:** Kale is low in oxalates. In a study that measured actual absorption, about 41% of the calcium in kale was absorbed, compared with about 32% of the calcium in milk in the same volunteers — in contrast with the poor absorption reported for spinach. [Heaney & Weaver, 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2321572/). That makes it especially useful on a vegan diet.
* **Vitamin C:** Roughly three to four times as much as spinach or chard per 100 g.
* **Fiber and shelf life:** The most fiber of the three, and it keeps in the fridge for a long time.
* **Glucosinolates:** Like broccoli and cabbage, kale contains sulfur compounds that are being studied for their health effects. That is an active research area, not proof of protection against cancer.

**Downsides:**
* **Digestion:** The tough fiber can cause bloating in people who aren't used to it. Massaging the leaves with a little oil and lemon juice before eating them raw softens them.
* **Taste:** A bitterness not everyone likes, especially in mature leaves.

### 3. Swiss Chard

A relative of the beet, with a soft leaf and a crunchy stalk that may be white, red or yellow.

**Benefits:**
* **Vitamin K and magnesium:** The leader of the three in both per 100 g.
* **Two vegetables in one:** Cook the stalks like celery and the leaves like spinach. Especially good for stuffed leaves, savory pies and stews.
* **Color:** The colored stalks carry betalain pigments, the same family as in beets.

**Downsides:**
* **Oxalates:** Like spinach, chard is high in oxalates, so its calcium is poorly absorbed. Boiling and discarding the water reduces them.
* **Sodium:** It carries more natural sodium than the other two (about 200 mg per 100 g). That is still small next to the salt added to food, but relevant on a strict low-sodium diet.

### A shared caution: warfarin and vitamin K

All three greens are very rich in vitamin K, which affects how the blood thinner warfarin works. People on warfarin do not need to avoid leafy greens, but they should keep their intake **consistent** from day to day — not jump from none to a daily green smoothie — and discuss changes with their doctor. [Source: NIH ODS, Vitamin K](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminK-HealthProfessional/). This does not apply to the newer anticoagulants, which do not work through vitamin K.

### Which green should you choose?

* **A plant calcium source (vegans, people who don't drink milk):** kale. Its calcium is well absorbed.
* **A green smoothie that doesn't change the taste:** spinach — but rotate it rather than drinking a large amount of raw spinach every day.
* **Pregnancy and planning a pregnancy:** spinach, for its folate — in addition to the supplement your doctor recommends, not instead of it.
* **Prone to kidney stones:** kale, or spinach and chard boiled with the water discarded.
* **Stews, stuffed leaves and savory pies:** chard.
* **People on warfarin:** any of the three, in a steady amount and in coordination with the doctor.
* **A strict low-sodium diet:** spinach or kale before chard.

### Practical use

* **Fat helps absorption:** Vitamin K, vitamin A and the carotenoids are fat-soluble, so a drizzle of olive oil in a salad or stew helps.
* **Frozen is fine:** Frozen spinach is a cheap, nutritious option, and because it is already lightly cooked, more of it fits in a serving.
* **Wash well:** Leafy greens collect sand and soil, especially spinach and chard. Wash them thoroughly, including "pre-washed" leaves when in doubt.
* **Storage:** Kale keeps for a week or more in the fridge; spinach and chard wilt within a few days, so buy them closer to when you cook.

### Summary

All three greens are highly nutritious and low in calories, and the differences between them are more practical than they first seem. **Kale** is the pick for calcium and vitamin C, **spinach** for folate, iron and smoothies, and **chard** for stews, vitamin K and magnesium. People prone to kidney stones or taking warfarin should watch oxalates and vitamin K, respectively. The simplest advice: rotate between them over the week.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Which is healthier, spinach or kale?**
Answer: It depends on what you are after. Kale is richer in vitamin C and fiber, and its calcium is well absorbed because it is low in oxalates. Spinach is richer in folate and iron and has a milder taste. Both belong in a healthy diet.

**Question: Is the calcium in spinach absorbed?**
Answer: Only a little. Spinach is high in oxalates, which bind its calcium and block absorption, so it is not considered a good calcium source. Kale, broccoli and bok choy are better plant sources.

**Question: Does cooking spinach reduce oxalates?**
Answer: Yes, boiling in particular. Soluble oxalate moves into the cooking water, so pour it away. Steaming removes less than boiling.

**Question: Who should be careful with spinach, kale and chard?**
Answer: People on warfarin should keep their intake of vitamin K-rich greens steady. People prone to calcium-oxalate kidney stones should limit raw spinach and chard. Anyone on a strict low-sodium diet may prefer spinach or kale over chard.

**Question: What is the difference between chard and spinach?**
Answer: Chard is a relative of the beet, with a large leaf and a thick, crunchy stalk, and it suits stews and stuffed dishes. Spinach is softer and milder. Chard has more vitamin K and sodium; spinach has far more folate.

**Question: Can you eat kale raw?**
Answer: Yes. To soften it, strip out the tough stem, slice it thinly and massage the leaves with a little oil and lemon juice for a few minutes. If it bloats you, start with small amounts or cook it lightly.
