For years white wheat ruled the bakeries, but the health revolution has brought ancient grains and whole-grain flours back to center stage. Let's bring some order to the flour shelf.

### 1. Whole Wheat Flour

The most standard and widely available flour.

* **Composition:** Milled from the whole wheat kernel. Rich in [gluten](/en/food/gluten/).
* **Benefits:**
    * **Availability and price:** The cheapest and most accessible whole-grain flour.
    * **Baking:** Its high gluten content makes it relatively easy to create risen, airy loaves.
    * **Fiber:** It contains soluble and insoluble fiber that support bowel function.
* **Downsides:**
    * **Digestion:** Many people report bloating and discomfort after wheat baked goods. Crossover studies indicate that the main culprit is actually the fructans in wheat (carbohydrates from the FODMAP family), not the gluten itself — see [gluten](/en/food/gluten/).
    * **Taste:** A dominant flavor and dense texture that some people (and children) like less.

### 2. Whole Spelt Flour

An ancient grain, a "cousin" of wheat, that has kept its original form for thousands of years.

* **Composition:** Spelt is a species of wheat in every respect and contains full [gluten](/en/food/gluten/), but the gluten network it forms is more fragile and weaker than that of bread wheat.
* **Benefits:**
    * **Better tolerated by some people:** Many report better digestive comfort with spelt. The controlled evidence for this is thin, and the explanation may have to do with a lower fructan content and a longer fermentation time — not with the gluten structure. **Either way, it's not relevant to celiac disease:** spelt is completely off-limits.
    * **Nutritional value:** Richer in protein, [iron](/en/food/iron/), [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/) and B vitamins compared with wheat.
    * **Taste:** A subtly sweet, nutty flavor that children usually like more.
* **Downsides:**
    * **Baking:** Because of the fragile protein, over-kneading will make the dough fall apart and the bread come out flat. It requires a gentle hand.
    * **Price:** More expensive than wheat flour.

### 3. Rye Flour

A grain typical of Northern European countries, with a completely different character.

* **Composition:** It contains [gluten](/en/food/gluten/) — its prolamin is called secalin — but its ability to form a strong gluten network is very low. It is rich in pentosans (a type of water-absorbing polysaccharide), and these, not the gluten, give rye bread its body.
* **Benefits:**
    * **Glycemic index:** Especially low. Rye fiber breaks down slowly and provides a very long-lasting feeling of fullness. Excellent for people with diabetes.
    * **Taste and texture:** It gives bread a deep, tangy flavor and a dense, moist texture that stays fresh for many days.
* **Downsides:**
    * **Difficult baking:** It's almost impossible to bake 100% rye without [sourdough](/en/food/sourdough/) and a high level of skill (it comes out like a "brick"). It's usually mixed with another flour.
    * **Heaviness:** [Rye bread](/en/food/rye-bread/) is heavy and dense, not for anyone looking for a "cloud."

| **Glycemic index** | Medium-high | Medium | **Low (the winner)** |
| **Digestive difficulty** | Medium-high | Low | Low |

### Summary and conclusions

**It's important to stress:** all three flours contain [gluten](/en/food/gluten/), and none of them is suitable for people with celiac disease. For those who suffer from bloating and mild sensitivity (not celiac disease), **spelt flour** is the savior that will let you keep enjoying baked goods. For those who want to manage their weight and keep blood sugar levels balanced, **rye** bread is the smartest choice. For easy, cheap everyday baking without special sensitivities, **whole wheat flour** is still an excellent, nourishing choice.
