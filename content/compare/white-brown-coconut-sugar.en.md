## A Scientific Comparison: White, Brown and Coconut Sugar

**To your body there is almost no difference: white sugar, brown sugar and coconut sugar are all mostly sucrose, with about 16 calories per teaspoon, and all count as "free sugar" worth limiting. The minerals in brown and coconut sugar are negligible, and the claim that coconut sugar has a much lower glycemic index rests on very little data. Choose by taste, texture and price — and use less.**

In Hebrew these are "סוכר לבן", "סוכר חום" and "סוכר קוקוס". The decision that really matters is how much sugar you eat, not which kind. The World Health Organization recommends keeping free sugars below 10% of daily energy — about 50 g, or roughly 12 teaspoons, on a 2,000-calorie diet — and notes that going down to 5% may bring further benefit. The definition covers any sugar added to food, as well as the sugar in honey, syrups and juices. [Source: WHO, healthy diet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet).

### Nutrition comparison

| Component | White sugar | Brown sugar | Coconut sugar |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Calories per 100 g** | ~387 | ~380 | ~375–390 |
| **Calories per teaspoon (4 g)** | ~16 | ~15 | ~15–16 |
| **Sugars per 100 g** | ~100 g | ~97 g | ~85–95 g |
| **Main component** | Sucrose | Sucrose + molasses | Mostly sucrose, some glucose and fructose |
| **Minerals** | None | Traces (calcium, potassium, iron) | Traces (potassium and others), varies by product |
| **Glycemic index** | ~65 | ~65 | Little data; sometimes reported slightly lower |
| **Taste** | Neutral | Caramel, moist | Caramel, earthy |
| **Price** | **Cheapest** | Slightly more | **Several times more** |

For perspective, the potassium in a teaspoon of brown or coconut sugar is far less than in a single bite of banana. Getting a meaningful amount of minerals from it would mean eating a harmful amount of sugar.

### 1. White Sugar

Refined table sugar from sugarcane or sugar [beets](/en/food/beets/).

**Benefits:**
* **Baking:** A clean taste, even crystals and predictable behavior — it helps with aeration, browning and crispness.
* **Price:** The cheapest sweetener there is.

**Downsides:**
* **Calories without nutrients:** No vitamins, minerals or fiber.
* **Free sugar:** High intake, especially from sweetened drinks, is linked to weight gain and tooth decay. [Source: WHO, guideline on sugars intake](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241549028).

### 2. Brown Sugar

In most cases, white sugar with molasses added back, or sugar that was not fully refined.

**Benefits:**
* **Moisture and flavor:** The molasses adds a caramel taste and keeps cookies and cakes softer and moister.

**Downsides:**
* **A health illusion:** About 95% sucrose. The traces of calcium, potassium and iron from the molasses are negligible, and your body handles it exactly like white sugar.
* **Color doesn't mean "natural":** Dark brown sugar is simply sugar with more molasses.

### 3. Coconut Sugar

Made from the sweet sap of coconut palm flowers (not the fruit) by evaporation and drying. See also [coconut](/en/food/coconut/).

**Benefits:**
* **Taste:** Deep and caramel-like, good in coffee, cookies and Southeast Asian cooking.
* **Minimal processing:** It isn't fully refined and keeps a few minerals and antioxidants.

**Downsides:**
* **Still sugar:** Mostly sucrose — so about half of its sugar is fructose, as in white sugar. It counts as free sugar in every respect.
* **Glycemic index:** The claim of a much lower value than white sugar is based on few tests with varying results. Even if it is a little lower, at the amounts used for sweetening it does not change the picture.
* **Price:** Significantly more expensive, with no health payoff.

### Which sugar should you choose?

* **Classic baking (airy cakes, meringue, light caramel):** white sugar.
* **Soft cookies, barbecue sauces and a caramel flavor:** brown sugar.
* **A special flavor in coffee or Southeast Asian dishes:** coconut sugar, if the price doesn't bother you — not as a "healthy substitute".
* **Diabetes and prediabetes:** none of the three is "more allowed". The amount and the rest of the meal decide. A dietitian can advise on alternatives such as non-caloric sweeteners.
* **Children:** as little added sugar as possible of any kind, and especially not in drinks.

### Practical use

* **Cut back gradually:** In most cake recipes you can remove a quarter to a third of the sugar without hurting the result.
* **Swapping types:** Brown and coconut sugar replace white sugar one to one, but they change color and flavor and make a moister bake.
* **Hidden sugar:** Most free sugar comes from sweetened drinks, yogurts, breakfast cereals and sauces — not from the spoonful in your coffee. See also [sugary soft drinks](/en/food/sugary-soft-drinks/).
* **Taste adapts:** People who cut back on sweetness usually find, after a few weeks, that the same foods taste sweet enough with less sugar.

### Summary

Brown sugar and coconut sugar are not healthy substitutes for white sugar. All three are mostly sucrose with about the same calories, and the minerals in the other two are negligible. **White sugar** is the cheap and predictable baking choice, **brown sugar** adds moisture and a caramel flavor, and **coconut sugar** is an expensive flavor choice. What affects health is the amount: under 12 teaspoons of free sugar a day, and preferably less.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Is brown sugar healthier than white sugar?**
Answer: No. Brown sugar is white sugar with a little molasses. Its mineral content is negligible, the calories are almost identical, and your body handles it the same way.

**Question: Is coconut sugar suitable for people with diabetes?**
Answer: No more than regular sugar. It is mostly sucrose and raises blood sugar. Claims of a much lower glycemic index rest on little data. People with diabetes should limit every kind of sugar.

**Question: How many calories are in a teaspoon of sugar?**
Answer: About 15 to 16 calories in a teaspoon of roughly 4 g, with no meaningful difference between white, brown and coconut sugar.

**Question: How much sugar can you eat per day?**
Answer: The WHO recommends getting less than 10% of daily energy from free sugars — about 50 g or 12 teaspoons for an adult — and preferably less than 5%. That includes sugar in drinks and processed food, as well as honey and syrups.

**Question: Is coconut sugar a natural sugar?**
Answer: It is less processed than white sugar, but "natural" does not mean "healthy". To your body it is free sugar like any other.

**Question: Can you swap white sugar for brown sugar in baking?**
Answer: Yes, one to one. The bake will be moister, darker and more caramel-flavored, and cookies will come out softer.
