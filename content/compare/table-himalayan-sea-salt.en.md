## A Scientific Comparison: Table, Himalayan and Sea Salt

**Health-wise, the differences between table salt, pink Himalayan salt and sea salt are negligible: all are roughly 98–99% sodium chloride, and the "extra" minerals come in amounts that do nothing for you. The one difference that matters is iodine — which makes iodized table salt the right everyday choice, while specialty salts are a matter of taste and texture.**

In Hebrew these are "מלח שולחן", "מלח הימלאיה" and "מלח ים". What actually matters is the amount. The World Health Organization recommends that adults eat less than 5 g of salt a day (under 2,000 mg of sodium), about one teaspoon, while average intake worldwide is roughly double that. Excess sodium raises blood pressure and the risk of heart disease and stroke. [Source: WHO, salt reduction](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/salt-reduction).

### Comparison table (per 100 g of salt)

| Component | Table salt | Himalayan salt | Sea salt |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sodium chloride** | ~99% | ~96–98% | ~95–99% |
| **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/)** | ~39 g | ~37–38 g | ~37–39 g |
| **Sodium per teaspoon** | ~2,300 mg (teaspoon ~6 g) | Less per teaspoon if coarse | Less per teaspoon if coarse |
| **[Iodine](/en/food/supplements/iodine/)** | **High if iodized** (check the label) | Negligible | Negligible unless iodized |
| **Other minerals** | Almost none | Traces (iron, potassium, magnesium) | Traces |
| **Microplastics** | Low | Low | **Highest of the three** |
| **Additives** | Sometimes an anti-caking agent | Usually none | Usually none |
| **Price** | **Cheapest** | Expensive | Moderate to expensive |

**A note on "less sodium":** Coarse salt or flakes contain less sodium *per teaspoon* only because less salt fits in the spoon. Gram for gram, the sodium is almost identical. People who season to taste usually just add more.

### 1. Table Salt

The everyday salt, mined from salt deposits or made from seawater and then refined.

**Benefits:**
* **Iodine:** In many countries table salt is iodized. Iodine is needed to make thyroid hormones and for brain development in the fetus and infant, and iodized salt is one of the main dietary sources of it. [Source: NIH ODS, Iodine](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-HealthProfessional/). Not all table salt is iodized — in Israel, for example, look for "מועשר ביוד" (iodized) on the package; elsewhere, look for "iodized salt".
* **Price and consistency:** Cheap, with a fine, even grain that dissolves quickly and measures accurately for baking.

**Downsides:**
* **Anti-caking agents:** Small amounts are added so the salt doesn't clump in humidity. These are approved additives, and there is no evidence of harm at the levels used in salt.
* **Refining:** It strips out the trace minerals — which, as explained below, never contributed anything meaningful anyway.

### 2. Himalayan Pink Salt

A rock salt mined mainly in Pakistan, colored pink by traces of iron oxides.

**Benefits:**
* **Taste and looks:** Attractive crystals and a pleasant crunch for finishing dishes.
* **Minimal processing:** Usually free of additives.

**Downsides:**
* **The mineral myth:** A study of 31 pink salt samples sold in Australia did find a range of minerals, but calculated that you would need to eat more than 30 g of salt a day (about six teaspoons) for them to make any meaningful nutritional contribution — an amount that supplies a harmful excess of sodium. [Fayet-Moore et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33086585/).
* **Contaminants:** In the same study, one sample contained lead above Australia's maximum permitted level. "Natural" does not guarantee "clean".
* **No iodine:** Switching entirely to pink salt means giving up an important iodine source.

### 3. Sea Salt

Made by evaporating seawater, and sold as coarse grains, flakes or "fleur de sel".

**Benefits:**
* **Texture:** Crunchy flakes are excellent sprinkled on a finished dish, and a few deliver a lot of flavor.
* **Minimal processing:** Usually no anti-caking agents.

**Downsides:**
* **Microplastics:** A study of 39 salt brands from around the world found 0 to 1,674 microplastic particles per kilogram in sea salts, versus 0 to 148 in rock salts; levels tracked how polluted the sea was where the salt was made. [Kim et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30285421/). The health effects of such amounts are not yet known, and they are small next to other sources of exposure.
* **Iodine:** Usually not iodized, and its natural iodine content is negligible.

### Which salt should you choose?

* **Everyday cooking at home, especially in pregnancy, breastfeeding and families with children:** iodized table salt.
* **Finishing a salad, fish or steak:** sea salt flakes or pink salt — for texture, not health.
* **If you need to cut sodium (high blood pressure, kidney disease, heart failure):** none of the three is a "low-sodium salt". What helps is less salt and less processed food. Potassium-based salt substitutes exist, but anyone taking blood pressure medication or living with kidney disease should ask their doctor first.
* **An egg-like taste in vegan cooking:** [Indian black salt (kala namak)](/en/food/kala-namak/), whose sulfur compounds give it a distinctive aroma.

### Practical use

* **Most salt doesn't come from the shaker:** For most people, most sodium comes from bread, cheese, processed meats, sauces and snacks. Reading labels does more than switching salt types.
* **Salt at the end:** Coarse flakes on the finished dish give a strong salty hit with less salt overall.
* **Acid and spices:** Lemon, vinegar, garlic and herbs let you cut salt without food tasting flat.
* **Storage:** Iodine in iodized salt slowly degrades with heat, light and humidity, so keep it in a closed container away from the stove.

### Summary

Pink salt and sea salt are not healthier than table salt. Their minerals are negligible, and sea salt carries more microplastics. The iodine in iodized salt has real health value, which makes it the basic choice for the kitchen. Specialty salts are worth paying for only for texture and flavor. What really affects health is the amount: under 5 g of salt a day, most of it hidden in processed food.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Is Himalayan salt healthier than regular salt?**
Answer: Not meaningfully. It is about 98% sodium chloride, and you would need to eat more than 30 g of salt a day to get a real contribution from its minerals — a harmful amount. It also usually contains no iodine.

**Question: Does pink salt have less sodium?**
Answer: Gram for gram, almost the same. A teaspoon of it sometimes has less sodium only because the crystals are coarse and less salt fits in the spoon.

**Question: Does sea salt contain microplastics?**
Answer: Often, yes — more than rock salt. A global study found up to about 1,700 particles per kilogram in sea salts, depending on how polluted the local sea was. The health impact of such amounts is not yet known.

**Question: Why does iodine in salt matter?**
Answer: Iodine is needed to make thyroid hormones and for the brain development of the fetus and infant. Iodized salt is a simple, cheap way to get it, especially during pregnancy and breastfeeding.

**Question: How much salt can you eat per day?**
Answer: The WHO recommends that adults eat less than 5 g of salt a day, which is under 2,000 mg of sodium — about one teaspoon, including the salt in processed food.

**Question: Which salt is best for cooking?**
Answer: For cooking and baking, iodized table salt, which dissolves quickly and measures accurately. For finishing, sea salt flakes or pink salt, for the texture.
