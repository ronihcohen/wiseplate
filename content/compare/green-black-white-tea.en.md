## A Scientific Comparison: Green, Black and White Tea

**For morning energy, pick black tea, with the most caffeine of the three (about 40–70 mg per cup). For heart health, pick green tea, the most studied: regular drinking lowers LDL cholesterol slightly. For a delicate taste and little caffeine, pick white tea. The health differences between them are small, and any of them beats a sweetened drink.**

All true teas come from the same plant, Camellia sinensis (in Hebrew "תה ירוק", "תה שחור" and "תה לבן"). The difference lies in how the leaves are processed after picking: how much they oxidize, and how much heat they meet.

**In short:** A cup of black tea has about 40–70 mg of caffeine, [green tea](/en/food/green-tea/) about 20–45 mg, and white tea about 15–30 mg — all less than a cup of [coffee](/en/food/coffee/) (95–165 mg). Yes, **green tea has caffeine**, about a third to a half of a cup of coffee. The full breakdown is in the section [how much caffeine is in each type of tea](#how-much-caffeine-is-in-each-type-of-tea).

### 1. Black Tea

The most common tea in the West (such as [Earl Grey](/en/food/earl-grey/) and English Breakfast).

* **Processing:** The leaves are withered, rolled and fully oxidized (the oxidation turns them black) before final drying.
* **Benefits:**
    * **Energy:** It contains the highest amount of [caffeine](/en/food/supplements/caffeine/) of the tea types, making it an excellent morning substitute for coffee.
    * **Distinctive polyphenols:** Rich in theaflavins, antioxidants formed during oxidation. In population studies, drinking tea, mostly black, is linked to a lower risk of heart disease, but the controlled trials on cholesterol are small and inconsistent.
    * **Hydrates like water:** in a controlled trial, 4–6 cups of black tea a day maintained hydration just as well as the same amount of water. [Ruxton and Hart, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21450118/).
    * **Taste:** A bold, strong and slightly bitter flavor that pairs well with [milk](/en/food/milk/).
* **Downsides:**
    * **Acidity:** It may be more acidic on the stomach.

### 2. Green Tea

The national drink of Japan and China, and a symbol of health.

* **Processing:** The leaves are heated quickly (steamed or pan-fired) right after picking to stop the oxidizing enzyme. The leaf stays green.
* **Benefits:**
    * **Cholesterol:** a meta-analysis of 31 trials (3,321 participants) found that drinking green tea lowered LDL cholesterol by about 4.5 mg/dL, a small but consistent drop. [Xu et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32434539/).
    * **[EGCG](/en/food/egcg/):** the main catechin in green tea, widely studied. Most of the impressive results on cancer and fat burning came from the lab or from high-dose extracts, not from a cup of tea.
    * **[L-Theanine](/en/food/l-theanine/):** It contains an amino acid that promotes calm and improves concentration, balancing the effect of the [caffeine](/en/food/supplements/caffeine/) ("calm alertness").
    * **Metabolism:** the effect on metabolic rate is very small, and you shouldn't expect weight loss from drinking tea.
* **Downsides:**
    * **Sensitivity:** It may cause nausea if drunk on an empty stomach (because of the [tannins](/en/food/tannins/)).

### 3. White Tea

The most delicate and luxurious tea, made from the youngest buds.

* **Processing:** The most minimal processing. The buds are harvested before they have fully opened and dried in the sun or in a controlled room. No heating and no deliberate oxidation.
* **Benefits:**
    * **Catechins:** thanks to minimal processing, it contains catechins much like green tea. The amount in the cup depends heavily on brewing, so it can't be called "the richest in antioxidants".
    * **Skin:** protection of [collagen](/en/food/collagen/) and elastin has been tested mainly with extracts and in the lab, and there is no evidence that a cup of white tea affects the skin.
    * **Taste:** A delicate, floral and slightly sweet flavor, without the bitterness typical of green or black tea.
* **Downsides:**
    * **Price:** More expensive because of the specific, delicate harvesting process.
    * **Weak flavor:** People who like strong flavors may feel they're drinking "hot water."

### How much caffeine is in each type of tea

This is the most common question about tea, and the short answer is that every true tea has caffeine — including green tea and white tea. A completely caffeine-free "tea" is only an herbal infusion that isn't tea at all (mint, chamomile, rooibos).

| Drink (240 ml cup) | Caffeine |
|---|---|
| Filter coffee | 95–165 mg |
| Espresso (30 ml shot) | 60–80 mg |
| **Black tea** | 40–70 mg |
| **Matcha** (a teaspoon of powder) | 60–70 mg |
| **Green tea** | 20–45 mg |
| **White tea** | 15–30 mg |
| Decaf green tea | 2–5 mg |
| Herbal infusion (mint, chamomile) | 0 mg |

**What affects the actual amount?** The ranges are wide because three factors change the result more than the type of tea itself:

- **Water temperature:** Hotter water extracts more caffeine. Green tea is brewed at 70–80 degrees (158–176°F) and therefore yields less caffeine than black tea brewed in boiling water — even when the caffeine content of the dry leaf is similar.
- **Steeping time:** Most of the caffeine is released in the first minute. A 5-minute steep will yield almost double that of a one-minute steep.
- **Amount of leaves:** A standard tea bag contains about 2 grams. Loose-leaf brewing tends to be stronger.

**A point that surprises many people:** The dry leaf of white tea sometimes has **more** caffeine than green tea, because it is made from young buds, where the plant concentrates caffeine as a defense against insects. But since white tea is brewed in lukewarm water for a short time, the final drink usually contains less caffeine.

**For people sensitive to caffeine:** White tea or green tea brewed briefly (one minute) in 70-degree water provides a minimal amount. Another effective trick — steep the leaves for 30 seconds, pour off the first water and steep again; this removes a considerable share of the caffeine while keeping most of the flavor and antioxidants.

**Tea's unique advantage:** The caffeine in tea comes with [L-theanine](/en/food/l-theanine/), an amino acid that slows its absorption and softens its effect. The result is "calm alertness" — a moderate, sustained rise in alertness instead of the sharp spike and crash typical of coffee. That's why people who get jittery from coffee do fine with tea.

### Downsides — what to know about each type

| Type | Main downsides |
|---|---|
| **Black tea** | The most caffeine of the three — may disturb sleep if drunk in the afternoon; relatively acidic and may worsen heartburn; stains teeth more than the others because of the theaflavins; blocks plant iron absorption if drunk with a meal |
| **Green tea** | May cause nausea on an empty stomach because of the tannins; becomes very bitter if brewed in boiling water; blocks iron absorption; concentrated extracts (unlike the drink) have been linked to liver damage; according to EFSA, doses of 800 mg EGCG a day or more from supplements are associated with raised liver enzymes. [EFSA, 2018](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2018.5239) |
| **White tea** | Significantly more expensive; a delicate flavor perceived as weak; less clinical research than on green and black tea |
| **All types** | Block absorption of non-heme [iron](/en/food/iron/) when drunk with a meal (in one study, black tea cut absorption from a bread meal by 79–94%) [Hurrell et al., 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10999016/); contain caffeine and so aren't suitable in large amounts during pregnancy (accepted limit: up to 200 mg of caffeine a day from all sources) |

### Which tea should you choose?

- **For morning energy and as a coffee substitute:** black tea, or matcha if you want more caffeine.
- **For heart health and cholesterol:** green tea, 2–3 cups a day, without sugar.
- **If you're sensitive to caffeine, or in the afternoon:** white or green tea brewed briefly, and an herbal infusion in the evening.
- **For heartburn or a sensitive stomach:** white or green tea brewed gently, not on an empty stomach.
- **For people low in iron, vegetarians and vegans:** any type, but an hour before or after a meal rather than with it.
- **In pregnancy:** up to 2–3 cups a day, within the 200 mg limit for caffeine from all sources.
- **If you like milk in your tea:** black tea; its strong flavor stands up to milk.

### Summary and conclusions

All three teas come from the same plant, and the differences between them are smaller than marketing suggests. **Green tea** is the most studied and lowers LDL slightly; **black tea** has the most caffeine and the boldest flavor; **white tea** is the most delicate. None of them has been shown to "burn fat" or boost the immune system. Choose by taste and by how much caffeine suits you, drink it without sugar, and keep it away from meals if you are low in iron.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Does tea really cause dehydration?**
Answer: **Myth.** Caffeine is indeed a mild diuretic, but the amount of water in a cup of tea far exceeds its diuretic effect. In a controlled trial, 4–6 cups of black tea a day maintained hydration just as well as the same amount of water.

**Question: Does adding [milk](/en/food/milk/) ruin the health benefits?**
Answer: **Yes and no.** Milk contains a protein called casein, which can bind to the [polyphenols](/en/food/polyphenols/) (antioxidants) in tea and somewhat reduce their absorption in the body. If you're after maximum antioxidants — it's better to drink it plain. If your goal is enjoyment — the effect isn't that dramatic, and it's worth enjoying the taste.

**Question: Why does green tea sometimes turn bitter?**
Answer: The reason is **water temperature and steeping time**. Green tea contains [tannins](/en/food/tannins/) (bitter) and amino acids (sweet). Boiling water (100 degrees, 212°F) scorches the leaves and releases the bitter tannins immediately. Cooler water (70-80 degrees) releases the sweetness and aroma before the bitterness. **Tip:** Don't leave the tea bag in the cup forever. 2-3 minutes is more than enough.

**Question: Does tea interfere with [iron](/en/food/iron/) absorption?**
Answer: **Yes.** The [polyphenols](/en/food/polyphenols/) and tannins in tea can bind to iron from plant sources (non-heme iron) and prevent its absorption in the gut. **The solution:** Separate drinking tea from meals. Drink tea an hour before or an hour after eating. [Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) (such as [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) in your tea) can help improve absorption and neutralize some of the inhibitory effect.

**Question: Which tea has the most caffeine?**
Answer: Surprisingly, it isn't always black tea. **Black tea** usually contains the most caffeine because it's brewed in boiling water for a long time, which extracts the maximum from the leaf. **Green tea** usually contains less, both because of the type of leaves and because of the gentler brewing. **White tea** can contain a high amount of caffeine because it's made from buds (where the plant concentrates caffeine as a defense against insects), but because of the short, lukewarm brewing, the final drink usually has less caffeine.

**Question: How long can brewed tea be kept?**
Answer: It's best to drink tea soon after making it. After two hours at room temperature, it starts to oxidize and its taste changes. In the fridge it can keep for up to 24 hours ("iced tea"), but after that it may lose some of its antioxidants and develop off-flavors.

**Question: What's the difference between loose-leaf tea and tea bags?**
Answer: **Tea bags:** usually contain "tea dust" (fannings/dust) – small leaf particles that give color and flavor very quickly, but are usually lower quality and contain fewer essential oils. **Whole tea leaves:** stay fresh and aromatic for much longer, allow a richer, more complex flavor experience (and can also be steeped several times).
