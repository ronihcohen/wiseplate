## Running Shoes

**Research has not found a single shoe that prevents injuries for all runners; the best practical rule is to choose the shoe that feels most comfortable to you when running, and to increase your load gradually.** Softer cushioning was found to be somewhat protective in lighter runners, modern carbon-plate shoes save about 4% of energy for fast runners, and switching to barefoot or minimalist running requires a very slow adaptation. In Hebrew they are called "נעלי ריצה".

Running shoes are a tool, not a cure. The shoe is only one part of your kit: the base layer is covered on the [running underwear](/en/run/run_shorts/) page, and managing training load on the [heart rate zones](/en/run/running-zones/) page.

### What research says about shoes and injuries

**The "comfort filter":** a review by Benno Nigg, one of the leading researchers in running biomechanics, found no conclusive evidence that impact forces or the degree of pronation predict running injuries. Instead he suggests that runners choose shoes by comfort, because comfort reflects a shoe that fits their natural movement pattern. [Nigg 2015, Br J Sports Med](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26221015/)

**Cushioning:** in a randomised trial of 848 recreational runners given the same shoe in a soft or hard version, the hard-shoe group had more injuries. When split by body mass, the difference was found in lighter runners but not in heavier ones. [Malisoux 2020, Am J Sports Med](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31877062/) In other words, softer cushioning may help, but the assumption that "heavier runners need more cushioning" was not supported.

**Pronation:** in a study that followed 927 novice runners for a year, all running in a neutral shoe, runners with pronated feet were not injured more often than runners with neutral feet. [Nielsen 2014, Br J Sports Med](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23766439/) So there is no need to buy a "stability shoe" just because of moderate pronation.

### Types of shoes

| Type | Features | Who it suits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neutral cushioned | Medium-to-thick, flexible midsole | Most runners, everyday runs |
| Stability | Support on the inner side | Those who find it more comfortable; not required for pronation |
| Maximalist | Very thick midsole | Long runs, those who want a softer ride |
| Carbon plate with springy foam | Light, stiff along its length | Races and fast paces |
| Minimalist | Thin sole, low or zero drop | Only after gradual adaptation |
| Trail | Lugged outsole, rock protection | Paths and off-road |

### Carbon-plate shoes: a real energy saving

In a trial of 18 high-calibre runners, a prototype shoe with soft, springy foam and a stiff plate reduced the energy cost of running by about 4% compared with established marathon racing shoes, in all 18 participants. [Hoogkamer 2018, Sports Med](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29143929/) The benefit was measured at fast speeds (14–18 km/h); for slower runners it is probably smaller. These shoes are expensive, the foam wears out relatively quickly, and some runners find them less stable.

### Barefoot and minimalist running

**What was found:** runners who habitually run barefoot often land on the forefoot or midfoot, and generate a smaller initial collision force than shod runners who land on the heel. [Lieberman 2010, Nature](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20111000/)

**What was not found:** there is no proof that barefoot running lowers the overall injury rate. Changing the landing pattern shifts load from the knee to the ankle, the Achilles tendon and the calf.

**The risk of a fast transition:** in a trial in which experienced runners switched to Vibram FiveFingers over 10 weeks, more than half (10 of 19) showed bone marrow oedema in the foot on MRI — a sign of bone overload — compared with the control group. [Ridge 2013, Med Sci Sports Exerc](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23439417/)

### Benefits of running shoes

* **Protection:** against glass, stones, hot asphalt and cold.
* **Comfort:** subjective comfort is probably the best selection measure we have.
* **Cushioning:** may lower injury risk in lighter runners.
* **Performance:** modern racing shoes improve running economy.

### Downsides and limitations

* **There is no "corrective" shoe:** the idea that a shoe will correct foot structure and prevent injuries is not well supported by research.
* **Wear:** the foam loses its properties over time; many runners replace shoes after 500–800 km or when they feel "dead".
* **Price and marketing:** a high price does not guarantee a good fit. New technology is not necessarily comfortable for you.
* **Adaptation:** any big change in shoe — in drop, thickness or type — needs a gradual transition.

### Practical use: how to choose

1. **Shop in the afternoon:** feet swell during the day and when running. Leave about a thumb's width between your longest toe and the end of the shoe.
2. **Run in them:** in the shop or on a treadmill, for a few minutes. Choose the pair that feels most natural and comfortable.
3. **Don't change everything at once:** if your current shoe works for you, a similar model is a safe choice.
4. **Rotate two pairs:** some runners like to alternate between an everyday shoe and a faster one.
5. **Moving to minimalist shoes:** start with 5–10 minutes of running two or three times a week, and build slowly over months. Pain on the top of the foot or in the heel is a sign to stop.
6. **Keep it easy while adapting:** during a transition, keep most runs in [zone 2](/en/run/running-zones/).

### Summary

The research on running shoes is more modest than the adverts: no shoe prevents injuries for everyone, moderate pronation does not require a stability shoe, and soft cushioning helps mainly lighter runners. Carbon-plate shoes improve running economy at fast paces, and barefoot running moves load from one place to another rather than removing it. Choose by comfort, replace worn shoes, and change anything gradually.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How do I know which running shoe suits me?**
Answer: Comfort while running is the best measure. Run for a few minutes in several models and choose the one that feels most natural, with about a thumb's width of room at the front.

**Question: If I overpronate, do I need a stability shoe?**
Answer: Not necessarily. In a study of about 900 novice runners in neutral shoes, runners with pronated feet were not injured more often. If a stability shoe feels more comfortable to you, choose it.

**Question: Do cushioned shoes prevent injuries?**
Answer: In a trial of 848 runners, a soft shoe was linked to fewer injuries in lighter runners, but not in heavier ones. Cushioning helps to a degree, but it is not a magic fix.

**Question: Are carbon-plate shoes worth the money?**
Answer: For races and fast paces they save about 4% of energy. For slow everyday runs the benefit is smaller, and they are expensive and wear out quickly.

**Question: How often should I replace running shoes?**
Answer: Many runners replace them after 500–800 km, but there is no exact number. When the sole is visibly worn or the shoe feels less springy, it is time.

**Question: Should I switch to barefoot running?**
Answer: There is no proof that it reduces injuries, and a fast switch can overload the foot bones and the Achilles tendon. Anyone who wants to try should start with a few minutes and progress slowly over months.
