## Biomechanical Analysis: Running Shoes

Running shoes are a technological tool designed to bridge the human foot and hard running surfaces. Their effect on the runner's anatomy and physiology is a broad topic in kinesiology research. The shoe is only one component of the gear: the base layer is discussed on the [running underwear](/en/run/run_shorts/) page, and managing the load itself on the [running heart rate zones](/en/run/running-zones/) page.

### Scientific benefits

* **Shock absorption:** Running shoe midsoles, usually made of polymer materials such as EVA (ethylene-vinyl acetate), reduce the impact force returned from the ground. Studies show that this cushioning reduces the load on the joints (ankle, knee and hip) and prevents stress fractures.
* **Energy return:** New technologies, including expanded foams and carbon plates, allow more efficient use of mechanical energy. The shoe stores elastic energy on landing and releases it during push-off, which improves running economy.
* **Biomechanical correction (stability & motion control):** For runners with overpronation (the foot collapsing inward), dedicated shoes provide medial arch support. This support helps maintain alignment of the kinetic chain and prevent injuries such as Achilles tendon inflammation.

### Downsides and risks

* **Weakening of the foot muscles:** Regular use of shoes with massive support can lead to relative atrophy of the foot's intrinsic (internal) muscles. "Barefoot running" studies indicate that over-reliance on cushioning reduces the neuromuscular stimulus needed to strengthen the arch.
* **Changed landing pattern:** Shoes with a high "drop" (the height difference between heel and toes) encourage landing on the heel (heel strike). This pattern produces a sharp braking force that can increase the load on the knees, unlike the midfoot landing common in natural running.
* **An illusion of protection:** Excess cushioning can cause the runner to apply too much force to the ground without feeling it, because of reduced proprioceptive feedback (the sense of the body's position in space). Insensitivity to the surface can lead to poor running technique and chronic injuries.

## Biomechanical Comparison: Sole Types vs. Barefoot Running

The choice between highly cushioned shoes and barefoot (or minimalist) running substantially changes the kinematics of the lower limbs and the distribution of loads in the body.

### 1. Sole technology and its effects

* **Highly cushioned soles (maximalist):** These soles use thick foam that spreads out the peak impact force. The advantage is local protection for the foot, but the downside is a "disconnect" from the ground that reduces the brain's ability to make fine motor adjustments in real time.
* **Carbon-plated shoes:** This technology is not meant only for cushioning, but for leverage. The stiff plate acts as a mechanical spring that reduces the work required of the big toe joint, which improves the runner's metabolic efficiency.

### 2. Barefoot running

Running without shoes is characterized by marked physiological changes compared with running in shoes:

* **A change in the point of contact:** Barefoot runners tend to land on the forefoot (forefoot strike) or the midfoot (midfoot strike). This pattern uses the foot's longitudinal arch and the Achilles tendon as natural shock absorbers, which significantly reduces the joint load on the knees.
* **Improved proprioception:** Direct contact with the ground increases sensory input to the central nervous system. This allows a faster muscular response and strengthens the stabilizing muscles of the ankle.
* **Injury risks:** Switching to barefoot running without a long adaptation period can lead to overload of the Achilles tendon and stress fractures in the metatarsal bones, since these are not accustomed to the loads of direct impact.

### 3. Comparative summary

| Parameter | Standard running shoes | Barefoot / minimalist running |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Main landing point** | Heel (heel strike) | Forefoot/midfoot |
| **Main load** | Knees and hips | Calf muscles and Achilles tendon |
| **Muscular work** | Low in the foot | Very high in the foot |
| **Shock absorption** | External (polymer materials) | Internal (arch and tendons) |

The optimal scientific choice depends on the runner's individual anatomy, injury history and running surface. In general, shoes provide protection from environmental hazards and hard surfaces, while barefoot running promotes more natural and stronger physiological function of the foot.

## Shoe-Dependent Injury Mechanisms (Injury Pathomechanics)

The choice between cushioned and minimalist running changes the body's kinetics (the forces acting on it), and thereby dictates the potential injury profile. The medical literature distinguishes between two main patterns of overload.

### 1. Running in traditional shoes: proximal load

When runners land on the heel (heel strike) because of the extra cushioning in their shoes, an "impact transient" is created — a sharp, fast shock force that travels up the skeleton.

* **Patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS):** Known as "runner's knee". Landing on the heel with a relatively straight knee increases compressive forces in the knee joint.
* **Tibial stress fractures (shin splints/stress fractures):** The initial shock is absorbed less by the muscles and more by the shinbone (tibia), which can lead to micro-cracks in the bone if the rate of breakdown exceeds the rate of rebuilding.
* **Hip and lower back injuries:** Transmitting the unabsorbed energy up the kinetic chain can create loads in the pelvic area and the lumbar vertebrae.

### 2. Barefoot/minimalist running: distal load

Switching to landing on the balls of the feet (forefoot strike) eliminates the heel shock, but shifts the load to the soft tissues of the lower leg.

* **Achilles tendinopathy:** The Achilles tendon is required to store and release elastic energy at high intensity. Without proper adaptation, the eccentric (braking) load leads to degenerative changes in the tendon's [collagen](/en/food/collagen/) structure.
* **Metatarsalgia and metatarsal stress fractures:** The heads of the metatarsal bones absorb the full body weight on landing. Without the protection of a thick sole or a well-developed fat pad in the foot, focused pressure is created that can cause fractures.
* **Calf strain:** The calf muscles (gastrocnemius & soleus) work at a higher intensity to brake the landing, which raises the risk of microscopic tears.

## The Physiology of Adaptation (Tissue Adaptation)

Preventing injuries when switching between running styles relies on the physiological laws of tissue remodeling.

* **Wolff's Law:** Bones get stronger in response to the mechanical load placed on them. However, this process is slow; a full bone remodeling cycle takes about 3-4 months. Increasing the load too quickly doesn't allow osteoblasts (bone-building cells) to strengthen the structure before damage occurs.
* **Davis's Law:** Soft tissues (tendons and ligaments) lengthen and strengthen under constant tension. Tendons, having a poor blood supply (avascular), adapt more slowly than muscles, so they are the "weak link" in switching to minimalist running.

### Clinical recommendation for the transition

To allow proper biomechanical adaptation, the literature recommends the **"10% rule"**: increasing running volume or intensity by no more than 10% a week. When switching to minimalist shoes, it's recommended to start with tiny volumes (for example, 5 minutes of running) to allow the collagen tissue in the Achilles tendon and the bone density in the metatarsals to rebuild.

## Barefoot Running: Between Theory and Research Reality

In recent years, public interest in barefoot running and minimalist shoes has grown, based on the assumption that the modern shoe "shackles" the foot and forces an unnatural landing pattern. Below is a summary of the scientific findings and the professional reservations of podiatry associations:

### 1. Energy efficiency and performance

There is some evidence supporting the idea that barefoot running may be more energy efficient. However, **there is no research proof** that this improvement leads to better competitive performance or a reduction in the overall injury rate.

### 2. Muscle strengthening and gait mechanics

There is no doubt that running without shoes builds the foot muscles and helps develop a different walking/running pattern. Nevertheless, it's important to note:

* There is no peer-reviewed research evidence that increased muscle mass in the foot or a change in running pattern reduces injuries.
* The improvement in body awareness (proprioception) and the "connection" to the ground does exist, but its link to injury prevention remains unproven.

### 3. The myth of reduced impact

Barefoot running does reduce the force of the initial impact on landing. However, research shows that **reduced impact does not equal fewer injuries**. In practice, changing your gait can shift the load and cause injuries to the Achilles tendon and the calf muscles.

### 4. At-risk populations and clinical recommendations

The scientific research is unequivocal about certain groups:

* **Loss of protective sensation:** People with loss of sensation in their feet (for example due to diabetes) are at significantly higher risk of injuries and infections.
* **Orthotics users:** Although no studies point to a direct risk, podiatrists generally do not recommend that this population switch to barefoot running, for fear of worsening existing problems.
* **Minimalist shoes:** This solution offers partial protection from road hazards, but can create an "illusion of invulnerability" that leads to overload and injuries among beginner runners.

### 5. Safety points for the transition (Practical Guidelines)

For those who choose to try barefoot running, the scientific and medical community recommends:

1. **Choosing a surface:** Surfaces free of hazards (glass, stones, insects), and avoiding exposure to extreme temperatures.
2. **Extremely gradual progression:** Even experienced runners need a very long adaptation period for the anatomical structures in the feet and shins.
3. **Professional advice:** It's recommended to consult a podiatrist who specializes in sports medicine before making a significant change to your training regime.
4. **Reducing load at the same time:** During the adaptation period, it's a good idea to shift most of your training volume into [Zone 2](/en/run/running-zones/), where impact forces are lower.
