## Ginger

**Ginger's scientific name is *Zingiber officinale*.** In Hebrew it is usually called "ג'ינג'ר", and in standard Hebrew "זנגביל". (A point worth getting right: ginger is not horseradish. **Horseradish** — "חזרת" in Hebrew, with which ginger is sometimes confused — is a completely different plant, *Armoracia rusticana*, from the cabbage family, with an entirely different, volatile kind of pungency.)

Ginger is a rhizome from the same family as [turmeric](/en/food/turmeric/), and it is one of the few cases in which **an ancient folk remedy has stood up to clinical trials** — at least for one well-defined use: nausea. That is also where the discussion should focus, because the other claims are much weaker.

### Gingerols and shogaols — and why dried is not "weaker"

This point is almost always presented wrongly, including in professional sources.

* **Gingerols** (mainly 6-gingerol) are the main pungent compounds in **fresh ginger**.
* When dried or heated they undergo **dehydration and turn into shogaols** (6-shogaol).
* Long cooking also produces **zingerone**, which is sweeter and less pungent.

The common claim that "fresh contains more active compounds than dried" **is not accurate**. Shogaols are not a wasted breakdown product — they are **more pungent than gingerols**, and in some studies they showed **stronger** anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity.

The correct conclusion: fresh and dried ginger are **different profiles**, not strong versus weak. Fresh is bright, lemony and sharp; dried is deep, warm and concentrated. Both are active.

### Nausea — the best-established use

This is where the research is strongest, and it is worth breaking down by type.

**Nausea in pregnancy — the best evidence.** Several meta-analyses of controlled trials found that ginger **significantly reduces nausea in pregnancy** compared with placebo, and in some comparisons it was about as effective as [vitamin B6](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b6/). Leading obstetric organizations, including the American ACOG, mention ginger as an **accepted non-drug** option for nausea and vomiting in pregnancy.

**This is a point that needs correcting:** many sources issue a blanket warning against ginger in pregnancy. The research picture is the opposite — it **has been studied precisely in this context and found effective and safe in usual amounts** (up to about 1 gram a day). The caution applies to high doses and concentrated supplements, not to ginger tea or culinary use. Still — in pregnancy, consult your doctor or midwife before taking it regularly.

**Motion sickness:** mixed results. Some trials found it effective, others found no advantage over placebo. Reasonable for a personal trial, not a promise.

**Post-operative nausea:** meta-analyses found a moderate reduction in incidence, usually at a dose of about 1 gram before anesthesia.

**Chemotherapy-induced nausea:** the evidence is **weak and inconsistent**. Some studies showed improvement as an add-on to drug treatment, others did not. **Especially important:** cancer patients must consult before taking ginger, because of its effect on platelets and possible interactions.

**The proposed mechanism:** gingerols and shogaols act as antagonists of **5-HT3** (serotonin) receptors in the digestive tract — the same receptor family targeted by anti-nausea drugs such as ondansetron — and they also speed up gastric emptying.

### Other uses — what was found and what wasn't

* **Period pain (dysmenorrhea) — relatively good evidence.** Several controlled trials and meta-analyses found that ginger (about 750-2,000 mg a day during the first days of menstruation) reduces pain **about as effectively as ibuprofen or mefenamic acid** in some comparisons. The mechanism: inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis via COX — the same pathway NSAID drugs act on. This is the second-strongest use after nausea.

* **Osteoarthritis:** meta-analyses found a **statistically significant but small** improvement in pain and function. A real effect, but a modest one — not a substitute for treatment.

* **Post-exercise muscle soreness (DOMS):** several trials found a moderate reduction in pain with regular intake of about 2 grams a day. The findings are inconsistent.

* **Gastric emptying and dyspepsia:** ginger speeds up gastric emptying — an established finding in imaging studies. It may help with a feeling of fullness and slow digestion.

* **Blood sugar and blood lipids:** meta-analyses found small drops in HbA1c and fasting glucose in type 2 diabetes. The effect is small and the studies are heterogeneous — not a substitute for treatment, but not meaningless either.

### Nutrition facts — and why they're not the point

| [Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) | 0.5 mg | 5 mg |

**It should be said clearly:** a realistic serving of ginger is 5-15 grams, so **its mineral and vitamin contribution is negligible**. It should not be treated as a source of nutrients. All of its value lies in its active compounds — the gingerols and shogaols — not in this table.

### Downsides and risks

* **Heartburn and reflux — the most common side effect.** Ginger relaxes the lower esophageal sphincter and increases acidity, so it **may worsen GERD** in some people — exactly those who sometimes turn to it to improve digestion. If ginger gives you heartburn, that is a good reason to stop.

* **Anticoagulant effect.** Ginger has platelet-inhibiting activity (thromboxane inhibition). **At food doses this is negligible**, but it is relevant at high supplement doses. Consult a professional if you take warfarin, aspirin or clopidogrel, and stop supplements a week or two before surgery.

* **Lowering blood sugar and blood pressure.** A mild effect that may add up with drugs for diabetes or high blood pressure. It calls for monitoring, not necessarily avoidance.

* **Gallstones.** Ginger increases bile secretion. In people with gallstones this may trigger an attack. Consult a professional.

* **High doses:** above about 4-5 grams a day — abdominal discomfort, gas, diarrhea and mouth irritation.

* **Pregnancy:** as noted — considered effective and safe in usual amounts (up to about 1 gram a day) and one of the recommended non-drug options for nausea. **High-dose supplements are not recommended**, and use should be coordinated with your doctor or midwife.

* **Commercial ginger drinks — the big practical downside.** "Ginger ale" and most bottled ginger drinks usually contain **flavor extract or a negligible amount of real ginger, plus 25-35 grams of sugar per can**. They are not a way to consume ginger — they are a [sugary drink](/en/food/sugary-soft-drinks/). The same goes for commercial "shots" with apple juice as the first ingredient.

* **Candied ginger sweets:** candied ginger has sugar as its main ingredient.

### How much and in what form

| Form | Usual amount | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh root | 5-15 grams a day | Grated, in tea or in cooking |
| Dried powder | 1-2 grams a day (about ½-1 teaspoon) | More concentrated; rich in shogaols |
| Ginger tea | 2-3 cups | Fresh slices steeped for 10 minutes |
| Supplement (for nausea/periods) | 750-2,000 mg a day | With professional advice |

**The dose studied in most trials is about 1-1.5 grams a day** of powder, divided into several doses.

### Practical use

* **Real ginger tea:** 4-5 thin slices of fresh root, boiling water, cover and leave for 10 minutes. Covering matters — it keeps the volatile oils in the cup. Add [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) and a little [honey](/en/food/honey/) to taste.
* **Grate it from the freezer.** The best practical trick: frozen root grates easily on a grater, without peeling, and keeps for months without drying out or going moldy.
* **Peel with a teaspoon** — the skin is thin, and scraping with the edge of a spoon removes it without wasting the flesh.
* **Add it at the end of cooking** for a sharp, fresh flavor, or at the beginning for a deep, rounded one.
* **For nausea:** tea or real ginger candy, in small amounts throughout the day rather than one large dose.
* **For period pain:** start on the first day of menstruation, about 1 gram a day in divided doses — that is the protocol in most trials.
* **Read the label on drinks** — real ginger in the ingredient list, not "flavor extract", and without 30 grams of sugar.
* **Storage:** in the freezer (recommended), or in the fridge in a paper bag for up to about two weeks.

### Summary

Ginger is one of the cases in which **the folk claim has stood the test**: for reducing **nausea** — especially in pregnancy — there is a good research basis, and obstetric bodies recognize it as an accepted non-drug option. A common misunderstanding deserves correcting here: the caution about ginger in pregnancy applies to concentrated supplements, while usual amounts (up to about 1 gram a day) were studied precisely there and found effective. The second-strongest use is **period pain**, where trials found effectiveness similar to ibuprofen in some comparisons.

Two clarifications that prevent common mistakes: **dried ginger is not "weaker" than fresh** — drying turns gingerols into shogaols, which are more pungent and sometimes more active, simply different. And **commercial ginger drinks are not ginger** — usually flavor extract with 25-35 grams of sugar per can. Finally, if it gives you heartburn — that is its most common side effect, and a good reason to stop.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Does ginger really help with nausea?**
Answer: Yes, and this is its best-established use. Meta-analyses of controlled trials found a significant reduction in nausea in pregnancy, in some comparisons about as effective as vitamin B6. A moderate reduction was also found in post-operative nausea. For motion sickness the results are mixed, and for chemotherapy-induced nausea the evidence is weak. The mechanism: action on 5-HT3 receptors and faster gastric emptying.

**Question: Is ginger safe in pregnancy?**
Answer: In usual amounts — yes, and it has actually been studied precisely in this context. Up to about 1 gram a day is considered effective and safe for pregnancy nausea, and leading obstetric bodies mention it as a non-drug option. The caution applies to high-dose supplements, not to tea or culinary use. It is still advisable to coordinate with your doctor or midwife.

**Question: Does ginger help with period pain?**
Answer: There is relatively good evidence for this. Controlled trials found that about 750-2,000 mg a day during the first days of menstruation reduces pain about as effectively as ibuprofen or mefenamic acid in some comparisons. The mechanism is inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis — the same pathway NSAID drugs act on.

**Question: Which is better — fresh or dried ginger?**
Answer: They are different, not strong versus weak. In fresh ginger, gingerols dominate; when dried they turn into shogaols, which are more pungent and in some studies have stronger anti-inflammatory activity. Fresh is bright and lemony, dried is deep and concentrated. Both are active.

**Question: How much ginger can you have a day?**
Answer: 5-15 grams of fresh root, or 1-2 grams of dried powder — about half to one teaspoon. Most clinical trials used 1-1.5 grams a day in divided doses. Above 4-5 grams a day, abdominal discomfort, gas and diarrhea may appear.

**Question: What are the downsides of ginger?**
Answer: The most common is heartburn — it relaxes the esophageal sphincter and may worsen reflux. In addition: an anticoagulant effect (relevant mainly at supplement doses, and before surgery), a mild lowering of blood sugar and blood pressure that may add up with medications, and gallbladder stimulation in people with gallstones.

**Question: Is ginger ale healthy?**
Answer: No. Most commercial ginger drinks contain flavor extract or a negligible amount of real ginger, along with 25-35 grams of sugar per can. Nutritionally it is a sugary drink, not a way to consume ginger. Read the label and look for real ginger in the ingredient list.

**Question: How do you make ginger tea?**
Answer: 4-5 thin slices of fresh root, boiling water, cover and leave for 10 minutes. Covering matters — it stops the volatile oils from evaporating. You can add lemon and a little honey. 2-3 cups a day is a reasonable amount.

**Question: How should you store ginger?**
Answer: The best way is in the freezer — frozen root grates easily without peeling, and keeps for months without drying out or going moldy. In the fridge, in a paper bag, it keeps for about two weeks.

**Question: Does ginger help with joint pain?**
Answer: To a limited extent. Meta-analyses in osteoarthritis found a statistically significant but small improvement in pain and function. It is a real but modest effect, and not a substitute for medical treatment.
