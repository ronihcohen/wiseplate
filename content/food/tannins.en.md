Tannins are a diverse group of water-soluble polyphenolic compounds found in abundance in the plant world. Their name comes from the ancient word for processing hides ("tanning"), since in the past tannin-rich extracts (such as those from oak bark) were used to turn animal hides into durable leather that would not rot.

### Their role in nature
For the plant, tannins are a **defense mechanism**. Their bitter taste and the drying sensation they create in the mouth deter insects and animals from eating the leaves, unripe fruit or seeds before they have matured.

### The sensory experience: astringency
Tannins are responsible for the familiar "dry mouth" or rough feeling after a sip of heavy red wine, tea left steeping too long, or eating an unripe persimmon.
*   **How does it happen?** Tannins bind to proteins in our saliva, make them precipitate and strip away the mouth's natural lubricating layer. The result is increased friction between the tongue and the palate.

### Where are they found?
*   **Tea:** mainly in black and green tea (leaves of *Camellia sinensis*).
*   **Wine:** in grape skins and seeds (and also in the wooden barrels in which wine is aged).
*   **Chocolate:** in dark cocoa.
*   **Fruit:** [pomegranate](/en/food/pomegranate/), cranberries, persimmon.
*   **Nuts:** in the thin brown skin of [walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/) and [almonds](/en/food/almonds/).

### Health benefits and downsides
Like many things in nutrition, tannins are a double-edged sword:
*   **The good:** they are very strong antioxidants. They have anti-inflammatory, antibacterial and antiviral properties. Studies point to potential for lowering blood pressure and improving heart health.
*   **The bad:** they are known as "anti-nutrients" when it comes to mineral absorption. They bind to plant-based [iron](/en/food/iron/) and prevent its absorption. In large amounts they may also cause stomach irritation and nausea.

### Frequently asked questions about tannins

**Question: Are some people sensitive to tannins?**
Answer: **Yes.** Some people experience headaches, migraines or stomach pain after consuming tannin-rich foods (such as red wine and [dark chocolate](/en/food/dark-chocolate/)). This is sometimes known as the "red wine headache," although the exact cause may be a combination with other substances (such as histamine or sulfites).

**Question: How do you reduce the bitterness of tannins in tea?**
Answer: The secret is **controlling the steeping**: don't leave the tea bag in the cup forever. Most of the [caffeine](/en/food/supplements/caffeine/) and flavor come out in the first 2 minutes. The bitter tannins are released more slowly, over time. Use water at the right temperature (80 degrees Celsius, 176°F, for [green tea](/en/food/green-tea/)). Boiling water "burns" the leaf and releases tannins immediately. Adding [milk](/en/food/milk/) binds the tannins and neutralizes the bitterness (but slightly reduces the antioxidants).

**Question: Are they safe?**
Answer: In the reasonable amounts eaten in food — **absolutely.** The body knows how to handle them, and we benefit from their antioxidant advantages. Problems arise only with very excessive intake, or for people with a particularly sensitive stomach or severe [iron](/en/food/iron/) deficiency (who should be careful about combining them with meals).

**Question: Does cooking fruit reduce tannins?**
Answer: **Yes.** Cooking or baking (as in [pears](/en/food/pear/) poached in wine or quince jam) breaks down some of the tannins and softens the astringent taste. Ripening the fruit (like a [banana](/en/food/banana/) going from unripe to ripe) also dramatically reduces the amount of active tannins.
