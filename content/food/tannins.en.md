## Tannins

**Tannins are a group of plant polyphenols that bind to proteins, which is why they create the dry, puckering feeling in the mouth after black tea, red wine or an unripe persimmon; their best-established health effect is reducing the absorption of plant iron when eaten with a meal.** Their antioxidant benefits have a laboratory basis but little proof in people. In Hebrew they are called "טאנינים".

The name comes from tanning: for centuries, tannin-rich extracts such as oak bark were used to turn animal hides into durable leather. The same property, binding to proteins and making them precipitate, is what you feel in your mouth.

### Types and food sources

There are two main groups: **hydrolyzable tannins**, such as tannic acid and the ellagitannins in pomegranate and nuts, and **condensed tannins** (proanthocyanidins), found in tea, wine, cocoa and grapes. The amount in a food varies widely with variety, ripeness and processing, so the table gives a relative estimate:

| Food | Tannin content (relative) | Main type |
|---|---|---|
| Strongly brewed black tea | high | condensed and theaflavins |
| [Green tea](/en/food/green-tea/) | medium-high | catechins and condensed |
| Red wine | high | condensed (from skins and seeds) |
| Cocoa and [dark chocolate](/en/food/dark-chocolate/) | high | condensed |
| [Pomegranate](/en/food/pomegranate/) (peel and juice) | high | hydrolyzable (punicalagin) |
| Unripe persimmon | very high | condensed |
| Brown skin of [walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/) and [almonds](/en/food/almonds/) | medium | hydrolyzable and condensed |
| Dark beans (red and black) | medium | condensed, in the skin |
| White wine | low | - |

### Benefits

**Antioxidants.** In the test tube, tannins are strong antioxidants, and they also have antibacterial and antifungal activity [(Chung et al., 1998)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9759559/). In the body the picture is more complicated: most large tannins are not absorbed intact but are broken down by gut bacteria into smaller compounds whose effects are still being studied.

**Heart and blood pressure.** Foods rich in tannins and related polyphenols, such as tea and cocoa, have been linked in trials to small drops in blood pressure. In a meta-analysis of trials, chocolate lowered systolic blood pressure by about 6 mmHg in the short trials included, and green tea lowered LDL [(Hooper et al., 2008)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18614722/). The whole effect cannot be credited to tannins specifically, because these foods contain many polyphenols.

**Gut.** Tannins reach the colon largely intact, where they feed certain bacteria and shift the makeup of the microbiome. The practical health outcome is still unclear. Their astringent effect is also why strong tea was once a folk remedy for diarrhea.

### Downsides

**Iron absorption.** This is the best-established downside. Tannins bind non-heme iron (from plants and supplements) and form complexes the body does not absorb. In a human study, tea drunk with a meal inhibited iron absorption from bread, rice and an iron supplement, even when vitamin C was present [(Disler et al., 1975)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1168162/). Heme iron from meat is affected less [(NIH: iron)](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/).

**But not equally in everyone.** A review found a gap: single-meal studies show a clear drop in absorption, but long-term and population studies show little or no drop in iron stores in healthy people, perhaps because the body adapts [(Delimont et al., 2017)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29955693/). People at risk of iron deficiency, such as vegetarians, women with heavy periods, pregnant women and children, should still keep tea and coffee away from meals.

**Sensitive stomachs.** In large amounts, for example very strong tea on an empty stomach, tannins can cause nausea and discomfort.

**Proteins and digestive enzymes.** Because they bind proteins, very high tannin intakes slightly reduce protein digestion. In a normal diet this is not significant.

**Red wine headaches.** Some people get headaches from red wine and chocolate. Tannins are one suspect, along with histamine, alcohol and other compounds, and the exact cause is unknown.

### Practical use

- **Timing:** if you need iron, drink tea and coffee an hour before or after meals rather than with them.
- **Vitamin C:** including [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) in the meal (pepper, lemon, tomato) improves iron absorption and offsets part of the effect.
- **Bitter tea:** short steeping (2–3 minutes) and cooler water for green tea (about 80°C) reduce the tannins that end up in the cup.
- **Milk:** [milk](/en/food/milk/) binds some tannins and softens astringency.
- **Fruit:** ripening sharply lowers active tannins, which is why a ripe persimmon or [banana](/en/food/banana/) is not astringent. Cooking, for example poached [pear](/en/food/pear/), softens them too.

### Summary

Tannins are polyphenols that give tea, wine, cocoa and pomegranate their astringency. They are antioxidants in the test tube, and foods rich in them are linked to small, positive effects on blood pressure, but their most certain effect is lowering the absorption of plant iron from the same meal. For most people that does not matter; for those at risk of iron deficiency, the fix is simple: keep tea and coffee away from meals.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What are tannins?**
Answer: A group of plant polyphenols that bind to proteins. They give the dry, bitter feel of tea, red wine and unripe fruit, and they protect the plant from animals and insects.

**Question: Do tannins in tea block iron absorption?**
Answer: Yes, when tea is drunk with a meal. They reduce absorption of plant iron and iron from supplements. Drinking tea an hour before or after meals avoids most of the effect.

**Question: Are tannins healthy?**
Answer: At the amounts found in food they are safe, and foods rich in them, such as tea and cocoa, are linked to small benefits for the heart. The benefits measured in the test tube have not been shown as such in people.

**Question: Do tannins cause wine headaches?**
Answer: Possibly in some people, but it has not been proven. Histamine, alcohol and other compounds in wine are also suspects.

**Question: How can I make tea less bitter?**
Answer: Steep it for less time (2–3 minutes), use cooler water for green tea, and add a little milk if you like. Strong bitterness is a sign of over-steeping.

**Question: Does white wine contain tannins?**
Answer: Very little. Most tannins in red wine come from the skins and seeds, which stay with the juice during fermentation; in white wine they are removed early.
