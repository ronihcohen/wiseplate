## What Is EGCG?

**EGCG (epigallocatechin gallate) is the main catechin in green tea, and a cup of green tea contains about 20–50 mg of it; in tea it is safe, but in concentrated supplements at 800 mg a day or more it has been linked to liver injury.** The benefits measured in the lab, such as "fat burning" and protection against cancer, have weak support in people. In Hebrew it is written "EGCG" or "אפיגלוקטכין גלאט".

Catechins are a subfamily of [polyphenols](/en/food/polyphenols/), and they make up a substantial share of the dry weight of green tea leaves. EGCG accounts for about half or more of the catechins in the leaf. When a supplement label promises "green tea extract", the extract is usually standardized to its percentage of catechins or EGCG. In black tea, most catechins are converted into theaflavins and thearubigins during oxidation, so it contains far less EGCG.

For the drink itself see [green tea](/en/food/green-tea/), which also covers green tea extract supplements in more detail.

### How much EGCG is in a cup of tea?

| Source | EGCG (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Matcha (2 g powder) | 70–130 mg |
| Japanese green tea (gyokuro), cup | 60–90 mg |
| Ordinary green tea, cup (240 ml) | 20–50 mg |
| White tea, cup | 10–30 mg |
| Black tea, cup | about 5 mg |
| Green tea extract supplement, capsule | 100–400 mg or more |
| Level EFSA flagged as a concern in supplements | 800 mg a day or more |

The amount in tea varies widely with variety, harvest, water temperature and steeping time. Three to five cups of green tea a day provide roughly 100–250 mg of EGCG.

### Benefits

#### Weight and "fat burning"

In the test tube, EGCG inhibits COMT, an enzyme that breaks down noradrenaline, which led to the idea that it slightly boosts fat breakdown, especially together with [caffeine](/en/food/supplements/caffeine/). In practice, a Cochrane review found that in trials outside Japan, green tea preparations led to an average weight loss of only 0.04 kg compared with placebo, essentially zero; trials in Japan showed better but inconsistent results [(Jurgens et al., Cochrane 2012)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23235664/). The conclusion: EGCG is not a meaningful weight-loss tool.

#### Blood pressure and blood lipids

A meta-analysis of controlled trials found that green tea and catechins slightly lowered blood pressure (by about 2 mmHg on average) as well as total and LDL cholesterol, with a larger effect in people with higher blood pressure [(Khalesi et al., 2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24861099/). In observational studies, regular green tea drinking has been linked with a lower risk of stroke and heart disease, but that is a statistical association, not proof. Proposed mechanisms include protecting LDL from oxidation and improving the function of the blood vessel wall.

#### Brain

EGCG crosses the blood-brain barrier to some degree. In mice it reduced the build-up of amyloid and tau proteins linked to Alzheimer's disease and protected neurons in models of Parkinson's disease. In people there are only epidemiological links between tea drinking and a lower risk of cognitive decline, with no proof that EGCG is the reason.

#### Cancer

Lab and animal studies showed that EGCG inhibits blood vessel formation in tumors and triggers the death of cancer cells. In people the picture is different: the concentrations used on cells in the lab are far higher than what reaches the blood after drinking tea or taking a supplement, and clinical studies have not shown clear protection. EGCG should not be seen as a way to prevent or treat cancer.

#### Blood sugar

In the test tube EGCG inhibits enzymes that break down starch in the gut and improves glucose uptake by muscle cells. In human trials the effect on fasting glucose and HbA1c is small and inconsistent, and it is not a treatment for [insulin resistance](/en/food/supplements/insulin/).

#### Inflammation

In lab studies EGCG inhibits the NF-κB inflammation pathway and the COX-2 enzyme. It is unclear whether these effects occur in people at ordinary doses.

### Absorption

EGCG is poorly absorbed: most of it is broken down in the gut, modified in the liver or excreted. Taking it on an empty stomach greatly increases absorption, and that is exactly why it raises the risk of liver injury [(USP, Oketch-Rabah et al., 2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32140423/). In lab studies [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) (for example [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) in tea) kept catechins more stable under simulated digestion, but there is no evidence that this changes health outcomes.

### Downsides and side effects

**Liver injury from concentrated supplements.** This is the most important downside. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) reviewed the evidence in 2018 and found that EGCG intakes from supplements of 800 mg a day or more were associated with raised liver enzymes, and that no safe dose for supplements could be identified; catechins from brewed tea, by contrast, are considered safe [(EFSA: safety of green tea catechins, 2018)](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2018.5239). Following this, the EU restricted the EGCG content of supplements. A United States Pharmacopeia review found case reports of liver injury at 140 to about 1,000 mg of EGCG a day, with large differences between individuals, and set a warning: do not take on an empty stomach, do not use with liver disease, and stop at once if abdominal pain, dark urine or jaundice appear [(USP, Oketch-Rabah et al., 2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32140423/). The problem concerns supplements, not drinking tea.

**Nausea and stomach pain.** Catechins and [tannins](/en/food/tannins/) irritate the stomach lining, especially on an empty stomach.

**Iron absorption.** EGCG binds plant [iron](/en/food/iron/) and reduces its absorption. People who are iron deficient, vegetarians and vegans should keep tea and supplements an hour or two away from iron-rich meals and iron supplements.

**Medication.** Green tea and its extracts can affect the absorption and breakdown of some drugs, such as the beta-blocker nadolol and the chemotherapy drug bortezomib. Anyone on regular medication, especially anticoagulants, should check with a pharmacist before taking a supplement.

**Caffeine.** Many extracts contain caffeine, which can cause restlessness and sleep problems.

**Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** EGCG supplements are not recommended. In the test tube EGCG inhibits an enzyme involved in [folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/) metabolism, so high doses are best avoided at a time when folate matters most. Moderate tea drinking, within caffeine limits, is a different matter.

### Practical use

- **From tea, not capsules:** 2–4 cups of green tea or a cup of matcha a day provide EGCG in safe amounts.
- **Brewing:** water at 70–80°C and 2–3 minutes of steeping give a less bitter taste and keep more catechins.
- **If you do take a supplement:** no more than 300–400 mg of EGCG a day, always with food, and not at all with liver disease or heavy drinking.
- **Warning signs:** abdominal pain, unusual tiredness, dark urine or yellowing of the skin or eyes mean stop and see a doctor.
- **Iron:** separate tea and supplements from iron-rich meals.

### Summary

EGCG is the main catechin in green tea and has been studied extensively, but most of the impressive results come from test tubes and animals. In people there are small drops in blood pressure and cholesterol and almost no effect on weight. Drinking green tea is safe; concentrated supplements, especially above 800 mg of EGCG a day and on an empty stomach, are linked to liver injury. If you want EGCG, the right way is a cup of tea.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is EGCG?**
Answer: Epigallocatechin gallate, the main catechin in green tea and a member of the polyphenol family. A cup of green tea provides about 20–50 mg. It is absorbed in small amounts, so its effects in people are much milder than those measured in the lab.

**Question: Is green tea extract dangerous for the liver?**
Answer: At high doses, yes. EFSA found that 800 mg or more of EGCG a day from supplements was linked to raised liver enzymes, and liver injury has been reported at lower doses too, mainly when taken on an empty stomach. Drinking green tea has not been linked to such problems.

**Question: Is an EGCG supplement better than drinking green tea?**
Answer: Green tea or matcha is better. The positive observational studies were done on the drink, which provides EGCG in safe amounts along with L-theanine and other compounds. The supplement has no proven advantage and carries a liver risk.

**Question: Does EGCG help with weight loss?**
Answer: Barely. In the Cochrane review, trials outside Japan found an average weight loss of 0.04 kg with green tea preparations compared with placebo. It is no substitute for diet and exercise.

**Question: Why does green tea or EGCG make me feel sick?**
Answer: Catechins irritate the stomach lining, especially on an empty stomach. Drinking or taking it after a light meal solves most cases, and for supplements it also lowers the liver risk.

**Question: Does matcha contain more EGCG?**
Answer: Yes. With matcha you drink the whole ground leaf, so a cup contains several times more EGCG than a cup of ordinary green tea, and more caffeine too.

**Question: Does EGCG interact with medication?**
Answer: It may affect the absorption and breakdown of some drugs, such as certain beta-blockers and chemotherapy drugs, and it reduces absorption of iron supplements. If you take regular medication, check with a pharmacist before using a supplement.

**Question: Does milk in tea cancel the benefits?**
Answer: Possibly to some extent. [Milk](/en/food/milk/) proteins bind catechins, and in a study on black tea, adding milk cancelled the improvement in blood vessel function. How much this matters in practice is unclear.
