## White Rice (*Oryza sativa*)

Rice feeds about half the world's population, and it is also the easiest grain to attack. "White rice = empty carbs" is a common rule of thumb, and it is roughly true — but the full picture is more interesting, and it includes three practical things most people don't know:

1. **Rice concentrates inorganic arsenic from the soil** more than any other common crop — and there is a simple cooking method that removes most of it.
2. **Brown rice contains more arsenic than white rice**, not less — because it is concentrated in the bran.
3. **Cooking and chilling rice creates resistant starch** and changes its glycemic response.

### Nutrition facts per 100 grams (cooked)

|---|---|---|---|
| Calories | 130 | 130 | 123 |
| Protein | 2.7 g | 2.7 g | 2.7 g |
| Carbohydrates | 28.2 g | 28.2 g | 25.6 g |
| **Fiber** | **0.4 g** | 0.4 g | **1.6 g** |
| Fat | 0.3 g | 0.3 g | 1.0 g |
| [Magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) | 12 mg (3%) | 12 mg | **39 mg (9%)** |
| [Vitamin B1](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b1/) | 0.02 mg (2%) | 0.16 mg (13%) | 0.10 mg (8%) |
| [Folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/) | 3 mcg | 58 mcg (15%) | 4 mcg |
| [Iron](/en/food/iron/) | 0.2 mg (1%) | 1.2 mg (7%) | 0.6 mg (3%) |
| [Manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/) | 0.47 mg (20%) | 0.47 mg | 0.97 mg (42%) |

**What exactly refining removes:** white rice is the grain without the bran and without the germ — that is, without most of the fiber, magnesium, B vitamins and oils. All that remains is the endosperm, which is almost pure starch.

**And why it is done:** not just for texture. The oils in the germ oxidize, so **brown rice spoils within 3-6 months while white rice keeps for years**. In regions without refrigeration, this was a perfectly sensible decision.

### Arsenic — the topic that doesn't get talked about enough

The rice plant grows in flooded fields and absorbs **inorganic arsenic** from the soil and water far more efficiently than other crops. Inorganic arsenic is classified as carcinogenic to humans.

Three practical points:

* **Brown rice contains more, not less.** The arsenic is concentrated in the bran — exactly the part that refining removes. This is one of the few cases in which the white version is better in some respect.
* **The cooking method makes a significant difference.** Cooking in a large amount of water (a 6:1 ratio or more) and draining the excess removes a considerable part of the arsenic — according to studies, 40-60%. Rinsing beforehand removes more. Cooking at a 1:2 ratio in a covered pot, where all the water is absorbed, keeps the arsenic in the dish.
* **Who needs to be careful:** babies and toddlers (low body weight relative to intake), pregnant women, and people for whom rice is the mainstay of their diet. Health authorities recommend not relying on rice porridge as the only food for infants.

**This does not mean "stop eating rice"** — but rather vary your grains and cook using the lots-of-water method.

### Resistant starch — what happens when you chill it

When rice is cooked, its starch undergoes **gelatinization** and becomes accessible to digestive enzymes. When it cools, some of it undergoes **retrogradation** — the molecules rearrange into a crystalline structure that enzymes struggle to break down. The result is **resistant starch**: a carbohydrate that behaves like dietary fiber.

What this means:
* **Fewer available calories** from the same amount of rice.
* **A more moderate glycemic response.**
* **A substrate for bacteria in the colon**, which produce short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate from it.

**Important:** reheating does **not** cancel most of the effect — most of the resistant starch formed during chilling remains. So rice cooked yesterday, chilled in the fridge and reheated today behaves better than fresh rice.

**But — only with proper chilling.** Cooked rice left at room temperature is [a real safety risk](/en/food/reheated_rice_dangers/) because of **Bacillus cereus**, a bacterium whose spores survive cooking and produce a heat-stable toxin. **Chill within an hour; don't leave it on the counter.**

### Benefits

* **Especially easy to digest.** Low in fiber, fat and fermentable compounds — so it is suitable during gastroenteritis, after surgery, during an inflammatory bowel disease flare-up and with nausea.
* **Low FODMAP.** One of the safest carbohydrate sources for people with irritable bowel syndrome.
* **[Gluten-free](/en/food/gluten/)** — suitable for celiac disease.
* **Available energy after exertion.** In the window after intense training, a fast carbohydrate is an advantage, not a drawback — it refills glycogen stores.
* **[Manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/):** 20% of the daily value per 100 grams cooked.
* **Enriched versions** provide significant amounts of [folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/), [iron](/en/food/iron/) and [vitamin B1](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b1/).
* **Price, availability and shelf life** — real advantages.
* **Less arsenic than brown rice** — as detailed above.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Almost no fiber.** 0.4 grams per 100 grams. Low satiety relative to calories.
* **High glycemic index.** It depends on the variety — jasmine rice reaches a GI of 70-80, while basmati rice, thanks to its high amylose content, is much lower (50-58). **Switching from jasmine to basmati is an upgrade with no change in habits.**
* **Arsenic** — as detailed above.
* **[Bacillus cereus](/en/food/reheated_rice_dangers/)** with improper storage — as detailed above.
* **Low micronutrient density.** Without enrichment, white rice contributes very little besides energy.
* **An easy portion to overdo.** A plate of rice at a restaurant is sometimes 300-400 grams, that is, 390-520 calories — a much larger carbohydrate portion than planned.

### Practical use

* **Rinse until the water runs clear** — removes excess starch, dust and some of the arsenic.
* **Cook in lots of water and drain.** 6 cups of water per cup of rice, cooked uncovered, drained at the end. The most effective method against arsenic, and it also produces separate, fluffy grains.
* **Chill and use it tomorrow** — resistant starch and a better glycemic response. But **chill within an hour of cooking**, in a shallow container.
* **Basmati instead of jasmine** — a significantly lower glycemic index, the same use.
* **Combine with protein, fat and fiber.** Rice with [lentils](/en/food/lentil/), vegetables and [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) produces a completely different glycemic response from rice on its own.
* **Vary your grains.** [Buckwheat](/en/food/buckwheat/), [quinoa](/en/food/quinoa/), [barley](/en/food/barley/), [teff](/en/food/teff/) — variety reduces both arsenic exposure and dietary monotony.
* **Measure.** A reasonable carbohydrate portion is 150-200 grams cooked (about 200-260 calories).
* **Brown rice — store it in the fridge.** The oils in the germ oxidize, and refrigeration extends the shelf life.

### Summary

White rice is exactly what it is: a clean, cheap, easily digested source of carbohydrate that is low in everything else. It has a clear place — after training, in sensitive digestive conditions, as the base of a meal with legumes and vegetables. Three practical points are worth more than any "white versus brown" debate: **cook in lots of water and drain** (arsenic), **chill and eat the next day** (resistant starch), and **chill within an hour** ([Bacillus cereus](/en/food/reheated_rice_dangers/)). And if you want one effortless upgrade — **basmati instead of jasmine**.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is the nutritional value of white rice?**
Answer: 100 grams of cooked white rice provide 130 calories, 2.7 grams of protein, 28.2 grams of carbohydrates and only 0.4 grams of fiber. It is low in micronutrients — 3% of the daily value of magnesium and 2% of vitamin B1 — unless it is enriched rice, which provides 15% of folate and 13% of vitamin B1.

**Question: Is there arsenic in rice, and what can you do about it?**
Answer: Yes. The rice plant grows in flooded fields and absorbs inorganic arsenic from the soil more efficiently than other crops. The practical solution is the cooking method: rinsing beforehand and cooking in a large amount of water (6 cups per cup of rice) and draining at the end removes 40-60% of the arsenic. Cooking at a 1:2 ratio in a covered pot keeps it in the dish.

**Question: Which is healthier — white rice or brown rice?**
Answer: On most measures, brown rice: four times the fiber, three times the magnesium and twice the manganese. But there is a significant exception — the arsenic is concentrated in the bran, so brown rice contains more arsenic than white. White rice is also much easier to digest, which makes it more suitable in sensitive gut conditions.

**Question: Is it right to chill rice before eating it?**
Answer: Yes, and there is a basis for it. Chilling after cooking causes some of the starch to undergo retrogradation and become resistant starch — a carbohydrate that behaves like fiber, provides fewer available calories and produces a more moderate glycemic response. Reheating does not cancel most of the effect. It is important to chill within an hour of cooking for safety reasons.

**Question: Why is it dangerous to leave cooked rice out?**
Answer: Because of Bacillus cereus, a bacterium whose spores survive cooking. In rice left at room temperature they germinate and produce a heat-stable toxin — meaning reheating does not neutralize it. The rule: chill rice within an hour of cooking, in a shallow container that cools quickly, and don't leave it on the counter for hours.

**Question: Which rice is best for people with diabetes?**
Answer: Basmati. Its high amylose content gives it a glycemic index of 50-58, compared with 70-80 for jasmine rice. Switching from jasmine to basmati is a significant upgrade with no change in habits. In addition, cooking and chilling before eating, and combining with legumes, vegetables and oil, further moderate the glycemic response.

**Question: Why is rice refined at all?**
Answer: Not just for texture. The oils in the germ oxidize, so brown rice spoils within 3-6 months while white rice keeps for years. In regions without refrigeration this was a sensible decision that made it possible to store a staple food. For this reason it is best to store brown rice in the fridge.

**Question: How much rice is a reasonable portion?**
Answer: 150-200 grams cooked, that is, about 200-260 calories. By comparison, a plate of rice at a restaurant is sometimes 300-400 grams — 390-520 calories, a much larger carbohydrate portion than planned. Combining it with protein, vegetables and olive oil substantially changes the glycemic response to the same amount.
