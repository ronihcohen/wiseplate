## Moringa Powder

**Moringa powder is the ground, dried leaves of the moringa tree; by weight it is rich in calcium, iron, vitamin A and protein, but a normal serving of one teaspoon (about 3 g) provides small amounts of each, and small trials have found a modest effect on blood sugar and not much more.** It is a reasonable green addition to food, not a replacement for a varied diet or a vitamin supplement. In Hebrew it is called "אבקת מורינגה".

The moringa tree (Moringa oleifera) is native to northern India and now grows across many tropical regions. Almost every part of it is eaten or used in traditional medicine, but leaf powder is the common product. Because of its nutritional value, aid organizations promote it in areas with undernutrition, where large amounts of fresh leaves are a meaningful addition to a poor diet.

### Nutrition facts

The composition of moringa powder varies widely with growing region, leaf age and drying method. Values are an approximate range from different sources:

| Nutrient | Per 100 g powder (approx.) | Per teaspoon, about 3 g (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 250–300 | about 9 |
| Protein | 25–29 g | about 0.8 g |
| Fiber | 19–30 g | about 0.6–0.9 g |
| Calcium | 1,500–2,000 mg | about 45–60 mg |
| Iron | 15–28 mg | about 0.5–0.8 mg |
| Potassium | 1,300–1,500 mg | about 40–45 mg |
| Vitamin A (as beta-carotene) | very high | about 10–20% of daily needs |
| Vitamin C | low, mostly lost in drying | negligible |
| Caffeine | none | none |

The popular comparisons ("more calcium than milk, more vitamin C than oranges") refer to a gram of leaves, often fresh. A teaspoon of powder contains less calcium than 50 ml of milk, and vitamin C barely survives drying. For comparison, an adult needs about 1,000 mg of [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) and 8–18 mg of [iron](/en/food/iron/) a day [(NIH: calcium)](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/).

### Benefits

**Blood sugar: promising but early.** In a 12-week double-blind trial in people with prediabetes, 2.4 g of leaf powder a day (six capsules) slightly lowered fasting glucose and HbA1c compared with placebo, in which the values rose [(Gómez-Martínez et al., 2021)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35010932/). In another trial, adding 20 g of powder to a meal reduced the post-meal rise in blood sugar in people with diabetes but not in healthy people, and the researchers noted that the taste of that amount was poorly accepted [(Leone et al., 2018)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30322091/). These are small trials, and longer studies are needed.

**Antioxidants.** The leaves contain [polyphenols](/en/food/polyphenols/) such as quercetin and chlorogenic acid, and glucosinolates that give rise to isothiocyanates. A review found that in animals and in the test tube, leaf extracts have antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective activity, but human studies are few and use inconsistent products and doses [(Stohs et al., 2015)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25808883/).

**A convenient green addition.** In households or regions where the diet is low in vegetables, leaf powder can add a little calcium, iron, vitamin A and fiber. In a typical diet that already includes leafy greens, its contribution is modest.

**Complete protein, in small amounts.** The leaves contain all the essential amino acids, like [quinoa](/en/food/quinoa/) and [spirulina](/en/food/spirulina/), but a teaspoon has less than a gram of protein, so this has no practical significance.

### Downsides

**Digestion.** At high doses moringa powder can cause diarrhea, gas and stomach pain. Start with half a teaspoon and increase gradually.

**Diabetes and blood pressure medication.** Because of its possible effect on blood sugar and blood pressure, combining it with diabetes or blood pressure drugs may add to their effect. If you take such medication and want to use moringa regularly, tell your doctor. Moringa is also rich in vitamin K, so people on warfarin need to keep their intake steady.

**Pregnancy.** The leaves are considered safe as food, but the root and bark have a traditional use for inducing abortion and may cause uterine contractions. In pregnancy it is best to avoid moringa supplements and extracts, and certainly root or bark products.

**Breastfeeding.** Moringa is used traditionally to increase breast milk, but the evidence is limited to small trials. Anyone struggling with breastfeeding will benefit more from a lactation consultant.

**Quality and heavy metals.** Like other herbal supplements, moringa powders are not regulated like drugs and may contain contaminants or heavy metals depending on the soil. Choose a brand that publishes lab tests.

**Price.** For the nutrition it provides, the powder is expensive compared with spinach, parsley or other leafy greens.

### Practical use

- **Amount:** half to one teaspoon a day (1.5–3 g). Trials used 2.4 g a day.
- **In smoothies:** a teaspoon in a smoothie with [banana](/en/food/banana/), spinach or [apple](/en/food/apple/) hides the grassy taste.
- **In tahini:** a teaspoon stirred into raw tahini gives a vivid green color without changing the [sesame](/en/food/sesame-seeds/) flavor much.
- **On soups and stews:** sprinkle at the end of cooking. Long heating damages some of the vitamins.
- **With fat:** vitamin A and other fat-soluble components are absorbed better with olive oil or tahini.
- **Storage:** in an airtight jar in a cool, dark place. Good powder is bright green; a khaki or yellowish color suggests heat drying or long storage.

### Summary

Moringa powder is a dried green leaf with good nutrition for its weight, but a teaspoon contributes little calcium, iron and vitamin A. Small trials found a modest effect on blood sugar, and the other claims rest mainly on lab studies. It is safe as food in most cases; take care in pregnancy and with diabetes, blood pressure or anticoagulant medication, and choose a tested product.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much moringa powder a day?**
Answer: Half to one teaspoon (1.5–3 g) is a common amount. The blood sugar trial used 2.4 g a day. There is no proven benefit from higher doses, and they increase digestive side effects.

**Question: Is moringa a "natural multivitamin"?**
Answer: No. By weight it is rich in minerals and vitamin A, but a teaspoon contains small amounts. Most of the vitamin C is destroyed in drying, and it provides no meaningful vitamin B12 or vitamin D.

**Question: Does moringa powder contain caffeine?**
Answer: No. It is caffeine-free, unlike matcha and [green tea](/en/food/green-tea/). Any energy boost people report does not come from caffeine.

**Question: Can I take moringa during pregnancy?**
Answer: It is better to avoid supplements and extracts in pregnancy. The root and bark may cause uterine contractions, and there is not enough information on the safety of concentrated leaf doses.

**Question: Does moringa lower blood sugar?**
Answer: Small trials found slight drops in fasting glucose and HbA1c in people with prediabetes. It is not a replacement for treatment, and anyone on diabetes medication should talk to their doctor.

**Question: Powder or capsules?**
Answer: Most capsules are the same powder inside a shell. The powder is cheaper and easy to add to food; capsules suit people who dislike the taste.
