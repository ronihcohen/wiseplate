## Red Bell Pepper (*Capsicum annuum*)

There is a fact worth opening with, because it contradicts a common intuition: **a red bell pepper contains three times more [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) than an orange.** 127.7 mg per 100 grams compared with 53 mg — meaning one medium bell pepper provides almost twice the daily value, for 45 calories. In Israel the bell pepper is known as "גמבה" (gamba).

And a second fact that also surprises many people: **green and red bell peppers are the same fruit.** The green one was simply picked earlier. The time it stayed on the plant is the entire nutritional difference between them.

**For comparison, per 100 grams:** [orange](/en/food/orange/) — 53 mg of vitamin C. [Kiwi](/en/food/kiwi/) — 92 mg. [Broccoli](/en/food/broccoli/) — 89 mg. **Red bell pepper — 128 mg. Yellow bell pepper — 183 mg.**

### Green, yellow, red — the same fruit at different stages

The green bell pepper is **an unripe fruit**. When it stays on the plant, it goes on to turn yellow, orange and finally red, and along the way three things happen:

* **The chlorophyll breaks down** and carotenoids build up in its place — mainly **capsanthin**, the red pigment unique to the Capsicum family, alongside beta-carotene, zeaxanthin and lutein.
* **The [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) content rises** — from 80 mg in green to 128 in red and 183 in yellow.
* **Sugar rises and bitterness falls** — which is why red is sweet and green tastes sharp and bitter.

This is also the reason for the price: the red fruit occupied space on the plant for longer.

**And what a bell pepper doesn't have:** **capsaicin**. The sweet bell pepper is the only variety of *Capsicum annuum* with almost no capsaicin — the compound responsible for the heat in chili peppers and hot peppers. A bell pepper is not "a mild version of a hot pepper"; it is a variety bred for a complete absence of heat.

### Vitamin C and cooking — how much is left

[Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) is the bell pepper's strongest nutrient, and also **the most vulnerable**: water-soluble, heat-sensitive and oxidized by air.

| Preparation | Estimated vitamin C retention |
|---|---|
| Raw | 100% |
| Brief steaming | 80-90% |
| Quick stir-frying | 70-80% |
| Oven roasting | 50-65% |
| Boiling in water | 40-55% (the rest stays in the pot) |

**The conclusion:** if you want the vitamin, you need to eat some of the bell pepper **raw**. If you cook it, you haven't lost everything: the carotenoids (vitamin A, capsanthin, zeaxanthin) are actually **released better by cooking**, because heat breaks down the cell wall.

**The practical conclusion:** eat both — raw slices in a salad and roasted bell pepper in a hot dish — and make sure there is fat in the meal, because carotenoids are fat-soluble.

### Benefits

* **An exceptional amount of [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/):** 142% of the daily value per 100 grams in red, and 204% in yellow. Supports [collagen](/en/food/collagen/) production, immune function and the absorption of non-heme [iron](/en/food/iron/).
* **Carotenoids:** capsanthin (unique to Capsicum), beta-carotene, zeaxanthin and lutein — the last two accumulate in the macula of the eye.
* **[Vitamin A](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-a/):** 17% of the daily value in red, compared with only 2% in green.
* **[Vitamin B6](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b6/):** 17% — important for amino acid metabolism and neurotransmitter synthesis.
* **[Vitamin E](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-e/):** 11% per 100 grams — a relatively high figure for a low-fat vegetable.
* **Low energy density:** 31 calories per 100 grams, 92% water.
* **Low FODMAP** — well tolerated by people with irritable bowel syndrome, unlike many other vegetables.
* **Boosts iron absorption.** Bell pepper in a salad with [lentils](/en/food/lentil/), [tofu](/en/food/tofu/) or [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/) significantly improves the use of plant iron.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Loss of vitamin C in cooking** — as detailed above.
* **Pesticide residues.** Bell peppers regularly appear on lists of vegetables with relatively high pesticide residues, partly because the skin is eaten rather than peeled. Thorough washing is important; if you choose organic, this is a category where it is more relevant than in others.
* **Nightshades.** The bell pepper belongs to the nightshade family along with the [tomato](/en/food/tomato/), [eggplant](/en/food/eggplant/) and [potato](/en/food/baked-potato/). The claim that nightshades worsen arthritis is not well supported by controlled trials; there are personal reports, but it is not a blanket recommendation.
* **Digesting the skin.** Bell pepper skin is made of cellulose that is hard to break down, and some people experience discomfort. **Peeling after charring over a flame or roasting in the oven solves this completely.**
* **Cross-allergy.** People allergic to mugwort pollen or to latex sometimes report a reaction to bell peppers.
* **Price.** Red is significantly more expensive than green — because it spent longer on the plant.

### Practical use

* **Eat some of it raw.** Slices in a salad, in a sauce or as a dipper — this is the only way to get the full 127 mg of vitamin C.
* **Roast the rest.** Roasting or charring over a flame breaks down cell walls, concentrates sweetness and releases carotenoids. Combining both methods on the same day gives you the best of both worlds.
* **With fat.** [Olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/), [tahini](/en/food/tahini/) or [avocado](/en/food/avocado/) — a condition for absorbing the carotenoids.
* **In a salad with legumes** — to improve absorption of plant [iron](/en/food/iron/).
* **Wash well**, and consider organic in this category.
* **Peel after charring** if you have digestive sensitivity.
* **Choose one that is heavy and firm** for its size, with a fresh green stem and glossy skin with no soft spots or wrinkles.
* **Red or yellow** — for eating raw, both are excellent and yellow is even richer in vitamin C. Green suits those who prefer a less sweet taste, at the cost of eight times less vitamin A.
* **Freeze it cut up** — bell pepper freezes very well for use in stews, though the texture changes.

### Summary

The red bell pepper is one of the best sources of [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) in the entire vegetable aisle — **142% of the daily value for 31 calories, three times more than an orange** — and alongside it carotenoids, vitamin B6 and vitamin E. Two practical points: **green and red are the same fruit at different stages of ripeness**, and the nutritional difference between them is real (eight times the vitamin A); and **vitamin C is heat-sensitive** — to get it you need to eat some of the bell pepper raw, while the carotenoids are best eaten roasted and with fat.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much vitamin C is in a red bell pepper?**
Answer: 127.7 mg per 100 grams — 142% of the daily value, three times more than an orange (53 mg). A whole medium bell pepper provides almost twice the daily value for just 45 calories. Yellow bell pepper contains even more — 183.5 mg per 100 grams.

**Question: What is the difference between red and green bell peppers?**
Answer: They are the same fruit at different stages of ripeness — the green one was picked early. The longer the fruit stays on the plant, the more the chlorophyll breaks down, carotenoids build up and vitamins rise. The result: red contains 128 mg of vitamin C versus 80 in green, and eight times the vitamin A. It is also sweeter and more expensive, because it occupied space on the plant for longer.

**Question: Does cooking destroy the vitamins in bell peppers?**
Answer: Partly. Vitamin C is water-soluble and heat-sensitive — boiling leaves about 40-55% of it, roasting 50-65%, and brief steaming 80-90%. But the carotenoids are actually released better by cooking, because heat breaks down the cell wall. The solution is to eat it both raw and roasted, and always with a source of fat.

**Question: Is a sweet bell pepper spicy?**
Answer: No, and it is not a matter of degree. The sweet bell pepper is the only variety of the species Capsicum annuum bred to have almost no capsaicin — the compound that creates the heat in hot peppers. It is not a "toned-down hot pepper", but a variety that does not contain the substance at all.

**Question: Why are bell peppers hard for me to digest?**
Answer: Because of the skin, which is made of cellulose that is hard to break down. The solution is simple: char the bell pepper over a flame or roast it in the oven until the skin blackens, then peel it. This removes the problem completely and leaves the flesh, which digests easily. The bell pepper itself is considered low FODMAP and is well tolerated by most people with irritable bowel syndrome.

**Question: How do you choose a good bell pepper?**
Answer: Heavy and firm for its size, with glossy, smooth skin with no soft spots or wrinkles, and a fresh green stem. A pepper that is too light has already lost water, and wrinkles on the skin indicate long storage. In the fridge it keeps for one to two weeks.

**Question: Is it worth buying organic bell peppers?**
Answer: This is one of the categories where it makes relative sense. Bell peppers regularly appear on lists of vegetables with relatively high pesticide residues, partly because the skin is eaten rather than peeled. If you don't buy organic, wash well — and if you are sensitive, peel after charring as well.

**Question: Does bell pepper improve iron absorption?**
Answer: Yes, significantly. Vitamin C considerably increases the absorption of non-heme iron from plant sources. A salad with red bell pepper alongside lentils, tofu, chickpeas or tahini takes advantage of this mechanism — and it is one of the simplest and cheapest ways to improve the iron balance in a vegetarian diet.
