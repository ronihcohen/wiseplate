Every food is a package of benefits and downsides, and almost no food is absolutely "healthy" or "unhealthy." This section reviews hundreds of foods using a consistent method: nutrition facts per 100 grams, what the research really shows about the benefits, and the downside most articles skip.

### How to read nutrition facts without falling into the traps

* **Numbers on the label aren't numbers in the body.** [Spinach](/en/food/spinach/) contains an impressive amount of [iron](/en/food/iron/), but plant iron is absorbed at a rate of just a few percent. Bioavailability matters no less than quantity.
* **"Per 100 grams" is misleading when the serving is different.** [Cinnamon](/en/food/cinnamon/) and [chia seeds](/en/food/chia-seeds/) look impressive per 100 grams, but the real serving is a teaspoon or a tablespoon.
* **Processing changes everything.** The same potato can be [baked](/en/food/baked-potato/) or [fried French fries](/en/food/fries/) — a threefold difference in calories and a complete change in the fat profile.
* **Nutrient density vs. calorie density.** [Walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/) are very rich in beneficial compounds and also in 650 calories per 100 grams. Both things are true at the same time.

### "Healthy" foods have downsides too

This is the guiding principle of the site. [Seaweed](/en/food/seaweed/) provides [iodine](/en/food/supplements/iodine/) in amounts that can overload the thyroid. [Green tea](/en/food/green-tea/) blocks iron absorption. [Kefir](/en/food/kefir/) can cause digestive discomfort at first. [Tuna](/en/food/tuna/) accumulates mercury. Anyone who knows this side too makes better decisions than someone who only reads lists of "superfoods."

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What are nutrition facts?**
Answer: The amount of energy (calories) and macronutrients — protein, fat, carbohydrates and fiber — alongside vitamins and minerals, usually per 100 grams of the food. The figure that is almost always missing is bioavailability: how much of all this the body actually absorbs.

**Question: How do you know how many calories are in a food?**
Answer: Calories are derived from the macronutrient composition — 4 calories per gram of protein or carbohydrate, 9 per gram of fat and 7 per gram of alcohol. That's why fatty foods like nuts and oils are much more calorie-dense than water-rich foods like fruits and vegetables.

**Question: Is there any food that is absolutely healthy?**
Answer: No. Every food also carries a downside — an iodine load in seaweed, mercury in predatory fish, phytic acid in legumes and whole grains, or simply high calorie density in nuts. What decides is the context: the amount, the frequency, and what else is in your diet.

**Question: What's the difference between processed food and ultra-processed food?**
Answer: Basic processing — grinding, fermenting, pasteurizing, freezing — is usually harmless and sometimes improves the food. Ultra-processed food is made of isolated industrial ingredients that don't exist in a home kitchen, and is designed to be especially rewarding. The details are on the [ultra-processed food](/en/food/ultra-processed-foods/) page.

**Question: How much protein do you need a day?**
Answer: The basic recommendation is 0.8 grams per kg of body weight, but that's a minimum to prevent deficiency, not an optimum. For athletes, strength trainers and adults over 60, a range of 1.2-2.0 grams per kg is accepted. Especially rich sources: [chicken breast](/en/food/chicken-breast/), [tuna](/en/food/tuna/), [Greek yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/), [lentils](/en/food/lentil/) and [tofu](/en/food/tofu/).
