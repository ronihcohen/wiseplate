## Earl Grey

**Earl Grey is black tea flavored with oil from the peel of the bergamot orange, and a cup contains about 40–70 mg of caffeine, roughly half a cup of filter coffee.** Health-wise it behaves like ordinary black tea: an almost calorie-free drink with flavonoids that reduces iron absorption when drunk with food. The benefits often attributed to bergamot have almost no evidence at the amounts found in a cup of tea. In Hebrew it is called "ארל גריי".

The base is usually black tea from Sri Lanka (Ceylon), India or China. Bergamot (Citrus bergamia) is a small, green, sour citrus fruit grown mainly in Calabria in southern Italy, and the oil from its peel gives the tea its floral-citrus scent. Cheaper blends often use flavorings instead of natural bergamot oil.

### A short history

The tea is named after Charles Grey, the second Earl Grey, British prime minister in the 1830s. According to legend, a Chinese diplomat gave him the recipe as a token of thanks. Historians doubt the story, partly because bergamot does not grow in China, and bergamot-scented tea was probably sold in England before the name "Earl Grey" became attached to it.

### Nutrition facts

Values are for a cup of brewed Earl Grey (about 240 ml, one bag or one teaspoon of leaves), without milk or sugar, approximate:

| Nutrient | Per cup (240 ml) | Per 100 ml |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | about 2 | about 1 |
| Caffeine | about 40–70 mg | about 17–30 mg |
| Flavonoids (theaflavins, catechins) | tens to hundreds of mg, depending on steeping time | - |
| Potassium | about 90 mg | about 37 mg |
| Fluoride | small, variable amounts | - |

Milk adds about 10–20 calories a cup, and each teaspoon of sugar about 16. The longer the tea steeps, the more caffeine and tannins it contains.

### Benefits

**Blood pressure, modestly.** A meta-analysis of 13 trials found that drinking black tea over time lowered blood pressure slightly, by about 1 mmHg systolic [(Ma et al., 2021)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33237083/). The effect is small but consistent. On the other hand, right after drinking, caffeine raises blood pressure briefly [(Hooper et al., 2008)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18614722/).

**Gentle alertness.** The caffeine in a cup of Earl Grey improves alertness at a lower dose than coffee. Tea also contains [L-theanine](/en/food/l-theanine/), an amino acid that has been studied with caffeine for focus, although a cup of tea contains less than the doses used in studies.

**An almost calorie-free drink.** Without sugar, Earl Grey is a good replacement for sweetened drinks, and many people find the citrus aroma lets them use less sugar or none.

**Polyphenols.** For people who drink it, black tea is one of the main sources of [polyphenols](/en/food/polyphenols/) in the diet. Their long-term health effect is still unclear.

**What about bergamot?** Small studies have tested concentrated bergamot polyphenol supplements (juice or extract) for lowering cholesterol. The amount of peel oil that reaches a cup of tea is tiny and different from those supplements, so there is no basis for the claim that Earl Grey lowers cholesterol. Claims about relaxation come from aromatherapy, not from drinking it.

### Downsides

**Iron absorption.** Tea tannins bind plant iron and reduce its absorption when tea is drunk with a meal [(Disler et al., 1975)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1168162/). People who are short of [iron](/en/food/iron/), vegetarians and pregnant women should drink tea an hour before or after meals rather than with them.

**Extreme amounts: muscle cramps.** A man who drank about 4 liters of Earl Grey a day developed muscle cramps, which went away when he cut down to about a liter a day [(Finsterer, Lancet 2002)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11988248/). The author attributed it to bergapten, a component of bergamot oil that affects potassium channels at high doses. It is a single case report, but it is a reminder that even an "innocent" drink has a limit.

**Caffeine.** In sensitive people, several cups a day, especially in the afternoon, can disturb sleep. In pregnancy, tea counts toward the limit of 200 mg of [caffeine](/en/food/supplements/caffeine/) a day [(EFSA: safety of caffeine)](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102).

**Tooth staining.** Black tea stains teeth over time. Rinsing with water afterwards helps.

**Bergamot oil on the skin.** Concentrated bergamot oil is phototoxic: on the skin and in sunlight it can cause burns and dark patches. Drinking the tea carries no such risk.

### Practical use

- **Brewing:** boiling water, 3–4 minutes. Longer steeping adds bitterness and tannins.
- **Milk or lemon:** a matter of taste. [Lemon](/en/food/lemon/) goes well with bergamot; [milk](/en/food/milk/) softens bitterness. There is no clear evidence that milk cancels the benefits.
- **Timing:** have the last cup several hours before bed.
- **Around meals:** if you need iron, drink it between meals.
- **Variations:** Lady Grey adds lemon and [orange](/en/food/orange/) peel; a London Fog is Earl Grey with steamed milk and vanilla syrup, which makes it a sweet drink with calories.
- **Choosing a product:** look for "natural bergamot oil" in the ingredients if flavor matters to you.

### Summary

Earl Grey is black tea with bergamot: almost no calories, about half the caffeine of a cup of coffee, and the modest effects of black tea, such as a small drop in blood pressure over time. Bergamot provides flavor, not medicine. Avoid drinking it with iron-rich meals, watch the caffeine in pregnancy and in the evening, and keep the amounts reasonable.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much caffeine is in a cup of Earl Grey?**
Answer: About 40–70 mg, depending on how much tea you use and how long it steeps. That is about half a cup of filter coffee. Decaffeinated Earl Grey is also available.

**Question: Can I drink Earl Grey while pregnant?**
Answer: Yes, in moderation. The limit is caffeine: up to 200 mg a day from all sources combined, so one or two cups of Earl Grey suit most women, as long as coffee and chocolate are counted too. Drink it between meals so it does not reduce iron absorption.

**Question: Does Earl Grey lower cholesterol?**
Answer: There is no evidence for that. Studies on bergamot and cholesterol used concentrated supplements, and the amount in a cup of tea is tiny and different.

**Question: With milk or without?**
Answer: A matter of taste. The British way adds a little milk; others prefer a slice of lemon, which goes well with bergamot. The health difference is small; sugar is what matters.

**Question: How much Earl Grey is too much?**
Answer: For most people several cups a day are fine. Muscle cramps were reported in a man who drank about 4 liters a day, so amounts like that are not advisable. Caffeine-sensitive people will feel the effect long before that.

**Question: Does all Earl Grey contain real bergamot?**
Answer: No. Many products use flavorings that imitate bergamot. If it matters to you, look for "natural bergamot oil" in the ingredients.
