## French Fries

French fries ("צ'יפס" in Hebrew) start out as a [potato](/en/food/baked-potato/) — a root vegetable with 77 calories per 100 grams, with [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/), [potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/) and fiber. They end up as a dish with 312 calories per 100 grams, half of them from fat.

**The whole difference is in the preparation, and that is also where the good news lies:** the same potato, with almost the same taste, can leave the kitchen anywhere on the scale — depending on three decisions you can make at home.

This page also has a fact almost nobody knows, and it is very easy to apply: **storing potatoes in the fridge increases the amount of acrylamide formed during frying.**

### Nutrition facts per 100 g — by cooking method

| Preparation | Calories | Fat | Fiber | [Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Potato baked in its skin | 93 | 0.1 g | 2.2 g | 10 mg |
| Oven potato wedges, a teaspoon of oil | 130-150 | 4-5 g | 2.5 g | 200 mg |
| Air-fryer fries | 160-180 | 5-7 g | 3.0 g | 250 mg |
| **Deep-fried fries** | **312** | **15 g** | 3.8 g | 210-400 mg |
| Fast-food chain fries | 320-330 | 15-16 g | 3.5 g | 250-400 mg |
| Coated / "crispy" fries | 350-400 | 18-22 g | 3.0 g | 500-700 mg |

**The gap between the first row and the last is fourfold in calories and 200-fold in fat** — from the same raw ingredient.

### Acrylamide — what it is and what reduces it

**Acrylamide** forms in the Maillard reaction between **asparagine** (an amino acid found in potatoes) and **reducing sugars**, at temperatures above about 120°C (248°F) in a dry environment. The International Agency for Research on Cancer has classified it as **"probably carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2A)**, based on animal studies; the evidence from human studies is still inconsistent. The European Union set benchmark levels for industry in Regulation 2017/2158.

**Four actions that reduce it significantly — all doable at home:**

1. **Don't store potatoes in the fridge.** In the cold the potato breaks starch down into reducing sugars ("cold-induced sweetening"), and these are exactly the raw material for acrylamide. **A potato stored in the fridge will produce more acrylamide when fried.** The right storage: a cool, dark, ventilated place — not the fridge, and not next to [onions](/en/food/onion/).
2. **Soak in water for 20-30 minutes** after cutting. Soaking removes some of the sugars and asparagine from the surface. Dry thoroughly before frying.
3. **Fry to golden, not brown.** The darker the color, the more acrylamide. "Golden" is the guiding rule of food safety authorities.
4. **A lower temperature and a shorter time.** 170-175°C (340-350°F) instead of 190°C (375°F).

### Benefits

* **[Potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/).** Potatoes are one of the good sources of potassium, and most of it survives frying — about 580 mg per 100 g of fries.
* **Fiber.** 3.8 g per 100 g — more than you would think, especially in skin-on fries.
* **Some [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/).** Some is destroyed by heat, but not all; fries provide about 10 mg per 100 g.
* **[Vitamin B6](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b6/)** in a reasonable amount.
* **Resistant starch after cooling.** A potato that was cooked and cooled develops resistant starch — relevant mainly to potato salad, not to hot fries.
* **Cultural value and enjoyment.** There is no point denying it — an occasional serving of fries is not a problem by any measure.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Calorie density.** 312 calories per 100 g, and a restaurant serving is sometimes 200-250 g — 620-780 calories in a side dish.
* **Acrylamide** — as detailed above.
* **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/).** Especially in seasoned and coated versions — up to 700 mg per 100 g.
* **Reused oil.** Repeated frying and prolonged heating produce lipid oxidation products — aldehydes and polymers — that are linked to inflammation. In restaurants and food stands, oil quality varies widely.
* **Trans fat — a note for accuracy.** Modern frying oils contain almost no trans fat, and regulation has removed partially hydrogenated oils from the market. **The concern about trans fat in fries is mostly a thing of the past**; the relevant concerns today are calories, acrylamide and oxidation products.
* **Metabolic risk with frequent consumption.** Large cohort studies linked frequent consumption of fried potatoes to weight gain and an increased risk of type 2 diabetes — a link not found for boiled or baked potatoes. **Frying is the variable, not the potato.**
* **Low satiety relative to calories.** Despite the fiber, the combination of starch and fat is not as filling as the number of calories it carries.

### Practical use

* **Air fryer or oven.** 200-220°C (390-430°F), one or two teaspoons of [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) per 100 g. **A saving of 150-180 calories per serving**, with a very similar result.
* **Don't store them in the fridge** — as detailed above. This is the simplest action on the page.
* **Soak for 20-30 minutes and dry** before frying or baking. Both less acrylamide and crispier fries.
* **With the skin on.** Fiber, [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) and polyphenols, and also less asparagine relative to the flesh.
* **Golden, not brown.**
* **Salt after, not before.** Salting early draws out moisture and interferes with browning — and this way you get the same taste with less salt.
* **Share a serving.** A restaurant serving is in practice meant for two.
* **If you fry at home** — an oil with a high smoke point (canola, high-oleic sunflower), 170-175°C, and don't reuse the same oil more than once or twice.
* **Add protein and vegetables.** Fries alongside protein and salad are a side dish in a meal; fries on their own are 700 calories of starch and fat.

### Summary

French fries are **a potato with four times the calories** — 312 per 100 g, 15 g of it fat. They are not "toxic," and an occasional serving is not a problem. The two actions worth the most in practice: **switching to the oven or an air fryer**, which saves 150-180 calories per serving with almost no compromise on the result; and **not storing potatoes in the fridge** — an action that costs nothing and reduces acrylamide formation. And for accuracy: **the concern about "trans fat in fries" is mostly a thing of the past** — what is relevant today is calories, acrylamide and oil quality.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in French fries?**
Answer: 312 calories per 100 g when deep fried, 15 g of it fat. A restaurant serving is sometimes 200-250 g, that is, 620-780 calories in a single side dish. For comparison, oven potato wedges with a teaspoon of oil are 130-150 calories per 100 g, and a potato baked in its skin is 93.

**Question: What is acrylamide and how do you reduce it?**
Answer: A compound formed in the Maillard reaction between asparagine and reducing sugars at temperatures above 120 degrees Celsius, and classified as "probably carcinogenic to humans." Four home actions reduce it: don't store potatoes in the fridge, soak the cut pieces in water for 20-30 minutes before frying, fry to golden and not brown, and work at a lower temperature (170-175 degrees Celsius).

**Question: Why shouldn't you store potatoes in the fridge?**
Answer: In the cold the potato breaks starch down into reducing sugars, in a process called "cold-induced sweetening." These sugars are exactly the raw material for acrylamide formation during frying, so a potato stored in the fridge will produce more acrylamide. The right storage is a cool, dark, ventilated place, not next to onions.

**Question: Is there trans fat in French fries?**
Answer: Hardly any, today. Modern frying oils are not partially hydrogenated, and global regulation has removed the main sources of trans fat from the market. The concern about trans fat in fries is mostly a thing of the past. What is relevant today: calories, acrylamide, and the oxidation products formed in oil that is reused many times.

**Question: Are air-fryer fries really healthier?**
Answer: Yes, significantly. 160-180 calories per 100 g compared with 312 when deep fried — a saving of about 150 calories per serving, with a very similar result in taste and texture. It also reduces exposure to reheated oil. An oven at 200-220 degrees Celsius with a teaspoon of oil gives a similar result.

**Question: Are potatoes themselves harmful?**
Answer: No. Large cohort studies linked frequent consumption of **fried** potatoes to weight gain and an increased risk of type 2 diabetes — but the same link was not found for boiled or baked potatoes. Frying is the variable, not the vegetable. A potato baked in its skin provides potassium, vitamin C, vitamin B6 and fiber in 93 calories.

**Question: When should you salt fries?**
Answer: After frying or baking, not before. Salting early draws moisture from the surface and interferes with browning and crispness. In addition, salt sprinkled on hot fries sticks well to the surface and the palate detects it immediately — so you get the same taste sensation with less salt in practice.

**Question: How many times can you reuse frying oil?**
Answer: Once or twice at most, and only if the oil has been properly strained and cooled. Repeated heating creates oxidation products — aldehydes and polymers — that are linked to inflammation. Signs it needs replacing: a sharp smell, foam on the surface, a dark color or smoke at a lower temperature than usual.
