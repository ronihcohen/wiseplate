## Seaweed

Seaweed — which includes types such as nori, wakame, kelp, dulse and chlorella — is a marine superfood that has been eaten in Asia for thousands of years. In Hebrew it is called "אצות ים". In the Western world it has drawn growing attention in recent years because of a unique mineral profile that does not exist in most land foods.

Biologically, seaweeds are photosynthetic and accumulate minerals from the sea at very high concentrations. Hundreds of species are used as food, each offering a different profile of pigments, polysaccharides and minerals.

**In short:** the main advantage of seaweed is that it is the richest dietary source of iodine, alongside unique polysaccharides (fucoidan) and marine minerals. That same advantage is also the main downside — **too much iodine is just as dangerous as too little**, and a single piece of kelp can contain twenty times the daily allowance. The safe and useful amount is detailed in the section [How much seaweed can you eat a day](#how-much-per-day).

### Research-based health benefits

* **[Iodine](/en/food/supplements/iodine/) — a unique source:** seaweed is the richest natural dietary source of iodine. 1 gram of dried nori contains 16-43 mcg; a gram of dried kelp can contain 2,500 mcg or even more. Iodine is essential for normal thyroid function, for producing the hormones T3 and T4, and for brain development in fetuses and infants.

* **Fucoidan:** a sulfated polysaccharide unique to brown seaweeds (kelp, wakame). It has been studied for antiviral, antitumor and anticoagulant activity. Preliminary evidence points to support for the activity of the immune system's NK (Natural Killer) cells.

* **Carrageenan and alginates:** polysaccharides from red and red-brown seaweeds that can adsorb heavy metals and inhibit their absorption in the gut — a gentle detoxification activity.

* **Active pigments:** fucoxanthin (in brown seaweeds) — a carotenoid unique to seaweed with anti-inflammatory and preliminary anticancer activity in studies. Phycocyanin (in blue-green algae such as spirulina) — an antioxidant and anti-inflammatory.

* **Marine minerals in high concentration:** about 56 different minerals with high bioavailability — [magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/), [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/), [iron](/en/food/iron/), [potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/), [manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/) and [selenium](/en/food/supplements/selenium/) at levels higher than most land foods.

* **Vitamin B12 (controversial):** some seaweeds contain [vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/), but most of it is in the form of inactive "analogues" that compete with real B12 and may even inhibit its absorption. For vegans — do not rely on seaweed as a B12 source; take a supplement.

### Downsides and health considerations

* **Excess iodine — a real danger:** excessive intake, especially of kelp, can lead to iodine levels above the safe limit (over 1,100 mcg a day), which can trigger hyperthyroidism, goiter and even reactive hypothyroidism. Nori (sushi) is the relatively safe type for daily consumption.

* **Heavy metals — a real risk:** seaweeds absorb arsenic, lead, cadmium and mercury from the water. The concentration of metals depends heavily on where it is grown. Buy from brands with quality testing and from clean-sea sources (Japan, Ireland, Norway).

* **High [sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/):** processed, dried and seasoned seaweed products (such as nori snacks) may contain significant added salt.

* **Interaction with anticoagulants:** fucoidan has anticoagulant activity; high intake combined with warfarin requires medical advice.

### How much seaweed can you eat a day {#how-much-per-day}

The answer depends almost entirely on the **type** of seaweed, because iodine content varies between them by a factor of a hundred or more.

**The numerical framework:**
- The recommended daily intake of iodine for an adult: **150 mcg**.
- The safe upper limit: **1,100 mcg a day**.
- During pregnancy and breastfeeding the recommended intake rises to 220–290 mcg, but the upper limit stays similar.

**Practical recommendation by type:**

| Type | Safe daily amount | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Nori** | 1–3 sheets a day | The safest. A sheet provides 16–43 mcg of iodine |
| **Wakame** | A tablespoon dried (about 2 grams) a day | A daily miso soup is perfectly fine |
| **Dulse** | About 2–3 grams a day | Relatively low iodine content |
| **Kombu / kelp** | **Not for every day** — up to once or twice a week, in a small amount | **One gram can contain 2,500 mcg — more than 2 times the upper limit** |
| **Kelp supplements** | Avoid without a doctor's instruction | The most common cause of iodine excess |

**Rule of thumb:** nori and wakame in normal food amounts are safe for daily consumption and provide exactly the desired amount of iodine. Kelp and kombu are a seasoning and a base for stock, not a dish — and with them you need to measure.

**Who should be especially careful:** people diagnosed with thyroid disease (Hashimoto's, Graves', multinodular goiter), people taking thyroid medication, and pregnant women. In these groups even a moderate excess of iodine can disrupt thyroid function, and consultation is needed.

**Signs of iodine excess:** a metallic taste in the mouth, burning in the mouth and throat, increased salivation, and in the long term — changes in thyroid function that show up in a blood test.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What are the health benefits of seaweed?**
Answer: The standout benefit is that it is the richest dietary source of iodine, a mineral essential for thyroid function and brain development. It also provides fucoidan — a sulfated polysaccharide studied in an immune and anti-inflammatory context; fucoxanthin, a carotenoid unique to brown seaweeds; and a wide range of marine minerals with high bioavailability, including magnesium, calcium, iron and selenium. It is also very low in calories and contains soluble fiber.

**Question: How much seaweed can you eat a day?**
Answer: It depends on the type. Nori — 1 to 3 sheets a day, completely safe. Wakame — a tablespoon dried a day. But kelp and kombu are a different story: one gram can contain 2,500 mcg of iodine, more than twice the safe upper limit of 1,100 mcg a day. They should be used as a seasoning or a base for stock, once or twice a week at most.

**Question: Is the nori in sushi enough as a source of iodine?**
Answer: One sheet of nori provides 16-43 mcg of iodine — about 10-29% of the daily intake. Eating 2-4 sheets a week makes an adequate contribution.

**Question: Is seaweed suitable for vegans as a source of B12?**
Answer: No — the analogues in it are inactive and may interfere with the absorption of real B12. Take a separate B12 supplement.

**Question: How many times a week should you eat seaweed?**
Answer: 2-4 times a week in moderate amounts (a sheet of nori, a tablespoon of dried wakame) — enough for nutritional benefit without worrying about excess iodine.

**Question: Are nori snacks (roasted seaweed) healthy?**
Answer: They are fine for snacking, but check the [sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) and oil levels. Versions made with [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) and low in salt are preferable.

**Question: Can you eat seaweed during pregnancy?**
Answer: Yes, in moderation — nori and wakame are fine. Be careful with kelp because of its high iodine concentration, which can affect the thyroid of both mother and fetus.

**Question: What is the difference between spirulina and regular seaweed?**
Answer: Spirulina is a microscopic blue-green alga, eaten as a powder with a high-protein profile. Seaweeds (nori, wakame) are macroalgae with less protein but with iodine and fucoidan. See the [spirulina](/en/food/spirulina/) entry for details.

### Summary

Adding seaweed to your diet in moderation — nori in sushi, wakame in [miso](/en/food/miso/) soup — adds unique minerals and health-promoting polysaccharides. It is important to choose reliable sources, to watch out for excess iodine with kelp, and not to rely on seaweed as a source of B12.
