## Pear (*Pyrus communis*)

The pear (in Hebrew "אגס") is seen as a "calm" fruit — sweet, soft, no surprises. Nutritionally it is indeed modest: 57 calories per 100 grams, few vitamins, lots of water. But two things about it do deserve attention, and both are practical.

The first is **the skin**: it holds a considerable share of the fruit's fiber and most of its polyphenols, and peeling removes both at once. The second is **sorbitol** — a sugar alcohol in which the pear is richer than almost any other fruit, which explains both why pear juice is traditionally used for constipation in children and why in some people pears cause gas and bloating.

A medium pear weighs about 180 grams — that is, about 100 calories and about 5.5 grams of fiber, about a fifth of the recommended daily intake in a single fruit.

### The skin — and why peeling costs you

The fiber in a pear falls into two kinds: **pectin** (a soluble fiber), found mainly in the flesh, and **insoluble fiber**, concentrated in the skin. The skin also holds most of the polyphenols — mainly hydroxycinnamic acids and flavonols — which give the pear its rapid browning when cut.

The practical meaning is simple: **a peeled pear is a different fruit**. It loses about a third to a half of its fiber and most of its polyphenols, leaving mainly water and sugar. If the goal is fiber and satiety — don't peel, just wash well.

### Sorbitol — the fiber that isn't fiber

Pears contain an unusual amount of **sorbitol** for a fruit (on the order of 2 grams per 100 grams, varying by variety and ripeness). Sorbitol is a sugar alcohol that is absorbed in the small intestine slowly and only partially. What isn't absorbed continues to the large intestine, draws water in and is fermented by bacteria. This produces two opposite outcomes of the same mechanism:

* **Benefit:** a gentle stool-softening effect. This is why pear or prune juice is traditionally considered a first remedy for mild constipation, including in toddlers.
* **Downside:** in people with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) or FODMAP sensitivity, the pear is one of the more problematic fruits — gas, bloating and abdominal pain. In pears, sorbitol is joined by excess fructose, and together these place it in the high-FODMAP category.

### Benefits

* **Good fiber density:** 3.1 grams per 100 grams — more than an [apple](/en/food/apple/) (2.4 grams) and more than a [banana](/en/food/banana/) (2.6 grams).
* **Pectin:** a soluble fiber that slows stomach emptying, moderates the rise in blood sugar after a meal and binds bile acids in the gut.
* **Low glycemic load:** despite its sweetness, the pear's glycemic index is low (around 38), thanks to its fiber and fructose.
* **High water content:** 84% water — a filling fruit relative to its calories, useful as a substitute for a processed snack.
* **[Copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/):** 9% of the daily value per 100 grams, a notable proportion for a fruit.
* **Polyphenols in the skin:** contribute to the fruit's antioxidant capacity, and are almost completely lost with peeling.

### Downsides and considerations

* **High FODMAP.** The combination of sorbitol and excess fructose makes the pear a fruit to avoid during the restriction phase of a low-FODMAP diet, and to reintroduce carefully.
* **Low in vitamins.** 5% vitamin C and 2% potassium are not impressive. The pear contributes fiber and water, not micronutrients; anyone looking for vitamin C will get it from [kiwi](/en/food/kiwi/), [red bell pepper](/en/food/red-pepper/) or [orange](/en/food/orange/).
* **Free sugar in juice.** Pear juice removes the fiber and leaves the sugar and sorbitol. The whole fruit and the juice are not the same thing.
* **Misleading ripening.** A pear that looks firm on the outside may be rotten at its center, because it ripens from the inside out (see below).
* **Cross-allergy.** People with birch pollen allergy may experience oral allergy syndrome (itching in the mouth) from fresh pears. Cooking or baking breaks down the protein responsible and usually solves the problem.

### Practical use

* **Don't peel.** Wash and eat with the skin — that's where the fiber and polyphenols are.
* **Check the neck, not the body.** A pear ripens from the inside out. Press gently on the narrow area near the stem: if it yields slightly — the fruit is ripe. A pear that is soft in the body is already past its peak and rotten at the center.
* **Speed up ripening** in a paper bag with a [banana](/en/food/banana/) or an [apple](/en/food/apple/), which release ethylene. To stop the process — move it to the refrigerator.
* **Combine with protein and fat:** pear with [goat cheese](/en/food/goat-cheese/), [walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/) or [Greek yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/) — adds satiety and slows sugar absorption even further.
* **A little lemon juice** on cut slices prevents browning, which comes from the oxidation of those same polyphenols.
* **Baking or roasting** softens the fruit and tempers some symptoms in people sensitive to FODMAPs, but does not remove the sorbitol.

### Summary

The pear is not a superfruit and doesn't pretend to be. It provides 3.1 grams of fiber for 57 calories, a low glycemic load and good satiety — and that's enough to justify its place on the menu. Two things are worth remembering: **the skin is not waste** but the place where half the fiber and almost all the polyphenols sit, and **its sorbitol** is a blessing for those suffering from constipation and a nuisance for those with an irritable bowel. The same component, two opposite outcomes.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What are the nutrition facts of a pear?**
Answer: 100 grams of fresh pear with skin provides 57 calories, 3.1 grams of dietary fiber, 9.8 grams of natural sugars, 0.4 grams of protein and 84% water. Its vitamins are modest — about 5% of the daily value of vitamin C and 9% copper. A medium pear (about 180 grams) provides about 100 calories and about 5.5 grams of fiber.

**Question: Should you peel a pear?**
Answer: No. The skin holds a considerable share of the insoluble fiber and most of the fruit's polyphenols. Peeling removes about a third to a half of the fiber and leaves mainly water and sugar. Washing well is enough.

**Question: Are pears suitable for people with diabetes?**
Answer: In reasonable portions, yes. The pear's glycemic index is low (around 38) and the glycemic load of a medium fruit is moderate, thanks to its fiber and pectin. It's better to eat it whole rather than as juice, and to combine it with protein or fat — yogurt, cheese or nuts — to moderate the rise in blood sugar even further.

**Question: Why do pears give me gas and bloating?**
Answer: Because of the sorbitol. Pears are richer in this sugar alcohol than almost any other fruit, and it is only partially absorbed in the small intestine. What isn't absorbed reaches the large intestine, draws water and is fermented by bacteria. Together with the excess fructose, this makes the pear a high-FODMAP fruit that is not suitable for people with an irritable bowel.

**Question: Do pears help with constipation?**
Answer: Yes, and it is exactly the same mechanism that causes gas in sensitive people. Sorbitol draws water into the large intestine and softens stools, and the fiber adds bulk. That's why pear juice is traditionally considered a first remedy for mild constipation, including in toddlers.

**Question: How do you know a pear is ripe?**
Answer: Press gently on the neck — the narrow area near the stem — not on the body of the fruit. A pear ripens from the inside out, so by the time the body feels soft the center is already rotten. If the neck yields slightly to pressure, the fruit is ready.

**Question: How much sugar is in a pear?**
Answer: About 9.8 grams per 100 grams, that is, about 18 grams in a medium pear. This is natural sugar packed together with fiber and water, so its effect on blood sugar is much more moderate than the same amount of sugar in a sweetened drink.
