## Pecans

{{< youtube id="2jYW0dcSR-I" title="Pecans: The Most Extreme Nut? (video)" loading="lazy" >}}

The pecan (*Carya illinoinensis*) is a nut from the walnut family, native to North America, and a relative of the walnut. In Hebrew it is called "אגוז פקאן". It stands out among nuts in two extremes: the **highest fat content** of the common nuts, and at the same time the **lowest carbohydrate content**.

100 grams of pecans provide about 691 calories, 72 grams of fat, 9.2 grams of protein, 13.9 grams of carbohydrate and 9.6 grams of fiber — that is, only about 4.3 grams of net carbs. This combination makes it the favorite nut in ketogenic and low-carb diets.

### The antioxidant profile

The pecan is considered one of the nuts richest in antioxidants. The main components are **proanthocyanidins** (condensed tannins, from the same family found in grapes and wine), **ellagic acid** and **gamma-tocopherol**.

**Gamma-tocopherol** deserves a separate mention. Most people know vitamin E as alpha-tocopherol — the form common in supplements. Pecans are rich in the gamma form instead, which is studied separately and has somewhat different activity, mainly in neutralizing nitrogen radicals. 100 grams provide about 24 mg of gamma-tocopherol.

**An important caveat:** pecans used to be marketed on the basis of a high ORAC score. The US Department of Agriculture removed its ORAC database in 2012 and stated that these values, measured in a test tube, have no proven biological meaning in the body. Pecans are indeed rich in polyphenols — but arguments based on ORAC are not valid.

### Health benefits

* **Fat profile:** about 60% of the fat in pecans is monounsaturated (mainly oleic acid, the same acid that dominates olive oil). Controlled trials in which some dietary fat was replaced with pecans showed a drop in LDL and total cholesterol, with no weight gain.

* **Especially low in carbs:** 4.3 grams of net carbs per 100 grams — the lowest of the common nuts (almonds about 9.9, [pistachios](/en/food/pistachios/) about 17). A clear advantage in a ketogenic diet or when managing blood sugar.

* **Fiber:** 9.6 grams per 100 grams, a high amount that contributes to fullness and gut health.

* **Manganese and copper:** 100 grams provide more than 200% of the daily intake of manganese and about 130% of copper — among the highest concentrations in food. Both are cofactors for antioxidant enzymes.

* **Zinc and magnesium:** in respectable amounts (4.5 mg and 121 mg respectively).

* **Fullness and weight management:** as with other nuts, long-term studies find no weight gain with controlled intake, despite the calorie density. Some of the fat is trapped in the cell-wall matrix and is not fully absorbed, and the effect on fullness is significant.

### Downsides and risks

* **The most calorific nut:** 691 calories per 100 grams. A handful (30 grams) is already about 200 calories. This is the main practical downside — not because the fat is harmful, but because it is very easy to eat a large amount without noticing.

* **Unusual sensitivity to oxidation and rancidity:** this downside is unique to pecans, and it matters more than for most nuts. The especially high fat content, together with a porous structure, makes them spoil quickly. Oxidized pecans take on a characteristic bitter, sharp taste. **Store them in the fridge or freezer** — at room temperature they last only about two months, in the fridge about nine months and in the freezer about two years.

* **Aflatoxins:** nuts stored in humid conditions can develop aflatoxin-producing mold, a known liver toxin and carcinogen. Avoid nuts that look or smell suspicious.

* **Tree nut allergy:** pecans are a recognized allergen, and there is often cross-reactivity with walnuts because they are closely related. This allergy usually does not go away with age.

* **Sweetened pecan products:** candied pecans, caramel-coated pecans and pecan pie are completely different products — high in sugar, and sometimes containing more sugar and syrup than nuts.

* **Phytic acid:** somewhat reduces the absorption of iron and zinc. Soaking reduces it partly.

### Practical use

**How much:** a handful a day — about 30 grams, about 19 pecan halves, about 200 calories. That is the standard amount in nut studies.

**Storage — the most important point:** in the fridge in an airtight container, or in the freezer for the long term. A nut that tastes bitter or sharp has oxidized and should be thrown away. Nuts in the shell keep better than sliced or chopped ones.

**Toasting:** improves flavor significantly but speeds up oxidation. If you toast, toast a small amount and eat it soon. Lightly toast in a dry pan for 3–4 minutes.

**Pairings:** in salads (especially with goat cheese and fruit), in oatmeal, on [yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/), or as a snack on its own. Thanks to its low carb content it is a key ingredient in ketogenic baking based on nut flour.

**Pecans vs. walnuts:** pecans are sweeter and milder, lower in carbs and richer in monounsaturated fat. Walnuts are significantly richer in plant omega-3 (ALA) and protein. For heart health, walnuts have a stronger research base.

### Summary

The pecan is the most extreme nut in its composition: the richest in fat, the lowest in carbs and the most calorific. It provides monounsaturated fat, polyphenols, gamma-tocopherol and exceptional amounts of manganese and copper. Its standout advantage is in low-carb diets. The downside that needs practical attention is its unusual sensitivity to oxidation — pecans are not a nut you keep in the cupboard.

## Frequently asked questions

### Question: How many calories are in pecans?
Answer: 691 calories per 100 grams — the highest of the common nuts. A 30-gram handful (about 19 halves) provides about 200 calories.

### Question: Are pecans suitable for a ketogenic diet?
Answer: Very. They contain only 4.3 grams of net carbs per 100 grams — the lowest of the nuts — alongside 72 grams of fat. They are the favorite nut in ketogenic diets.

### Question: How should you store pecans?
Answer: In the fridge or freezer, not the cupboard. The high fat content makes them especially sensitive to oxidation — about two months at room temperature, about nine months in the fridge, about two years in the freezer. A bitter or sharp taste means they have oxidized.

### Question: Pecans or walnuts?
Answer: Walnuts are significantly richer in plant omega-3 and protein, and have a stronger research base for heart health. Pecans are lower in carbs and sweeter in taste. Both are excellent and complement each other.

### Question: What is special about the antioxidants in pecans?
Answer: They are rich in proanthocyanidins, ellagic acid and gamma-tocopherol — a form of vitamin E that differs from the alpha-tocopherol common in supplements. Note that marketing claims based on the ORAC score are not valid; the USDA removed the measure in 2012.

### Question: How many calories are in one pecan?
Answer: A pecan half (as it is usually sold) weighs about 1.5 grams and has about 10 calories, so a whole nut (two halves) has about 20 calories. A 30-gram handful, about 20 halves, provides about 200 calories.

### Question: What are the downsides of pecans?
Answer: They are the most calorific of the common nuts (691 calories per 100 grams), they oxidize and go rancid faster than most nuts so they are best kept in the fridge, and they are an allergen with cross-reactivity to walnuts. Candied or caramel pecans are already a high-sugar product.
