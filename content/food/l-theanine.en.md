## L-Theanine

**L-theanine is an amino acid found almost only in the tea plant, studied at doses of 100–200 mg a day; the evidence shows a small improvement in subjective sleep quality and, combined with caffeine, a slight improvement in focus, but no strong effect.** It is considered safe, and a cup of green tea provides only a fraction of the doses studied. In Hebrew it is called "ל-תיאנין".

L-theanine is not used to build proteins in the body. It was discovered in Japan in 1949 and is responsible for part of the rounded, "umami" taste of good [green tea](/en/food/green-tea/). Tea that is shaded before harvest, such as gyokuro and matcha, contains more of it. It passes from the gut into the blood and from there into the brain.

### Dosage and amounts in food

| Source | L-theanine (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Cup of ordinary green tea (240 ml) | about 8–30 mg |
| Cup of black tea | about 5–25 mg |
| Matcha (2 g powder) | about 20–45 mg |
| Supplement, typical capsule | 100–200 mg |
| Dose in sleep and stress studies | 200–400 mg a day |
| Dose in studies with caffeine | 50–200 mg, usually about 2:1 to caffeine |

The amount in tea varies widely with variety, harvest and steeping time, so these values are a rough range.

### Benefits

**With caffeine for focus.** In a controlled trial, 50 mg of caffeine with 100 mg of L-theanine improved speed and accuracy on an attention-switching task more than caffeine alone, and reduced distraction on a memory task [(Owen et al., 2008)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18681988/). Other studies found similar results, but they were short and small, and some were funded by tea companies. The measured effect is small.

**Sleep.** A 2025 meta-analysis of 18 trials (about 900 participants) found that L-theanine produced small improvements in subjective time to fall asleep, daytime functioning and overall sleep quality scores [(Bulman et al., 2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40056718/). The authors noted that most studies used blends rather than pure L-theanine, so it is hard to attribute the effect to L-theanine alone.

**Stress.** In a double-blind trial in healthy adults, 200 mg a day for four weeks lowered questionnaire scores for stress, anxiety and depression and improved sleep quality compared with placebo [(Hidese et al., 2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31623400/). That trial had only 30 participants.

**Calm alertness.** In some studies L-theanine increases alpha waves in the brain, which are associated with relaxed wakefulness. The practical meaning of that change is unclear, and the findings are inconsistent.

### How it works

L-theanine is structurally similar to glutamate, an excitatory neurotransmitter, and in laboratory and animal studies it binds to glutamate receptors and affects levels of GABA, dopamine and serotonin in the brain. These are findings from animal and test-tube experiments, and it is unclear how far they apply to people at ordinary doses. In humans, the most consistent finding is a small change in brain waves and in feelings of calm, without drowsiness.

Caffeine and L-theanine act in roughly opposite directions: caffeine stimulates by blocking adenosine receptors, while L-theanine is mildly calming. That is the usual explanation for why many people experience tea as a "softer" stimulant than coffee, although the lower caffeine content of tea also plays a part.

### Who it may suit

- People who are sensitive to caffeine and want to drink coffee or tea without feeling jittery, and are willing to test whether the combination helps them.
- People who struggle to fall asleep because of racing thoughts and stress, and want a gentle option before sleeping pills, after checking basic sleep habits.
- People who already drink tea: switching to green tea or matcha is the simplest and cheapest way to get L-theanine.

Less suitable: children (there are few studies outside clinical trials), pregnant women, and anyone looking for a replacement for medical treatment.

### Downsides

**Relatively weak evidence.** A 2025 review concluded that despite its popularity, most health claims about L-theanine rest on small studies or animal research, and that "the science does not yet match the hype" [(Dashwood et al., 2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39854799/). It is not a treatment for anxiety, depression or ADHD.

**Side effects.** Few side effects were reported in studies: occasionally headache, dizziness or stomach discomfort. The same review notes a good safety profile in toxicology studies, but not enough data on long-term use at high doses.

**Blood pressure and medication.** L-theanine may lower blood pressure slightly. If you take blood pressure medication, sedatives or psychiatric drugs, check with a doctor or pharmacist before regular use.

**Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** There is not enough information on the safety of the supplement in pregnancy and breastfeeding, so it is better avoided. Moderate tea drinking is a different matter.

**Product quality.** Supplements are not regulated like drugs, and the actual amount may differ from the label. Choose a product with independent lab testing.

### Practical use

- **From tea:** one or two cups of green tea or matcha provide both caffeine and L-theanine, at lower amounts than in studies.
- **For focus:** studies used about 100–200 mg of L-theanine with 50–100 mg of [caffeine](/en/food/supplements/caffeine/).
- **For sleep:** 200 mg about an hour before bed is the most common dose in studies.
- **Onset:** blood levels of L-theanine peak within about an hour.
- **What not to expect:** it does not knock you out like a sleeping pill, and it does not replace treatment for anxiety.

### Summary

L-theanine is an amino acid from tea that is safe at the doses studied (100–400 mg a day). Small studies show slight improvements in sleep and stress, and combining it with caffeine may slightly improve focus. The effects are modest and the evidence is still limited, so it is best approached with modest expectations, starting with a cup of tea before buying a supplement.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is the recommended dose of L-theanine?**
Answer: There is no official dose. Studies usually used 100–200 mg, and up to 400 mg a day for sleep and stress. Most supplements contain 100–200 mg per capsule.

**Question: Does L-theanine make you sleepy?**
Answer: Not like a sleeping pill. It does not cause forced drowsiness, and in studies it slightly improved perceived sleep quality and time to fall asleep. You can take it during the day without losing alertness.

**Question: How much L-theanine is in a cup of green tea?**
Answer: About 8–30 mg, depending on the tea and how it is brewed. Matcha and shade-grown teas contain more. Getting 200 mg from tea would take large amounts, which would also bring a lot of caffeine.

**Question: Why combine L-theanine with caffeine?**
Answer: In short studies the combination improved focus and accuracy more than caffeine alone, and some people feel less jittery. The common ratio is about 2:1 in favor of L-theanine, for example 200 mg with 100 mg of caffeine.

**Question: Is L-theanine addictive?**
Answer: There is no evidence of dependence or withdrawal symptoms. However, there are few studies of daily use over years.

**Question: Does L-theanine help with anxiety?**
Answer: Small trials found slight reductions in stress and anxiety scores, but it is not a treatment for an anxiety disorder. Anyone with significant anxiety should see a professional.
