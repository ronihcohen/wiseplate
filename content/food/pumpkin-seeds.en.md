## Pumpkin Seeds

### Pumpkin seeds: downsides worth knowing {#downsides}

**The main downsides are calorie density, salt in the salted version and digestive discomfort when you eat a lot.** Pumpkin seeds also provide protein and minerals; the amount, the shell and the salting all change what to look for when choosing a product.

* **Calories and serving size:** 100 grams of shelled, roasted, unsalted seeds provide about 574 calories; a weighed 28-gram serving provides about 161 calories. It is worth measuring into a small bowl rather than relying on the size of a handful. The figures do not include shells that are not eaten.
* **Salt:** the sodium content of salted products varies between manufacturers. Compare labels and prefer an unsalted version if you want to cut down on sodium.
* **Bloating and heaviness:** a large amount of seeds and fiber, especially when eating the shell too or when you are not used to fiber, can cause discomfort. You can start with a smaller amount and choose shelled seeds. [An explanation from the American Heart Association](https://www.heart.org/en/news/2018/10/25/pumpkin-seeds-pack-a-healthy-punch).
* **Mineral absorption:** phytates in plant foods can reduce zinc absorption; the amount listed in the table is not the amount absorbed. This does not mean pumpkin seeds cause a deficiency, or that soaking at home guarantees better absorption. [NIH: zinc and its sources](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-HealthProfessional/).
* **Kidney stones:** adjusting your diet depends on the type of stone and your overall diet. If you have been told to cut down on oxalates or sodium, work out the type of seeds and the serving with a professional as well; don't infer a blanket recommendation to avoid a food from its name alone. [NIDDK guidance by stone type](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/kidney-stones/eating-diet-nutrition).

### How many pumpkin seeds should you eat?

**28 grams is an example serving, not a medical dose or an amount you must eat every day.** It provides about 8.4 grams of protein and about 154 mg of magnesium. Adjust the amount to your diet and personal tolerance. You can sprinkle a measured serving over a salad or Greek yogurt.

**The shelled green kind is known as pepitas**, from the Spanish *pepita de calabaza*. The plant's scientific name is *Cucurbita pepo*. In Hebrew they are called "גרעיני דלעת" or "זרעי דלעת" — both names refer to exactly the same thing.

Pumpkin seeds are among the plant foods richest in magnesium and zinc — and that is where nearly all of their virtue lies. They were a staple food in Mesoamerican cultures centuries before they reached Europe, and were also used there as a folk remedy, mainly for prostate health.

**An important distinction:** seeds "in the shell" (the white ones, common as a snack) and shelled seeds (the green ones, pepitas) are not the same product. The green ones are more nutrient-dense by weight, while the white ones add insoluble fiber from the shell — if you actually eat it.

### The benefits — what is actually backed by evidence

* **Magnesium in exceptional concentration.** One handful provides 37% of the daily intake. This mineral really is lacking in the Western diet — about half of the population does not reach the recommendation — and it is needed for more than 300 enzymatic reactions: ATP production, blood sugar regulation, muscle contraction and relaxation, and blood pressure. This is by far the strongest advantage of pumpkin seeds.

* **Zinc — one of the best plant sources.** 19% of the daily intake in a handful. Zinc is essential for immune function, wound healing, taste and smell, and testosterone synthesis. It is especially important in a plant-based diet, where zinc is one of the harder minerals to get.

* **Prostate health — what the research really says.** This is the best-known benefit, and it is worth being precise about it. Controlled studies found **an improvement in BPH symptoms** (benign prostatic hyperplasia) — urination frequency and flow quality — mainly with **concentrated pumpkin seed oil**, and not necessarily with eating the seeds. The proposed mechanism is phytosterols and zinc. **What has not been proven:** prevention of prostate cancer or an effect on the enlargement of the gland itself. It is supportive treatment for symptoms, not a treatment for the disease.

* **Protein and tryptophan:** 8.4 grams of protein in a handful, and pumpkin seeds are among the richest sources of tryptophan — a precursor of serotonin and melatonin. An effect on sleep is mechanistically plausible, but the direct studies are small. For tryptophan to reach the brain, a carbohydrate is needed alongside it, so a handful with a date or a slice of bread makes more sense than seeds on their own.

* **Phytosterols:** about 265 mg per 100 grams — among the highest of any seed. They compete with cholesterol for absorption in the gut.

* **Unsaturated fats and vitamin E**, mainly in the form of gamma-tocopherol.

### Practical use

* **Serving size:** 28 grams as an example of a measured serving — about 161 calories, 37% of the daily value for magnesium and 19% for zinc. This is a starting point for planning a meal, not a uniform recommendation for everyone.
* **Soaking is not a nutritional must:** you can use it when a recipe calls for it, but there is no guarantee it improves mineral absorption in pumpkin seeds.
* **Don't snack straight from the bag.** Measure into a small bowl — in practice the difference is threefold or more.
* **On salad, yogurt or granola** — for crunch, protein and magnesium.
* **Pumpkin seed oil** for dressings only — it is dark green with a strong nutty flavor, and it is **not suitable for heating**. This is also the form tested in the prostate studies.
* **Pair with a source of vitamin C** to improve absorption of non-heme iron.
* **Before bed** — with a small carbohydrate, if tryptophan is the goal.

### Summary

A handful of pumpkin seeds a day provides 37% of the magnesium and 19% of the zinc you need, along with 8.4 grams of protein — one of the best benefit-to-calorie ratios among seeds. The best-supported benefit is actually the simple one: magnesium, a mineral that about half of the population really lacks. The prostate benefit is real but limited to improving BPH symptoms, and mainly with the oil. The two downsides that really matter in practice are quantity — 574 calories per 100 grams, and snacking freely from the bag gets you there fast — and the salt in salted products. Phytates affect mineral availability, but there is no basis for promising that overnight soaking solves it.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What are the downsides of pumpkin seeds?**
Answer: The main downsides are calorie density (about 574 calories per 100 grams of shelled, roasted seeds), sodium in salted products and digestive discomfort in large amounts. Phytates may reduce mineral absorption. A measured serving and checking the label help fit them into your diet.

**Question: What are the benefits of pumpkin seeds?**
Answer: The most prominent is magnesium — 37% of the daily intake in one handful, a mineral that about half of the population does not get enough of. Alongside it come zinc (19%), 8.4 grams of protein, a high amount of tryptophan, phytosterols, and documented help with the symptoms of benign prostate enlargement — mainly in oil form.

**Question: What are pepitas?**
Answer: Pepitas are shelled pumpkin seeds — the green seeds without the white outer hull. The name comes from the Spanish *pepita de calabaza*. The meaningful distinction is not the name but between seeds **in the shell** (the white ones) and **shelled** seeds (the green ones, pepitas).

**Question: How many pumpkin seeds can you eat a day?**
Answer: 28 grams is an example serving, not a medical dose and not a safety limit. For shelled, roasted, unsalted seeds this is a serving of about 161 calories, 8.4 grams of protein and 154 mg of magnesium. The right amount depends on your diet, personal tolerance and any medical guidance you have.

**Question: Are pumpkin seeds good for the prostate?**
Answer: There is limited evidence. Controlled studies showed an improvement in the symptoms of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) — urination frequency and flow quality — mainly with concentrated pumpkin seed oil rather than eating the seeds. No effect on preventing prostate cancer or shrinking the gland has been proven.

**Question: Do pumpkin seeds help with sleep?**
Answer: Mechanistically it is plausible — they are among the richest sources of tryptophan, a precursor of serotonin and melatonin — but the direct studies are small. It matters that they come with a small carbohydrate (a date, a slice of bread), because without one tryptophan has a hard time reaching the brain.

**Question: Are pumpkin seeds better in the shell or shelled?**
Answer: Shelled seeds are convenient to eat and to weigh. Eating the shell adds fiber, but a large amount may cause discomfort for people who are not used to it. For a nutritional comparison, distinguish between the weight of the seeds you eat and a weight that includes shells you throw away.

**Question: Should you soak pumpkin seeds?**
Answer: There is no basis for promising that soaking at home improves mineral absorption in pumpkin seeds. In a study on almonds, hazelnuts, peanuts and walnuts, soaking did not improve the phytate-to-mineral ratio; that is not a study on pumpkin seeds, so it does not prove what the effect would be in them either. [The study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32199146/).

**Question: Does roasting reduce the nutritional value?**
Answer: Light roasting — up to 150°C (300°F), for about 15 minutes — preserves most nutrients and improves the flavor. Prolonged roasting at high heat oxidizes the polyunsaturated fats and damages vitamin E. Seeds that taste bitter are seeds whose fat has oxidized.
