## Eggs

The egg ("ביצה" in Hebrew) is a small food dense in nutrients: high-quality protein in both the white and the yolk, alongside choline, vitamins, minerals and carotenoids concentrated mostly in the yolk. It is not a "superfood," nor is it dangerous just because it is rich in cholesterol; the effect depends on quantity, dietary pattern and health status.

### Nutrition facts for one large egg (about 50 g)

| Nutrient | Approximate amount |
|---|---:|
| Calories | 72 |
| Protein | 6.3 g |
| Fat | 4.8 g |
| Saturated fat | 1.6 g |
| Carbohydrates | 0.4 g |
| Cholesterol | 186 mg |
| [Choline](/en/food/supplements/choline/) | 147 mg |
| [Vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/) | about 0.5 mcg |
| [Selenium](/en/food/supplements/selenium/) | about 15 mcg |
| [Vitamin D](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-d/) | about 1 mcg |

The values vary slightly by egg size and the hen's feed. Eggs enriched with omega 3 or vitamin D may contain more of the declared nutrient, so the label is the best source for a specific product.

### Benefits

* **Complete protein:** an egg provides all nine essential amino acids. Cooking improves protein digestion compared with a raw egg and neutralizes most of the activity of avidin, a protein that binds biotin.
* **[Choline](/en/food/supplements/choline/):** a large hard-boiled egg provides about 147 mg, about 27% of the daily value. Choline is needed for cell membranes, nerve signaling and transporting fat out of the liver.
* **Lutein and zeaxanthin:** carotenoids in the yolk that accumulate in the retina. Eggs are not the largest source by quantity, but the fat in them helps their absorption.
* **Satiety and convenience:** the combination of protein and fat makes eggs a filling, quick option. The value of the meal also depends on what surrounds them — vegetables and whole-grain bread are very different from sausage and fried dough.
* **Little saturated fat:** most of the fat in an egg is not saturated. However, butter, cheese and processed meat that sometimes accompany an egg meal can change the picture.

### Cholesterol: what really matters

A large egg contains almost all of its cholesterol in the yolk. For most people, saturated fat and the overall dietary pattern affect LDL more than dietary cholesterol does, but individual response varies. Observational studies are inconsistent and sometimes struggle to separate eggs from the foods eaten with them.

The American Heart Association guidance allows a healthy person to include up to one whole egg a day as part of a healthy diet. This is not a "quota" you must fill, nor a rule that suits everyone. People with high LDL, diabetes or heart disease should adjust the amount with a professional based on their overall diet and test results. You can use more egg whites when you want more protein without adding cholesterol.

### Safety, salmonella and allergy

A clean, intact shell does not guarantee that an egg is free of salmonella. Keep eggs refrigerated, avoid contact of the shell and the fork with ready-to-eat food, and wash hands and surfaces after touching raw egg.

Infants, pregnant women, older adults and people with a weakened immune system are advised to avoid raw or undercooked eggs. For dishes that are not heated well — homemade mayonnaise, tiramisu or mousse — use pasteurized eggs or egg products. There is no need to wash eggs before storage; washing can damage the protective layer and spread bacteria in the kitchen.

Egg allergy is common in childhood and many outgrow it, but a previous reaction requires medical advice, not a home "test." The allergy can be to the white, the yolk or both.

### Cooking and practical use

Frying, boiling, baking and hard-boiling do not change the amount of protein much. The calorie change comes mainly from oil: a teaspoon of oil adds about 40 calories. Prolonged cooking slightly reduces heat-sensitive vitamins, but improves safety and protein digestion.

A hard-boiled egg keeps in the fridge for up to about a week in its shell. Don't leave a cooked dish at room temperature for more than two hours, and on an especially hot day — no more than one hour. A strong smell after overcooking comes from sulfur compounds and does not by itself indicate spoilage.

### Summary

Eggs are an accessible source of complete protein, choline, B12 and selenium. For most healthy people, an egg a day can fit into a balanced diet; it is more important to look at saturated fat and the meal as a whole. Proper cooking and storage, or using pasteurized eggs in a raw dish, reduce the risk of salmonella.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much protein is in an egg?**
Answer: A large egg has about 6.3 g of protein. About 3.6 g is in the egg white and the rest in the yolk, so skipping the yolk also cuts some of the protein and nutrients.

**Question: Is it OK to eat an egg every day?**
Answer: For most healthy people, one whole egg a day can fit into a healthy dietary pattern. People with high LDL, diabetes or heart disease should adjust the amount individually and also look at their saturated fat intake.

**Question: Is a brown egg healthier than a white egg?**
Answer: No. Shell color is determined mainly by the hen's breed and says nothing about nutritional value, quality or safety.

**Question: Does a raw egg provide more protein?**
Answer: No. The amount is similar, but cooked protein is digested better. A raw egg also carries a risk of salmonella and avidin activity, which binds biotin.

**Question: When do you need a pasteurized egg?**
Answer: In recipes served raw or almost raw, such as homemade mayonnaise, mousse and tiramisu. It is especially important for groups at risk of severe salmonella illness.

**Question: Do you need to wash eggs?**
Answer: Not before storage. Keep them refrigerated and follow local labeling instructions. After cracking an egg, wash your hands, utensils and work surfaces.
