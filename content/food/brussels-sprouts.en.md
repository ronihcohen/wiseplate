## Brussels Sprouts (*Brassica oleracea* var. *gemmifera*)

Brussels sprouts (in Hebrew "כרוב ניצנים", literally "bud cabbage") are one of the most nutrient-dense vegetables there is: **100 grams provide 148% of the daily intake of [vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) and 94% of [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) — in 43 calories**, alongside 3.4 grams of protein and 3.8 grams of fiber. A ratio like that is rare even among cruciferous vegetables.

And it is also the vegetable that has changed its reputation more than any other in the past thirty years — **and this happened for a specific, documented scientific reason**, not because public taste changed.

### Why they're no longer bitter — the story

Anyone who grew up in the 1970s and 80s remembers bitter, sulfurous Brussels sprouts. Anyone eating them oven-roasted today struggles to understand what the fuss was about. **The difference is real, and it stems from two reasons that came together:**

**1. Breeding.** In the late 1990s a Dutch researcher identified the glucosinolates responsible for the bitterness — mainly **sinigrin and progoitrin**. Since old heirloom varieties with low concentrations existed, they could be crossed with modern high-yield varieties. **The result: commercial Brussels sprout varieties that contain fewer of the bitter compounds — without genetic engineering, through classical breeding.** These are the varieties sold almost everywhere today.

**2. The cooking method.** The old way of cooking was **prolonged boiling**, which breaks down the sulfur compounds and releases mercaptans — the source of the smell and taste associated with the vegetable. **Oven roasting does the opposite**: it caramelizes the sugars at the edges, creates Maillard compounds, and leaves the inside soft and sweet.

**Anyone who believes they don't like Brussels sprouts should try a roasted version** — in all likelihood they have never actually tasted this vegetable.

### Benefits

* **An exceptional amount of [vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/):** 148% of daily intake per 100 grams — blood clotting, and above all directing [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) to bone rather than to blood vessel walls.
* **[Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/):** 94% of daily intake — more than an [orange](/en/food/orange/) per 100 grams.
* **High protein for a vegetable:** 3.4 grams per 100 grams, three times as much as [zucchini](/en/food/zucchini/).
* **Fiber:** 3.8 grams per 100 grams, a mix of soluble and insoluble.
* **Glucosinolates and isothiocyanates.** As in [broccoli](/en/food/broccoli/), the enzyme **myrosinase** turns them into active compounds when the cell breaks — when cut or chewed. The enzyme is deactivated by heat above 60-70°C, so **cutting and waiting 30-40 minutes before cooking** preserves a large part of the reaction. The compounds are being studied in the context of detoxification and inflammation pathways; the evidence in humans is still early.
* **[Folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/) and [manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/):** 15% of daily intake each.
* **Kaempferol** — a flavonoid found in notable amounts in Brussels sprouts, studied as an antioxidant.

### Downsides and considerations

* **[Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) and anticoagulants.** 148% of daily intake per 100 grams is one of the highest amounts in food. **People on Coumadin don't need to avoid them — they need steady, consistent intake** to which the drug dose is calibrated.
* **Gas and bloating.** Raffinose and fructans, which are not digested in the small intestine and are fermented in the colon. Cooking well, cutting in half and increasing the amount gradually reduce the effect.
* **FODMAP.** A serving of up to about 38 grams (2-3 sprouts) is considered low-FODMAP; beyond that it may trigger symptoms in sensitive people.
* **Goitrogens and the thyroid.** As with other cruciferous vegetables — the effect is practical only with a combination of existing [iodine](/en/food/supplements/iodine/) deficiency and very large intake of the raw vegetable. Cooking reduces it.
* **Boiling ruins them.** Both the flavor and the [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/), which is water-soluble.
* **Old varieties or sprouts that are too large** are still more bitter. Small sprouts (2-4 cm) are much sweeter than large ones.
* **A note on accuracy — not alpha-lipoic acid.** Brussels sprouts contain **alpha-linolenic acid (ALA)**, a plant [omega-3](/en/food/supplements/omega-3/), in a small amount. It is sometimes confused with **alpha-lipoic acid** — an entirely different antioxidant, whose name starts similarly and whose abbreviation is identical. The amount of alpha-lipoic acid in Brussels sprouts is negligible, and claims about improved [insulin](/en/food/supplements/insulin/) sensitivity are attributed to it, not to the vegetable.

### Practical use

* **Roast, don't boil.** Oven at 200-220°C (392-428°F), halved lengthwise, [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/), salt, and the cut side down. 20-25 minutes until deeply browned at the edges. **This is the one recommendation that changes your whole relationship with this vegetable.**
* **Cut and wait 30-40 minutes** before cooking — lets myrosinase work while still cold.
* **Choose small, firm ones.** 2-4 cm, bright green, with no yellow leaves. Large = more bitter.
* **Remove only the damaged outer leaves**, and trim a little from the base — no more, or the sprout falls apart.
* **Combine with acid, fat or sweetness:** lemon, balsamic vinegar, roasted almonds, [honey](/en/food/honey/) or Parmesan — all of these balance any remaining bitterness in the caramelization.
* **Raw in a salad.** Very thinly sliced sprouts (on a grater) with [lemon](/en/food/lemon/), olive oil and Parmesan — keep the full [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/), and the texture is a pleasant surprise.
* **Frozen works** for stews, but not for roasting — it releases too much water and doesn't brown.
* **For FODMAP-sensitive people:** start with 2-3 sprouts and increase gradually.

### Summary

Brussels sprouts provide **148% of the daily intake of vitamin K, 94% of vitamin C, 3.4 grams of protein and 3.8 grams of fiber — in 43 calories**. That puts them in the front row of vegetables. And the most important practical thing about them is that their bad reputation is outdated: **commercial varieties were improved through breeding in the 1990s and are much less bitter**, and the method that ruined them — prolonged boiling — has been replaced by roasting, which caramelizes them. If you remember bitter Brussels sprouts, try again: it's a different vegetable.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What are the nutrition facts of Brussels sprouts?**
Answer: 100 grams raw provide 43 calories, 3.4 grams of protein, 3.8 grams of fiber, 177 mcg of vitamin K (148% of daily intake), 85 mg of vitamin C (94%), 61 mcg of folate and 389 mg of potassium. Their protein and fiber relative to calories are closer to a light legume than to an ordinary vegetable.

**Question: Why aren't Brussels sprouts bitter anymore?**
Answer: Two reasons came together. In the late 1990s the glucosinolates responsible for the bitterness were identified — mainly sinigrin and progoitrin — and commercial varieties were bred by crossing them with heirloom varieties low in these compounds, through classical breeding and not genetic engineering. At the same time the cooking method changed: prolonged boiling, which breaks down sulfur compounds, was replaced by roasting, which caramelizes the vegetable.

**Question: How do you make Brussels sprouts taste good?**
Answer: Roast, don't boil. Halve them lengthwise, toss in olive oil and salt, and arrange them on a tray cut side down — oven at 200-220 degrees for 20-25 minutes until deeply browned at the edges. Adding acid (lemon or balsamic), nuts or Parmesan balances any remaining bitterness.

**Question: Can you eat Brussels sprouts while taking Coumadin?**
Answer: Yes, and there is no need to avoid them. 148% of the daily intake of vitamin K per 100 grams is a significant amount, but the principle is steady, consistent intake to which the drug dose is calibrated — not avoidance. Sharp fluctuations in intake are the problem, and it is worth telling your doctor about any significant change in habits.

**Question: Why do Brussels sprouts cause gas?**
Answer: They contain raffinose and fructans, carbohydrates that are not broken down in the small intestine and are fermented by bacteria in the colon. This is a normal reaction. Cooking well, cutting in half and gradually increasing the amount reduce it significantly. A serving of 2-3 sprouts is considered low-FODMAP.

**Question: Do Brussels sprouts contain omega-3?**
Answer: Yes, but in a small amount — about 0.1 grams of alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) per 100 grams. It is important not to confuse it with **alpha-lipoic acid**, an entirely different antioxidant with the same abbreviation; its amount in Brussels sprouts is negligible. For real omega-3 sources, better to turn to oily fish, [walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/) or [flax seeds](/en/food/flax-seeds/).

**Question: How do you choose good Brussels sprouts?**
Answer: Small and firm, 2-4 cm in diameter, bright green, with no yellow or loose leaves. Large sprouts contain more of the bitter compounds. When preparing them, remove only the damaged outer leaves and trim a little from the base — cutting too deep makes the sprout fall apart.

**Question: Can you eat Brussels sprouts raw?**
Answer: Yes, and it's an excellent way to keep all the vitamin C, which is heat-sensitive. Slice them very thinly on a grater or in a food processor, and dress with lemon, olive oil and Parmesan. The texture is crunchy and the flavor much milder than most people expect. This is also the form in which the glucosinolates remain fully active.
