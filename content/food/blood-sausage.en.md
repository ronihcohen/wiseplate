## Blood Sausage (Black Pudding)

Blood sausage is one of the few foods with **an extreme gap between nutritional strength and downside**: on one hand, **the densest source of heme [iron](/en/food/iron/) that exists in food**; on the other, high saturated fat, high [sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/), and classification as **processed meat**.

It is common in many cuisines — Spanish **morcilla** with [rice](/en/food/rice/), French **boudin noir**, British **black pudding** with [oats](/en/food/oatmeal/) — and the composition varies greatly between them.

**A note on kashrut:** blood sausage is not kosher. **The prohibition on eating blood is one of the strictest in kashrut** and appears explicitly in the Torah, and the koshering process for meat (salting) is designed precisely to remove the blood. A relevant consideration for a substantial share of readers.

### The iron — why it is exceptional

Blood is the tissue where the body's iron is concentrated: **hemoglobin is a protein whose entire job is to carry iron.** It follows that **a product made from blood is, by definition, the densest dietary source of iron.**

And two points make it even more significant:

* **This is heme iron** — the form that is absorbed at a rate of 15-35%, compared with only 2-10% for plant iron. It is also **not affected by phytate, [tannins](/en/food/tannins/) or [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/)** to the same degree.
* **It comes with [vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/)** — the second component needed to make red blood cells.

**That is why in many food traditions blood sausage was recommended after childbirth, after blood loss and for people with anemia** — and there is real nutritional logic behind the custom.

### Benefits

* **Heme [iron](/en/food/iron/) at an unrivaled density** — as detailed above.
* **[Vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/):** 40-80% of the daily value per 100 grams.
* **Animal protein** with a complete amino acid profile.
* **[Zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/)** in a reasonable amount.
* **Using the whole animal.** Environmentally and ethically, using blood — which would otherwise be discarded — is a real reduction in waste, and a long-standing tradition in thrifty cuisines.
* **Cultural and culinary value.** A central ingredient in Spanish, French, British, Polish and Asian cuisines.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Saturated fat.** 10-14 grams per 100 grams — **50-70% of the recommended daily limit** in one serving. This is the most significant downside.
* **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/).** 650-1,200 mg per 100 grams, about a quarter to half of the daily limit.
* **Processed meat.** The World Health Organization classifies processed meat as **carcinogenic to humans (Group 1)** with respect to colorectal cancer. The practical recommendation is to **limit**, not to ban.
* **Hemochromatosis — a real contraindication.** People with **hereditary iron overload** — a disease more common than you might think in populations of Northern European descent — **must avoid concentrated sources of heme iron.** Excess iron accumulates in the liver, heart and pancreas and causes damage. **Anyone diagnosed with hemochromatosis or with persistently high ferritin should consult a professional.**
* **Microbial safety.** Blood is an excellent growth medium for bacteria, and blood products require **continuous refrigeration and full cooking**. Some products are sold cooked and need only reheating; others require full cooking. **Read the preparation instructions, don't assume.**
* **Cholesterol.** 100-120 mg per 100 grams. The weight given to dietary cholesterol has declined in recent years, but it is still a consideration for part of the population.
* **Kashrut** — as mentioned above.
* **Huge variation between products.** A product with 20% blood and 40% fat is completely different from a product with 40% blood and grains. **The nutrition label is the only reliable source.**

### Practical use

* **Treat it as a serving of processed meat** — count it within your weekly allowance, not as an extra.
* **Read the label.** Fat and sodium content vary between manufacturers by a factor of two.
* **Eat it with [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/)** — heme iron is absorbed well even without it, but the combination improves the use of the non-heme iron in the grain inside the sausage.
* **Avoid [coffee](/en/food/coffee/) and [tea](/en/food/green-tea/)** close to the meal — [tannins](/en/food/tannins/) inhibit iron absorption, although their effect on heme iron is milder.
* **Add vegetables and fiber.** A serving of blood sausage with a large salad or cooked vegetables balances the fat and adds volume.
* **Cook thoroughly and keep continuously refrigerated.**
* **If you need iron but not the saturated fat:** [liver](/en/food/red-meat/), [oysters](/en/food/oysters/), lean [beef](/en/food/red-meat/) or a dedicated supplement provide heme iron with a much better fat profile.

### Summary

Blood sausage is **the densest source of heme iron in food** — up to 110% of the daily value in 100 grams, in a form absorbed several times better than plant iron, along with [vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/) and protein. The nutritional logic behind the custom of serving it after childbirth or blood loss is entirely real. **But the same serving also provides 50-70% of the daily limit of saturated fat, up to half the sodium limit, and it is considered processed meat.** The practical conclusion: **an occasional dish, not a regular food** — and people with **hemochromatosis or iron overload must avoid it completely.** And for many readers, the question of kashrut is decisive anyway.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much iron is in blood sausage?**
Answer: 6-20 mg per 100 grams, that is, 33-110% of the daily value — the range is wide because the composition varies greatly between manufacturers and traditions. It is the densest dietary source of iron, and the explanation is simple: hemoglobin in blood is a protein whose entire job is to carry iron.

**Question: Why is the iron in blood sausage absorbed better?**
Answer: Because it is heme iron, which is absorbed at a rate of 15-35% compared with only 2-10% for plant iron. It is also less affected by inhibiting factors such as phytate, tannins and calcium. In addition, it comes together with vitamin B12, the second component needed to make red blood cells.

**Question: Who should avoid blood sausage?**
Answer: First and foremost, people with hemochromatosis — hereditary iron overload — or persistently high ferritin. For them, concentrated sources of heme iron are dangerous, because the excess iron accumulates in the liver, heart and pancreas and causes damage. People who need to limit saturated fat or sodium should also be careful, as should anyone who keeps kosher.

**Question: Is blood sausage kosher?**
Answer: No. The prohibition on eating blood is one of the strictest in kashrut and appears explicitly in the Torah, and the salting process in koshering meat is designed precisely to remove the blood. A product whose entire base is blood cannot be kosher in any version.

**Question: How much fat is in blood sausage?**
Answer: 20-35 grams per 100 grams, of which 10-14 grams are saturated fat — that is, 50-70% of the recommended daily limit in a single serving. This is the product's most significant downside, and it varies greatly between manufacturers: a product with 40% fat is completely different from one in which blood and grain dominate.

**Question: What is blood sausage made of?**
Answer: Blood (beef or pork, depending on the tradition), fat, spices, and usually also a grain that absorbs and stabilizes it — rice in Spanish morcilla, oats or barley in British black pudding. The ratio between the three components varies by orders of magnitude between traditions and manufacturers, which is the reason for the wide range in nutritional values.

**Question: Is blood sausage considered processed meat?**
Answer: Yes. It is preserved by salting and added ingredients, so it falls under the definition that the World Health Organization classifies as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) with respect to colorectal cancer. It is important to keep this in proportion: the classification describes the strength of the evidence and not the size of the risk, and the practical recommendation is to limit it and count it within your weekly allowance of processed meat.

**Question: Does blood sausage help with anemia?**
Answer: Nutritionally there is real logic to it — it provides heme iron at an unrivaled density together with vitamin B12, so the custom of serving it after childbirth or blood loss is no accident. However, anemia requires a medical evaluation of its cause, and anyone who needs iron but not the saturated fat would do better with liver, oysters, lean beef or a dedicated supplement.
