## Honey

**Honey is mostly sugar: a tablespoon (about 21 g) provides about 64 calories and about 17 g of sugar, and metabolically it counts as free sugar just like white sugar; its only proven benefit when eaten is relief of cough in children over one year old.** Under one year, honey must not be given because of the risk of botulism. In Hebrew it is called "דבש".

Honey forms when bees collect nectar from flowers, break down its sucrose into glucose and fructose with the enzyme invertase, and evaporate most of the water down to a moisture content of about 17%. The result is an extremely saturated sugar solution — microbiologically stable, which in practice never spoils.

Nutritionally, we have to start with a sober statement: **honey is sugar**. 100 grams of honey provides about 304 calories and more than 82 grams of carbohydrates, almost all of them simple sugars — about 38% fructose and about 31% glucose. Its vitamin and mineral content is practically negligible: to get a meaningful amount of any mineral from honey you would need an unreasonable amount of sugar.

That said, honey is not identical to white sugar. Two properties set it apart in a way supported by good evidence: **antibacterial activity** and **clinical effectiveness against coughs**. Both are detailed below.

### Research-based health benefits

* **Antibacterial activity — a dual mechanism:** honey has three characteristics that inhibit bacteria. First, its high osmotic concentration draws water out of bacterial cells and dries them out. Second, its pH is acidic (3.2-4.5). Third, the enzyme glucose oxidase that bees add produces hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) in a slow, controlled release on contact with body fluids. This combination is not theoretical — radiation-sterilized medical honey is approved for clinical use in dressing wounds and burns in many countries.

* **Manuka honey and methylglyoxal:** manuka honey, made from the nectar of the manuka shrub in New Zealand, contains methylglyoxal (MGO) — a compound with antibacterial activity that does not depend on hydrogen peroxide and is therefore more stable. The UMF or MGO ratings on the package indicate its concentration. Distinguish between the relatively well-established topical use (on wounds) and the broad claims of benefits when swallowed, for which the evidence is much weaker and does not justify the huge price gap.

* **Effectiveness against cough in children — the best-established benefit:** this is the area where the evidence is strongest. A Cochrane review of six trials (about 900 children) found that honey probably reduces cough frequency more than placebo or no treatment, and may be about as effective as dextromethorphan, a common cough medicine [(Oduwole et al., Cochrane 2018)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29633783/). In adults too, a review of 14 studies found that honey improved upper respiratory infection symptoms, mainly cough, more than usual care [(Abuelgasim et al., 2021)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32817011/). The proposed mechanism combines mechanical coating of the throat and stimulation of saliva secretion. **The absolute limitation: only from age one and up** (see below).

* **[Polyphenols](/en/food/polyphenols/) and antioxidants:** honey contains flavonoids and phenolic acids that come from the nectar. Their content varies greatly with the nectar source — dark honeys ([buckwheat](/en/food/buckwheat/), heather) contain several times more than light honeys (acacia, citrus blossom). The amounts are still modest compared with fruits and vegetables, and do not justify eating sugar to get them.

* **A glycemic index slightly lower than sugar:** honey's GI ranges from 35 to 87 depending on the source, averaging about 58 — compared with about 65 for white sugar. The reason is its relatively high proportion of fructose, which does not directly raise blood glucose. This is a real but modest difference, and it does not make honey suitable for unrestricted consumption. A meta-analysis of trials found that honey, especially unprocessed honey from certain sources, may slightly improve blood sugar control and blood lipids when it replaces sugar within a healthy diet, but the certainty of the evidence is low [(Ahmed et al., 2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36379223/).

### Downsides and health considerations

* **Infant botulism — an absolute warning:** honey may contain dormant spores of *Clostridium botulinum*. In a mature digestive system they pose no danger, but in the gut of a baby under one year old, whose microbiome has not yet developed, they can germinate, multiply and produce a toxin that causes infant botulism — a serious, life-threatening disease. **Do not give honey in any form to babies under one year old**, including cooked or baked honey, since the spores are heat-resistant. In Israel the disease is rare: a national review found 8 confirmed cases in 2007–2021, and in some of them the source was environmental rather than honey [(Goldberg et al., 2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36692296/). Its rarity does not change the advice.

* **Metabolically — still added sugar:** health organizations classify honey as a free sugar, just like white sugar and [corn syrup](/en/food/corn-syrup/). It counts toward the limit of less than 10% of daily calories, and preferably under 5% [(WHO: healthy diet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet). On a 2,000-calorie diet, 10% is about 50 g of sugar, roughly three tablespoons of honey. The claim that you can eat honey freely because it is "natural" is one of the harmful myths in the field.

* **Fructose load:** about half the sugar in honey is fructose, whose metabolism takes place almost entirely in the liver. At high, sustained intake it is linked to fat production in the liver (lipogenesis) and fatty liver. The amounts in reasonable honey use are too small for this to be a major concern, but the principle holds.

* **Cavities:** honey is sticky and clings to the teeth for a long time, so dentally it may be even more problematic than sugar in solution.

* **Adulteration and fraud:** honey is one of the most adulterated products in the world. Common methods include diluting it with corn or [rice](/en/food/rice/) syrup and feeding bees sugar. Buying from a known source or a local beekeeper reduces the risk.

* **Crystallization is not a defect:** honey that has crystallized is usually good-quality honey that has not been over-processed. Crystallization is a natural physical process of the glucose. Gentle warming in a water bath (up to about 40°C, 104°F) returns it to liquid; strong heating damages the enzymes and polyphenols.

### Practical use

The simple rule: honey is **a sweetener, not a dietary supplement**. The justified way to use it is as a substitute for sugar where you would sweeten anyway — in tea, in yogurt, in a sauce — and in an equivalent amount, not an additional one. Because it is sweeter than white sugar, you can sometimes use less of it.

For cough in children over one year old: one to one and a half teaspoons before bed, on its own or dissolved in a little warm water.

For adding to tea: it is worth waiting for the drink to cool a little (below about 40°C, 104°F) before adding the honey. High heat breaks down some of the enzymes and polyphenols — although the calories and sugar, of course, remain.

When choosing: dark, strongly flavored honeys (buckwheat, heather) are richer in antioxidants than light, delicate ones.

### Summary

Honey has two real, established benefits: antibacterial activity that justifies topical medical use, and proven effectiveness at reducing nighttime cough in children over one year old. Beyond that, metabolically it is sugar in every respect, and its mineral and vitamin content is negligible. Eat it as an enjoyable sweetener within your daily sugar limit — and never give it to a baby under one year old.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Is honey healthier than sugar?**
Answer: Slightly. Its glycemic index is a little lower (about 58 versus 65), and it contains a modest amount of polyphenols. But in terms of calories and metabolism it is sugar, and it is officially classified as a "free sugar" just like white sugar. The difference does not justify unrestricted consumption.

**Question: From what age can you give honey to a baby?**
Answer: Only from one year old, without exception. Below that age there is a risk of infant botulism from *C. botulinum* spores. The ban also applies to cooked or baked honey — the spores are heat-resistant.

**Question: Does honey really help with coughs?**
Answer: Yes, and this is its best-established benefit. A Cochrane review found that honey probably reduces cough in children more than placebo, and possibly about as well as a common cough medicine. It is intended for children over one year old.

**Question: Is manuka honey worth the price?**
Answer: For topical use on wounds — there is a basis, thanks to its methylglyoxal. For swallowing as a health supplement — the evidence is weak and does not justify a price several times that of ordinary honey.

**Question: My honey has crystallized — has it gone bad?**
Answer: No, quite the opposite. Crystallization is a natural process of the glucose and often a sign of honey that has not been over-processed. Gentle warming in a water bath (up to 40°C, 104°F) returns it to liquid.

**Question: Can people with diabetes eat honey?**
Answer: Treat it like any other sugar — count it as part of your carbohydrates and eat only limited amounts. The fact that it is natural does not significantly change its effect on blood sugar levels.
