## Frozen Pizza

Frozen pizza ("פיצה קפואה" in Hebrew) is a perfectly legitimate meal solution — twenty minutes in the oven, no kitchen work and no planning. The problem is not that it exists, but two things that are very easy to miss on its label:

1. **The [sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/).** A whole pizza, which an adult can easily eat alone, can supply the entire recommended daily intake of sodium — and sometimes exceed it.
2. **The "cheese."** In a considerable share of cheap products, what looks like mozzarella is **cheese substitute** — a mixture of vegetable fat, starch and milk protein that is not cheese at all.

### Nutrition facts — a typical frozen margherita pizza

| Nutrient | Per 100 g | Whole pizza (~350 g) |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 250-280 | 880-980 |
| Protein | 10-12 g | 35-42 g |
| Carbohydrates | 29-32 g | 100-112 g |
| Dietary fiber | 1.5-2.5 g | 5-9 g |
| Fat | 9-11 g | 32-38 g |
| of which saturated | 4-5.5 g | 14-19 g (70-95%) |
| **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/)** | **550-650 mg** | **1,900-2,300 mg** |

**The number worth stopping at:** the recommendation for a healthy adult is up to 2,300 mg of sodium a day, and for protecting heart health — up to 1,500 mg. **One frozen pizza reaches that limit or exceeds it**, before the rest of the day's meals are counted.

### The label exercise — whose "serving"?

Almost every frozen pizza package shows values **per 100 g and per "serving,"** and a "serving" is usually defined as **a third or a quarter of the pizza**. This presentation turns 950 calories into "320 calories per serving" — a number that looks perfectly reasonable.

**The simple rule:** look at the per-100 g column, check the weight of the whole pizza, and multiply. If you intend to eat all of it, that is the relevant number.

### "Cheese" or "cheese substitute"

This is the single difference that separates a reasonable frozen pizza from a bad one, and it appears quietly in the ingredient list:

* **Real cheese** — mozzarella, gouda, kashkaval. It provides protein, [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) and milk fat.
* **"Cheese substitute" / "vegetable fat-based product"** — palm or coconut fat, starch, water, casein or soy protein, salt and stabilizers. **Palm fat is about 50% saturated**, meaning it is no improvement over milk fat and usually equivalent or worse — and without the calcium and protein.

Identifying it is simple: in the ingredient list, "mozzarella cheese" versus "cheese substitute" or "cheese product." Some products contain a combination of both.

### Benefits

* **Real convenience.** A hot meal in 20 minutes, with no preparation and no dishes — an advantage not to be dismissed on a busy evening.
* **Protein.** A whole pizza provides 35-42 g of protein, mainly from the cheese (in versions with real cheese).
* **[Calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/)** — 300-400 mg in a pizza with real cheese.
* **Lycopene** from the [tomato](/en/food/tomato/) sauce: cooked sauce provides lycopene with better availability than fresh tomato, and the fat in the pizza helps its absorption.
* **Price.** Significantly cheaper than pizza delivery, for a similar amount.
* **A base that is easy to improve.** Unlike most convenience products, frozen pizza **takes toppings** — and that changes it substantially.

### Downsides and considerations

* **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/)** — as detailed above, the main downside.
* **Cheese substitutes made with palm fat** — as detailed above.
* **White-flour dough.** Only 1.5-2.5 g of fiber per 100 g. A high glycemic index, low satiety relative to calories.
* **Sugar in the sauce.** Commercial tomato sauce usually contains added sugar, sometimes 4-6 g per 100 g of sauce.
* **[Ultra-processed food](/en/food/ultra-processed-foods/).** Most frozen pizzas meet the definition: stabilizers, dough improvers, flavorings, modified vegetable fat.
* **A calorie load in one meal.** 900-1,000 calories in a pizza — about half an adult's daily intake.
* **Processed toppings.** "Pepperoni," "sausages" and processed meat toppings add sodium, saturated fat, nitrites and [processed meat](/en/food/salami/) to the picture.
* **Low in vegetables.** In practice, a frozen margherita pizza contains almost no vegetables at all.

### Practical use

* **Read the ingredient list before you buy.** Look for "mozzarella cheese" and avoid "cheese substitute." This is the single most significant change in the category.
* **Look at the per-100 g figure and multiply by the weight of the pizza** — not at a "serving" of a third.
* **Add vegetables before baking.** [Cherry tomatoes](/en/food/tomato/), [bell pepper](/en/food/red-pepper/), [onion](/en/food/onion/), [mushrooms](/en/food/mushrooms/), arugula after baking. It costs almost nothing in calories and adds fiber, volume and satiety.
* **Serve with a large salad.** Half a pizza + salad is no less filling than a whole pizza, with about 500 fewer calories.
* **Add protein:** an [egg](/en/food/eggs/) cracked onto the pizza in the last five minutes, [tuna](/en/food/tuna/), or chopped [chicken](/en/food/chicken-breast/).
* **Look for whole-wheat dough** if available — it roughly doubles the fiber content.
* **A really hot oven (230°C+ / 450°F+) and on a rack rather than a tray** — crisp dough rather than soft. It makes no nutritional difference, but it decides whether this is a meal.
* **Balance the rest of the day's sodium** if you ate a whole pizza.

### Summary

Frozen pizza is a reasonable convenience meal, and there is no need to apologize for it. What does matter is knowing exactly what goes in: **a whole pizza is about 950 calories and about 2,000 mg of sodium** — that is, the daily sodium allowance in one meal. Two actions change the picture more than anything else: **choosing a product with real cheese rather than a palm fat-based "cheese substitute,"** and **adding vegetables and serving it with a salad** — that way half a pizza becomes a full, filling meal at half the load.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in a frozen pizza?**
Answer: A typical frozen margherita pizza provides 250-280 calories per 100 g, and a whole pizza of about 350 g comes to 880-980 calories. Note that the label usually shows a "serving" of a third or a quarter of the pizza, which produces a small, misleading number.

**Question: How much salt is in a frozen pizza?**
Answer: 550-650 mg of sodium per 100 g, that is, 1,900-2,300 mg in a whole pizza. The recommendation for a healthy adult is up to 2,300 mg a day, and for protecting heart health up to 1,500 mg — so one pizza uses up or exceeds the entire day's allowance. This is the product's main downside.

**Question: What is "cheese substitute" in frozen pizza?**
Answer: A mixture of vegetable fat (usually palm or coconut), starch, water, milk or soy protein, salt and stabilizers — which looks and melts like cheese but is not cheese. Palm fat is about 50% saturated, so it is no improvement over real cheese, and it also lacks the calcium and protein. The difference appears in the ingredient list.

**Question: How do you turn frozen pizza into a reasonable meal?**
Answer: Three steps. Add vegetables before baking — cherry tomatoes, bell pepper, onion, mushrooms — at almost no calorie cost. Add a protein source such as an egg cracked onto it at the end of baking, or tuna. And serve half a pizza with a large salad, which is no less filling than a whole pizza at about 500 fewer calories.

**Question: Is frozen pizza considered ultra-processed food?**
Answer: Most products in the category meet the definition — they include stabilizers, dough improvers, flavorings and modified vegetable fat, alongside ingredients that don't exist in a home kitchen. Products with a shorter ingredient list, simple dough and real cheese are closer to actual pizza.

**Question: Which is better — frozen pizza or pizza from a pizzeria?**
Answer: It depends on the pizzeria and the product. Fresh pizza made from simple dough and real cheese is nutritionally better, and usually contains fewer additives. On the other hand, a pizzeria slice may contain more calories and sodium than the same weight of frozen pizza. The meaningful comparison is in the ingredient list and the amount actually eaten.

**Question: How do you spot a better frozen pizza at the supermarket?**
Answer: Read the ingredient list and look for two things — "mozzarella cheese" rather than "cheese substitute," and dough containing flour, water, yeast, oil and salt without a long list of improvers. If there is a whole-wheat version, it roughly doubles the fiber content. The sodium value per 100 g is also worth comparing between products.

**Question: Is there anything nutritionally positive in pizza?**
Answer: Yes. A pizza with real cheese provides 35-42 g of protein and 300-400 mg of calcium, and the cooked tomato sauce provides lycopene with good availability — the fat in the pizza even helps its absorption. These are real advantages, which get swallowed up by the excess sodium and the refined dough if you don't improve the dish.
