## Beef Jerky

Dried meat is one of the most efficient foods there is in terms of **protein per weight and volume**: 33 grams of protein per 100 grams, with no refrigeration, no preparation, and a shelf life of months. For hikes, treks and military reserve duty, that is a real advantage.

**And two problems come with it, only one of which is well known:**

1. **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/).** 100 grams of commercial jerky provide **about 2,000 mg of sodium** — that is, **the entire daily allowance, and even more**.
2. **Sugar.** Teriyaki-style or "honey barbecue" marinades add **10-25 grams of sugar per 100 grams** — a detail that disappears behind the "high protein" headline.

### Nutrition facts per 100 grams

| Nutrient | Typical commercial jerky | Lean jerky / biltong |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 410 | 250-300 |
| **Protein** | **33 grams (66%)** | **43-50 grams** |
| Fat | 25 grams | 4-9 grams |
| Of which saturated | 10-11 grams | 2-4 grams |
| Carbohydrates | 11 grams | 1-3 grams |
| **Of which sugars** | **10-25 grams** | 0-2 grams |
| **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/)** | **1,800-2,100 mg** | 900-1,400 mg |
| [Iron](/en/food/iron/) | 5.4 mg (30%) | 5-7 mg |
| [Zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/) | 5.8 mg (53%) | 6-8 mg |
| [Vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/) | 1.1 mcg (46%) | 1.5-2 mcg |

**In a realistic serving:** a standard bag of jerky is 28-50 grams — that is, **9-16 grams of protein and 500-1,000 mg of sodium**. The sodium in a single serving still takes up **a third to a half** of the daily allowance.

### Why so much salt

The salt in jerky is not just seasoning — **it is the technology that allows the product to exist.** Drying and salting together lower the **water activity** to a level at which bacteria and molds cannot multiply. That is why the product is shelf-stable at room temperature without refrigeration and without additional preservatives.

It follows that **"low-sodium jerky" is a technological compromise**, not just a nutritional one — and it usually requires refrigeration or vacuum packaging.

### Jerky, biltong and processed meat

* **Jerky** — thin slices soaked in a marinade and dried **at low heat**, usually with nitrite and a sweetener.
* **Biltong** — the South African method: thicker pieces that are salted, **marinated in vinegar and air-dried at room temperature**, without heat and without sugar. The result is usually **lower in sodium and sugar and richer in protein**.

**Health classification:** most commercial jerky products fall under the definition of **processed meat** — meat preserved by salting, smoking or added preservatives. The World Health Organization classifies processed meat as **carcinogenic to humans (Group 1)** with respect to colorectal cancer. **It is important to keep this in proportion:** the classification describes the strength of the evidence for a link, not the size of the risk, and the practical recommendation is to **limit**, not to ban.

### Benefits

* **Exceptional protein density:** 33 grams per 100 grams, and over 40 in lean versions — with a complete amino acid profile.
* **[Zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/):** 53% of the daily value per 100 grams.
* **Heme [iron](/en/food/iron/):** 30% of the daily value, in a form that is absorbed much better than plant iron.
* **[Vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/):** 46% of the daily value.
* **Portability and shelf life.** No refrigeration, no preparation, compact and light — a real advantage on hikes, treks, field service and long trips.
* **Satiety.** Protein is the most satiating macronutrient, and the prolonged chewing jerky requires extends the feeling of fullness even further.
* **Low in carbs** in unsweetened versions — suitable for low-carb diets.

### Downsides and considerations

* **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/)** — as detailed above, the main downside.
* **Hidden sugar.** Sweet marinades add 10-25 grams per 100 grams. **Check the "of which sugars" line, not the headline on the package.**
* **Processed meat** — as detailed above.
* **Nitrite (E250) and nitrate.** Used for preservation, color and preventing botulism. When heated and in the presence of protein they can form **nitrosamines**. Products marketed as "nitrate-free" usually use **fermented [celery](/en/food/celery/) powder** — which is **exactly the same nitrite** from a different source.
* **Price.** One of the most expensive sources of protein — several times more than [eggs](/en/food/eggs/), [chicken](/en/food/chicken-breast/) or legumes.
* **Hard to chew.** Not suitable for people with dental or jaw problems, and sometimes problematic for people with TMJ disorders.
* **Homemade jerky — a real safety risk.** Home drying temperatures are usually too low to kill pathogens. **The standard guideline is to heat the meat to 71°C (160°F) before drying** — not just to dry it.
* **Not an everyday food.** Its sodium density and its classification as processed meat make it a product for specific contexts — a hike, a trip, the field — not a regular snack.

### Practical use

* **Read two numbers per 100 grams: [sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) and sugar.** The gaps within the category are very large — some products have 900 mg and others 2,100.
* **Prefer biltong** when available — usually less sodium, less sugar and more protein.
* **Look for a short ingredient list:** meat, salt, spices. Syrup, dextrose or "smoke flavor" are signs of a more processed product.
* **Drink water with it.** A concentrated sodium load requires fluids, especially on a hike or in the heat.
* **Count it as a serving, not a bag.** 28-30 grams is a reasonable serving; a 100-gram bag eaten in one sitting is the daily sodium allowance.
* **Use it in the right context** — a hike, a trek, a long trip — and not as a daily office snack.
* **Alternatively, for everyday protein:** a hard-boiled [egg](/en/food/eggs/), [Greek yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/), canned [tuna](/en/food/tuna/) or [chicken breast](/en/food/chicken-breast/) — all much cheaper, and without the sodium load.
* **When making it at home:** heat the meat to 71°C before drying, and store it in the refrigerator.

### Summary

Dried meat does one thing very well: **33 grams of protein per 100 grams, with no refrigeration and no preparation** — along with [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/), heme [iron](/en/food/iron/) and [B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/). It is an excellent product for a hike, a trek or a trip. **The problem is that the salt is not seasoning but the technology itself**: it is what makes the product shelf-stable, so 100 grams provide about 2,000 mg of sodium — a whole day's allowance. On top of that come 10-25 grams of sugar in sweet marinades. **Two numbers on the package solve most of the problem: sodium and sugar per 100 grams** — and the gaps between products are huge.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much protein is in beef jerky?**
Answer: About 33 grams per 100 grams in typical commercial jerky, and as much as 43-50 grams in lean versions or biltong. This is one of the best protein-to-weight ratios of any shelf-stable food. In a realistic serving of 28-50 grams, that is 9-16 grams of protein.

**Question: How much salt is in beef jerky?**
Answer: 1,800-2,100 mg of sodium per 100 grams in commercial jerky — that is, the entire daily allowance, and even more. Even a 28-50 gram serving provides 500-1,000 mg, which is a third to a half of the allowance. The salt is not just seasoning: it lowers water activity to a level that prevents bacterial growth, so it is what makes the product shelf-stable.

**Question: Is beef jerky considered processed meat?**
Answer: Yes, most commercial products are — because they are preserved by salting, smoking or added preservatives. The World Health Organization classifies processed meat as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) with respect to colorectal cancer. It is important to keep this in proportion: the classification describes the strength of the evidence for a link and not the size of the risk, and the recommendation is to limit, not to ban.

**Question: What is the difference between jerky and biltong?**
Answer: Jerky is thin slices soaked in a marinade and dried at low heat, usually with nitrite and a sweetener. Biltong is the South African method — thick pieces that are salted, marinated in vinegar and air-dried at room temperature, without heat and without sugar. The result is usually lower in sodium and sugar and richer in protein.

**Question: How much sugar is in beef jerky?**
Answer: More than you might think. Teriyaki-style or "honey barbecue" marinades add 10-25 grams of sugar per 100 grams. This disappears behind the "high protein" headline on the package. The solution is to check the "of which sugars" line in the nutrition table, and to look for products whose ingredient list is just meat, salt and spices.

**Question: What is "nitrate-free jerky"?**
Answer: Usually a product that uses fermented celery powder as a source of nitrite, instead of added nitrite. Chemically it is exactly the same nitrite, and the claim is correct from a regulatory standpoint but nutritionally misleading. Nitrite plays a real role — it prevents botulism — so removing it completely is not necessarily desirable.

**Question: Can you make jerky safely at home?**
Answer: You can, but there is a safety rule many people miss. Home drying temperatures are usually too low to kill pathogens, so the standard guideline is to heat the meat to 71 degrees Celsius before drying — not just to dry it. The homemade product, which contains no preservatives, should be stored in the refrigerator.

**Question: Is beef jerky a good daily snack?**
Answer: Not really. The sodium load, its classification as processed meat and its high price make it a product for specific contexts — a hike, a trek, field service or a long trip — not a regular snack. For everyday protein, a hard-boiled egg, Greek yogurt, canned tuna or chicken breast are much cheaper and come without the sodium load.
