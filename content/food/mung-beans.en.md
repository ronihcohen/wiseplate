## Mung Beans (*Vigna radiata*)

Among legumes, mung beans (in Hebrew, "שעועית מש") are the most convenient compromise: **the protein and fiber of a legume, but without its demands.** They **need no soaking**, cook in 25-30 minutes, and usually cause less gas than white beans, chickpeas or [black beans](/en/food/black-beans/).

They are also the easiest legume to sprout at home, and sprouting changes them fundamentally: **a 105-calorie starchy food becomes a crisp 30-calorie vegetable with several times more [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/).**

Alongside that comes a safety caveat worth knowing, because it applies to raw sprouts in general.

### Nutrition facts per 100 grams

| Nutrient | Dry | Cooked | Raw sprouts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calories | 347 | 105 | 30 |
| **Protein** | **23.9 g (48%)** | **7.0 g (14%)** | 3.0 g |
| Carbohydrates | 62.6 g | 19.2 g | 5.9 g |
| **Fiber** | **16.3 g (58%)** | **7.6 g (27%)** | 1.8 g |
| Fat | 1.2 g | 0.4 g | 0.2 g |
| **[Folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/)** | 625 mcg | **159 mcg (40%)** | 61 mcg (15%) |
| [Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) | 4.8 mg | 1.0 mg | **13.2 mg (15%)** |
| [Magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) | 189 mg | 48 mg (11%) | 21 mg |
| [Potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/) | 1,246 mg | 266 mg (6%) | 149 mg |
| [Iron](/en/food/iron/) | 6.7 mg | 1.4 mg (8%) | 0.9 mg |
| [Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) | 9 mcg | 2.7 mcg | 33 mcg (28%) |

**The row that stands out:** 159 mcg of [folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/) per 100 grams cooked — **40% of the daily intake**. This is one of the best plant sources of a nutrient that is critical in pregnancy and during the childbearing years.

### Why they are easier to digest

Gas from legumes is caused mainly by **oligosaccharides** — raffinose, stachyose and verbascose — which the human body has no enzyme to break down, so they are fermented in the large intestine.

Mung beans are better tolerated than most legumes for three reasons:

* **Lower oligosaccharide content** than white beans or [black beans](/en/food/black-beans/).
* **A thin skin** — which splits quickly during cooking and allows a shorter cooking time.
* **Small size** — a high surface area per unit of volume, meaning fast, even cooking.

**That doesn't mean they are problem-free** — they are still a legume, and anyone not used to them should start with a small amount. But for anyone who gave up on legumes because of discomfort, they are the right starting point.

### Sprouting — what exactly it does

Sprouting mung beans is one of the simplest nutritional steps you can take in the kitchen: soak overnight, rinse twice a day, and after 2-4 days you have sprouts.

What happens in the process:
* **[Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) rises dramatically** — from about 1 mg to 13 mg per 100 grams. The plant produces it as it germinates.
* **[Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) rises** — from 2.7 to 33 mcg.
* **Phytate drops.** Phytase enzymes are activated and break down some of the phytic acid — meaning **[iron](/en/food/iron/), [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/) and [magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) become more available**.
* **Oligosaccharides drop** — less gas.
* **Calorie density plummets** — from 105 to 30 calories per 100 grams, because the sprout has absorbed water.

**The downside:** protein and fiber per unit of weight drop in the same proportion. **Sprouts are not a substitute for cooked legumes — they are a vegetable.**

### Sprout safety — the important caveat

Raw sprouts are **one of the foods most often involved in foodborne illness outbreaks** worldwide. The reason is structural: sprouting conditions — moisture, mild warmth and time — are exactly the ideal growing conditions for **Salmonella** and **E. coli**. If the seed carried bacteria, sprouting multiplies them.

**In practice:**
* Health authorities recommend that **pregnant women, young children, older adults and immunocompromised people** avoid raw sprouts and eat them **cooked only**.
* For everyone else — buy from a regulated supplier, keep refrigerated, wash well and eat fresh.
* **When sprouting at home** — use seeds intended for sprouting, rinse twice a day, make sure they drain completely (standing water is the problem), and work with clean utensils.
* **A quick stir-fry in a pan** neutralizes most of the risk while keeping the crunch — a good compromise.

### Benefits

* **Plant protein:** 7 grams per 100 grams cooked, 23.9 grams dry. An amino acid profile rich in lysine and relatively low in methionine — complementing grains.
* **Fiber:** 7.6 grams per 100 grams cooked — 27% of the daily intake in one serving.
* **[Folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/):** 40% of the daily intake — as detailed above.
* **Relatively easy to digest for a legume** — as detailed above.
* **No soaking and quick cooking:** 25-30 minutes. A big practical advantage.
* **[Potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/), [magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) and fiber** — a combination linked in studies to improved blood pressure.
* **A low glycemic index.** Around 25-35 — among the lowest of legumes.
* **Vitexin and isovitexin:** flavones being studied as antioxidants.
* **Price and shelf life** — very cheap, and keeps for years dry.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Raw sprout safety** — as detailed above, the most significant downside.
* **Gas** — less than other legumes, but not zero.
* **Phytate** — binds minerals. Soaking, sprouting and long cooking reduce it.
* **FODMAPs.** Cooked mung beans contain GOS; a serving of about 75 grams is considered low-FODMAP, and beyond that may trigger symptoms. **The sprouts are considered low-FODMAP** in normal amounts.
* **Incomplete protein.** Low in methionine and cysteine — combine with [rice](/en/food/rice/), [buckwheat](/en/food/buckwheat/) or another grain over the course of the day.
* **A legume, not a vegetable.** 105 calories and 19 grams of carbohydrates per 100 grams cooked — it counts as a carb-and-protein serving, not a free side.
* **Availability.** Less common in regular supermarkets than [lentils](/en/food/lentil/) or [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/), and usually found in health food stores and Asian groceries.

### Practical use

* **No soaking.** Rinse, and cook in a 1:3 ratio with water for 25-30 minutes until soft. Soak only if you want to cut it to 15 minutes.
* **Add salt at the end.** Salt at the start of cooking toughens the skin and lengthens the time.
* **With [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/)** — [lemon](/en/food/lemon/), [tomato](/en/food/tomato/) or [bell pepper](/en/food/red-pepper/) — to improve absorption of non-heme [iron](/en/food/iron/).
* **Combine with a grain:** mung beans with [rice](/en/food/rice/) is a traditional combination (kitchari) that completes the amino acid profile.
* **Sprout at home:** soak overnight, rinse twice a day, drain completely, 2-4 days. Use only seeds intended for sprouting.
* **Stir-fry the sprouts** for 60-90 seconds in a hot pan — neutralizes most of the microbial risk and keeps them crunchy.
* **Risk groups** — cooked sprouts only.
* **Start with a small amount** if you're not used to legumes: 3-4 tablespoons, and increase gradually.
* **Mung bean flour** is used to make glass noodles and Asian pancakes, and it is [gluten-free](/en/food/gluten/).

### Summary

Mung beans are the legume with the lowest barriers: **no soaking, 25-30 minutes of cooking, and less gas than any other legume** — with 7 grams of protein, 7.6 grams of fiber and 40% of the daily intake of folate per 100 grams cooked. For anyone who gave up on legumes because of discomfort, this is the right entry point. Sprouting changes them fundamentally — more [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/), less phytate, less gas — but **raw sprouts are a food with microbial risk**, and pregnant women, children, older adults and immunocompromised people should eat them cooked only.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is the nutritional value of mung beans?**
Answer: 100 grams cooked provide 105 calories, 7 grams of protein, 7.6 grams of fiber (27% of the daily intake), 159 mcg of folate (40%), 48 mg of magnesium and 266 mg of potassium. Dry, the values are three times higher — 347 calories, 23.9 grams of protein and 16.3 grams of fiber per 100 grams.

**Question: Do you need to soak mung beans before cooking?**
Answer: No. This is one of their big practical advantages — the skin is thin and the bean is small, so rinsing and cooking for 25-30 minutes in a ratio of one part mung beans to three parts water is enough. Soaking for two hours will shorten cooking to about 15 minutes and slightly reduce phytate, but it is not necessary.

**Question: Why do mung beans cause less gas than other legumes?**
Answer: Mainly because of their lower content of oligosaccharides — raffinose, stachyose and verbascose — which are the main cause of gas from legumes. In addition, the thin skin and small size allow fast, even cooking. They are still a legume, so anyone not used to them should start with 3-4 tablespoons and increase gradually.

**Question: Are mung bean sprouts safe to eat raw?**
Answer: Raw sprouts are among the foods most often involved in foodborne illness outbreaks, because sprouting conditions — moisture, mild warmth and time — are ideal for Salmonella and E. coli. Pregnant women, young children, older adults and immunocompromised people should eat them cooked only. For everyone else: buy from a regulated supplier, keep refrigerated and wash. A 60-90 second stir-fry in a pan neutralizes most of the risk and keeps the crunch.

**Question: What does sprouting do to mung beans?**
Answer: It raises vitamin C from about 1 mg to 13 mg per 100 grams and vitamin K from 2.7 to 33 mcg, breaks down some of the phytate and so improves the availability of iron, zinc and magnesium, and lowers oligosaccharides so there is less gas. At the same time, calorie density plummets from 105 to 30 calories per 100 grams — meaning sprouts are a vegetable, not a substitute for cooked legumes.

**Question: Are mung beans a complete protein?**
Answer: Not on their own. They are rich in lysine but relatively low in methionine and cysteine, like most legumes. Combining them with a grain — rice, buckwheat or quinoa — completes the amino acid profile, and this doesn't need to happen in the same meal but over the course of the day. Indian kitchari, mung beans with rice, is exactly this combination.

**Question: Are mung beans suitable for people with diabetes?**
Answer: Yes, especially so. Their glycemic index is particularly low — around 25-35, among the lowest of legumes — and the combination of 7 grams of protein and 7.6 grams of fiber per 100 grams moderates the glycemic response of the whole meal. They also provide magnesium, which is linked to insulin sensitivity.

**Question: How do you sprout mung beans at home?**
Answer: Soak overnight in water, drain, and rinse twice a day in a jar or sprouter. Complete drainage is the critical part — standing water is what invites bacteria. Within 2-4 days you get sprouts. It is important to use seeds sold for sprouting rather than seeds for planting, and to work with clean utensils.
