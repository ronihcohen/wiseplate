## Cabbage (*Brassica oleracea* var. *capitata*)

Cabbage (in Hebrew "כרוב") is the least glamorous vegetable in the supermarket, and also one of the best nutritional bargains in it. **100 grams of cabbage — less than the weight of a slice of bread — provide 63% of the daily intake of [vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) and 41% of [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/), in 25 calories and at a price of a few shekels per kilo.**

It belongs to the cruciferous family together with [cauliflower](/en/food/cauliflower/), [broccoli](/en/food/broccoli/), [Brussels sprouts](/en/food/brussels-sprouts/), [kale](/en/food/kale/) and kohlrabi — all of them, surprisingly, species or subspecies of the same wild plant domesticated thousands of years ago.

And this page also answers a question many people get wrong: **when sauerkraut really contains live bacteria, and when it's just a sour vegetable.**

**White or red?** Red cabbage provides almost twice as much [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/), plus anthocyanins — the purple pigments that are also antioxidants. White cabbage provides twice as much [vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) and more [folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/). Both are good; for variety, eat both.

### Sauerkraut — when it contains live bacteria

Sauerkraut and [kimchi](/en/food/kimchi/) are made by spontaneous lactic fermentation: lactic acid bacteria that are already present on the cabbage multiply in the salty environment, convert sugars into lactic acid and sour the vegetable. The process produces live bacteria, breaks down some of the gas-causing compounds and increases the availability of some minerals.

**And here's the detail people miss:** most sauerkraut sold in shelf-stable jars has been **pasteurized** after fermentation, so it won't keep fermenting in the package. Pasteurization kills the bacteria. **A jar from the dry shelf = a tasty sour vegetable, zero probiotics.**

How to recognize the real thing:
* **Sold refrigerated**, not on a room-temperature shelf.
* **Ingredient list:** cabbage, salt, and maybe spices. **No vinegar** — vinegar indicates quick pickling, not fermentation.
* It often says "unpasteurized" or "live cultures."
* The lid may be slightly bulging — a sign that fermentation is continuing.

Historically, sauerkraut was the reason sailors stopped getting scurvy on long voyages: lactic acid preserves the [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) that in most vegetables would be destroyed in storage.

### Benefits

* **A large amount of [vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/):** 76 mcg per 100 grams in white cabbage — 63% of daily intake, needed for blood clotting and for directing [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) to bone rather than to blood vessels.
* **[Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/):** 41% in white and 63% in red — more than an [orange](/en/food/orange/) per 100 grams, at half the calories.
* **Glucosinolates:** Sulfur compounds from the cruciferous family (in cabbage mainly sinigrin and glucobrassicin), studied in the context of detoxification enzymes and inflammation pathways. The evidence in humans is early.
* **Fiber:** 2.5 grams per 100 grams with an energy density of just 25 calories.
* **Fermentation:** In the unpasteurized version — live lactic acid bacteria and fermentation products, along with a reduction in some gas-causing compounds.
* **Price and availability:** One of the cheapest vegetables per kilo, keeps for weeks in the fridge, and hardly ever spoils. A real practical advantage.
* **Anthocyanins in red cabbage** — the same family of pigments that gives [blueberries](/en/food/blueberries/) and [pomegranate](/en/food/pomegranate/) their color.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Gas and bloating.** Cabbage contains raffinose and fructans that are not broken down in the small intestine and are fermented in the colon. This is the most common complaint about it. Cooking, fermenting or slicing thinly reduces it; increasing the amount gradually also helps.
* **FODMAP.** White cabbage is considered low-FODMAP in a serving of about 75 grams, and in larger amounts may trigger symptoms. Red cabbage is tolerated in a similar amount.
* **[Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) and anticoagulants.** 63% of daily intake per 100 grams is a significant amount. People on Coumadin **don't need to avoid** cabbage — they need steady, consistent intake, so the drug dose can be calibrated to it. Sharp fluctuations are the problem.
* **Goitrogens and the thyroid.** As with other cruciferous vegetables — the effect is practical only with a combination of existing [iodine](/en/food/supplements/iodine/) deficiency and very large intake of the raw vegetable. Cooking reduces it.
* **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) in sauerkraut.** Sauerkraut reaches 600-800 mg of sodium per 100 grams — almost a third of the daily recommendation. An excellent condiment, not a serving.
* **Loss of vitamin C when boiled.** Vitamin C is water-soluble and heat-sensitive; prolonged boiling draws much of it into the water.
* **Smell during long cooking.** Sulfur compounds break down and release mercaptans. Shorter cooking in an uncovered pot reduces this.

### Practical use

* **Eat some of it raw.** A thinly sliced cabbage salad with [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) and [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) keeps all the [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/), and also provides the glucosinolates in their active state.
* **Don't boil it for long.** Steam or stir-fry quickly for 4-5 minutes. If you do boil it — keep the water for soup.
* **Slice thinly.** The thinner the slicing, the softer the cabbage, the better it is digested and the less gas it causes.
* **Buy sauerkraut from the refrigerator**, with an ingredient list of cabbage and salt only — as detailed above.
* **Combine with fat:** [Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) is fat-soluble; a tablespoon of olive oil or [tahini](/en/food/tahini/) in the cabbage salad is not a garnish but a condition for absorption.
* **Red stays red with acid.** Anthocyanins change color according to pH — a little [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) or vinegar in the salad or the cooking will keep the purple hue instead of turning bluish-gray.
* **Store it whole.** A whole head of cabbage keeps in the fridge for 3-4 weeks. After cutting — about a week, in an airtight container.

### Summary

Cabbage provides 63% of the daily intake of vitamin K and 41% of vitamin C in 25 calories, at the lowest price per kilo in the produce section, and with a shelf life of weeks. It is one of the simplest justifications in the whole menu. Two things are worth remembering: **red and white are not the same** — red wins on vitamin C and anthocyanins, white on vitamin K; and **sauerkraut from the dry shelf is not a probiotic** — only the version sold refrigerated, with an ingredient list of cabbage and salt only and no vinegar, contains live bacteria.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What are the nutrition facts of cabbage?**
Answer: 100 grams of raw white cabbage provide 25 calories, 1.3 grams of protein, 2.5 grams of fiber, 76 mcg of vitamin K (63% of daily intake), 36.6 mg of vitamin C (41%) and 43 mcg of folate. Red cabbage provides 31 calories and almost twice the vitamin C — 57 mg (63%) — plus anthocyanins.

**Question: Does supermarket sauerkraut contain probiotics?**
Answer: Usually not. Sauerkraut sold in a shelf-stable jar has been pasteurized after fermentation, and the heat killed the bacteria. To get live bacteria you need a product sold refrigerated, whose ingredient list is cabbage and salt only — no vinegar, which indicates quick pickling rather than fermentation. Sometimes "unpasteurized" is stated on the label.

**Question: Which is better — white or red cabbage?**
Answer: It depends on what you're looking for. Red provides almost twice as much vitamin C (57 mg vs. 36.6) plus anthocyanins as antioxidants. White provides twice as much vitamin K (76 mcg vs. 38) and more folate. Both are cheap and nutritious, so the simple solution is to alternate between them.

**Question: Why does cabbage cause gas?**
Answer: It contains raffinose and fructans — carbohydrates that are not broken down in the small intestine and are fermented by bacteria in the colon. This is a normal physiological reaction. Thin slicing, cooking, fermenting and gradually increasing the amount significantly reduce the effect.

**Question: Can you eat cabbage while taking Coumadin?**
Answer: Yes, and there is no need to avoid it. Cabbage is rich in vitamin K, which is indeed Coumadin's antagonist, but the principle is **steady and consistent** intake — so the drug dose is calibrated to it. The problem is sharp fluctuations: a week without cabbage and then a big plateful. It is worth telling your doctor about any significant change in eating habits.

**Question: Is cabbage bad for the thyroid?**
Answer: Almost always not. Its glucosinolates may interfere with iodine uptake, but the effect is significant only with a combination of existing iodine deficiency and very large intake of the raw vegetable. Cooking reduces it significantly, and for anyone whose iodine intake is adequate it is not a practical consideration.

**Question: How long does cabbage keep in the fridge?**
Answer: A whole head keeps 3-4 weeks in the fridge — one of the longest shelf lives in the produce section. After cutting it keeps about a week in an airtight container, and it is best not to wash it before storing. The outer layers that wilt protect the ones beneath them.

**Question: Why did my red cabbage salad turn blue?**
Answer: The anthocyanins that give red cabbage its color react to acidity — in an alkaline or neutral environment they tend toward blue-gray, and in an acidic one toward red-purple. Adding a little lemon juice or vinegar to the salad or the dish keeps the hue. This poses no safety problem at all.
