## Malawach

Malawach (in Hebrew, "מלאווח") is a Yemenite layered flatbread, a close relative of jachnun and of Moroccan [msemen](/en/recipes/morocco/msemen/), and a distant cousin of the puff pastry in [bourekas](/en/food/bourekas/). The principle in all of them is the same: **the dough is spread with fat and folded again and again**, and when it heats up, the water in the layers turns to steam and separates them. The flakiness does not come from rising — it comes from the fat.

And that leads to the most important point on this page, which is entirely practical: **a frozen malawach sheet already contains 20-25 grams of fat before it touches the pan.** Frying it in extra oil does not make it flakier — it only adds 80-150 calories. **A dry pan works perfectly.**

### Nutrition facts

| | Per 100 grams | Whole sheet (~90-100 grams) | + frying in oil |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calories | 380-420 | 380-420 | 480-560 |
| Protein | 6 g | 5-6 g | 5-6 g |
| Carbohydrates | 36-40 g | 35-38 g | 35-38 g |
| Fiber | 1-1.5 g | ~1.3 g | ~1.3 g |
| **Fat** | **22-26 g** | **21-25 g** | **31-38 g** |
| of which saturated | 10-14 g | 10-13 g (50-65%) | 11-15 g |
| [Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) | 500-650 mg | 480-620 mg | 480-620 mg |

**Values vary significantly between brands** — it is worth comparing the fat per 100 grams on the package; the gaps in the category reach 30%.

### The fat — what exactly is in there

In the traditional Yemenite version, the fat between the layers was **samneh** — clarified butter. In commercial products today it is usually **margarine, palm fat or another vegetable fat**, for reasons of price, stability and pareve (dairy-free) kosher status.

That changes two things:

* **[Palm fat](/en/food/margarine/) is about 50% saturated**, meaning it is no improvement over butter — and is sometimes similar to it.
* **"Pareve" does not mean "low fat".** A pareve product based on palm oil can contain saturated fat at a rate similar to the butter version.

**It is worth reading the ingredient list.** Products made with butter or non-hydrogenated vegetable oil (canola, sunflower) exist, and they are a better choice.

### Benefits

* **Available, concentrated energy.** In the right context — breakfast before a long day, a family meal — this is an advantage, not a downside.
* **A base for a balanced meal.** Malawach itself is dough, but **the traditional way it is served is actually a complete meal**: a [hard-boiled egg](/en/food/eggs/), grated [tomato](/en/food/tomato/), and zhug (Yemenite hot sauce). The egg provides protein, the tomato fiber and lycopene, and the zhug adds [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) and [capsaicin](/en/food/red-pepper/) — with almost no calories.
* **Cultural value.** A central part of Yemenite Jewish cuisine and of Shabbat and holiday meals. A dessert or festive pastry has a place in the diet.
* **Convenience.** Frozen, available, and ready in five minutes.
* **Nutritional transparency.** Unlike a bakery item, the frozen product has a full label — you can compare brands before you buy.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Calorie density.** One sheet is about 400 calories **before** toppings — more than two slices of [bread](/en/food/sourdough/) with a spread.
* **Saturated fat.** 10-13 grams per sheet — about 50-65% of the recommended daily limit.
* **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/).** 480-620 mg per sheet, about a quarter of the daily limit, and that's before the zhug and salt added when serving.
* **Refined carbohydrates and almost zero fiber.** 1.3 grams per sheet — low satiety relative to the calories.
* **Unnecessary frying** — as detailed above, it adds 80-150 calories for nothing in return.
* **The sweet serving.** Malawach with [honey](/en/food/honey/), sugar or jam turns a high-fat pastry into a high-fat, high-sugar pastry — 500-600 calories with no protein or fiber at all.
* **The "evening malawach" serving.** As dinner after a sedentary day, it is a dish that is hard to justify. As a Shabbat morning with the family — less so.

### Practical use

* **A dry pan, not oil.** This is the single most worthwhile rule here: **it saves 80-150 calories without any loss in the result.** Malawach contains enough fat of its own to brown and turn flaky. A nonstick pan, medium heat, 3-4 minutes per side.
* **Or in the oven:** 200°C (390°F) on a rack, 12-15 minutes. Evenly flaky, with no need to stand by the pan.
* **Always with protein and a vegetable.** A hard-boiled egg, grated [tomato](/en/food/tomato/), zhug, [cheese](/en/food/cottage-cheese/) — they turn dough into a meal. This is the traditional form, and it is also the nutritionally correct one.
* **Half a sheet + a big salad** is just as filling as a whole sheet, at 200 fewer calories.
* **Don't cut it into triangles and serve it as a "snack"** — that is how you eat a sheet and a half without noticing.
* **[Tahini](/en/food/tahini/) instead of jam** — it adds protein, [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) and unsaturated fat instead of sugar.
* **Compare brands by fat per 100 grams.** The gaps reach 30%, on the same freezer shelf.
* **Look for butter or non-hydrogenated oil** in the ingredient list.

### Summary

Malawach is a festive pastry of about 400 calories per sheet, half of them from fat — that is what it is, and there is no point pretending otherwise. Two actions change it more than anything else: **heat it in a dry pan or in the oven** rather than frying it in oil, since it already contains 20-25 grams of fat of its own — a saving of up to 150 calories without giving anything up; and **serve it the traditional way** — with egg, grated tomato and zhug — which turns dough into a balanced meal. Malawach with jam in the evening is a completely different story from malawach with egg on a Shabbat morning, even if the dough is identical.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in malawach?**
Answer: A standard frozen malawach sheet (90-100 grams) provides 380-420 calories, including 21-25 grams of fat and 10-13 grams of saturated fat. Frying in oil adds another 80-150 calories. Values vary between brands by up to 30%, so it is worth comparing the fat per 100 grams on the package.

**Question: Can you make malawach without oil?**
Answer: Yes, and that is the recommendation. Malawach already contains 20-25 grams of fat between its layers, so a dry nonstick pan over medium heat, 3-4 minutes per side, gives a result just as flaky and browned as frying in oil — while saving 80-150 calories. Another option is the oven at 200 degrees Celsius on a rack, 12-15 minutes.

**Question: What fat is in commercial malawach?**
Answer: In the traditional Yemenite version it was samneh, clarified butter. In today's frozen products it is usually margarine, palm fat or another vegetable fat, for reasons of price, stability and pareve kosher status. Palm fat is about 50% saturated, so "pareve" does not mean low in saturated fat. There are products based on butter or non-hydrogenated oil, and it is worth looking for them in the ingredient list.

**Question: What should you serve with malawach?**
Answer: The traditional serving — a hard-boiled egg, grated tomato and zhug. It is not just custom: the egg adds protein that prolongs satiety, the tomato adds fiber and lycopene, and the zhug adds vitamin C with almost no calories. This combination turns a high-fat dough into a balanced meal.

**Question: Malawach with honey or jam — how much is that really?**
Answer: 500-600 calories, with almost no protein and no fiber. The dough already provides 400 calories, half of them from fat, and the sweet topping adds sugar on top of fat. If you want something sweet, tahini with a little honey is better — at least it adds protein, calcium and unsaturated fat.

**Question: What is the difference between malawach and jachnun?**
Answer: Both are Yemenite doughs layered with fat, but jachnun is baked slowly for a long time in a covered pot at low temperature, while malawach is fried or heated quickly in a pan. Nutritionally they are very close — both are high in fat and refined carbohydrates and low in fiber.

**Question: Is malawach suitable for breakfast?**
Answer: As a weekend occasion, with egg, tomato and salad — absolutely. As a daily habit — less so: 400 calories with only 1.3 grams of fiber provide relatively short-lived satiety, and the saturated fat adds up. Context matters a lot — malawach on a Shabbat morning before an active day is not the same as malawach in the evening after a day of sitting.

**Question: How do you choose a good malawach in the freezer?**
Answer: Compare two numbers between brands — fat and saturated fat per 100 grams. The gaps in the category reach 30%. In addition, it is worth reading the ingredient list and looking for butter or non-hydrogenated vegetable oil instead of palm fat or hydrogenated margarine.
