## Introduction: Kefir

**Kefir is a fermented [milk](/en/food/milk/) drink, sometimes also spelled kephir or kefier.** In Hebrew it is written two ways, "קפיר" and "כפיר" — both refer to exactly the same drink.

Kefir is an ancient fermented drink whose documented origins lie in the Caucasus Mountains, the dramatic range that separates Europe from Asia, between the Black Sea and the Caspian Sea. It is made by adding cultures known as "kefir grains" to a liquid base, usually milk (cow, goat or sheep) or sweetened water. Despite their name, kefir grains are not real grains like wheat or [oats](/en/food/oatmeal/). They are colonies of lactic acid bacteria and yeasts living in symbiosis (a SCOBY – Symbiotic Culture of Bacteria and Yeast). These structures, which look like tiny cauliflower florets, are made of a matrix of proteins and polysaccharides that holds dozens of species of bacteria and yeasts together.

The origin of the word "kefir" is itself disputed. Some trace it to the Turkish word *keyif*, meaning "feeling good", while others believe it comes from the Persian *kef* (foam) combined with *shir* (milk). In Old Turkic, *köpür* means foam – and indeed, a light fizz is one of the drink's hallmarks.

Kefir is considered a powerful functional food. It is especially rich in probiotics, well beyond standard yogurt (in both quantity and diversity of species), and it also provides high-quality protein, [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) and a range of potent vitamins and minerals whose bioavailability is boosted by fermentation.

### In short: kefir's pros and cons

**The main downsides of kefir:** bloating, gas and stomach pain in the first week or two while the gut adjusts; high histamine content, which bothers people with histamine sensitivity; a small amount of alcohol produced during fermentation (mostly in homemade kefir); unsuitability for people allergic to milk protein (unlike lactose intolerance, which kefir actually handles well); a real risk for people who are severely immunocompromised; and, often, added sugar in flavored commercial versions.

**The main benefits:** far greater probiotic diversity than yogurt (dozens of strains, including yeasts), complete protein and highly bioavailable calcium, vitamin K2 produced during fermentation, and low lactose content that lets most lactose-sensitive people drink it without symptoms.

Both sides are covered in full further down the page: [the benefits](#benefits-of-kefir) and [the downsides and side effects](#downsides-and-side-effects-of-kefir).

### A fascinating history: from the Caucasus to the supermarket shelf

Kefir is without doubt one of the oldest fermented drinks in the world. Surprising archaeological evidence uncovered remains of kefir [cheese](/en/food/cheese/) at the Xiaohe cemetery in China's Tarim Basin – on the necks of Bronze Age mummies around 3,600 years old. It is the oldest cheese ever found, and DNA analysis confirmed that it was fermented with kefir grains. Researchers found in the ancient samples the same bacterial species (*Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens*) still found in modern kefir grains – remarkable evidence of continuity across thousands of years.

In the Caucasus itself, the nomadic Ossetian tribes – descendants of the ancient Scythians – were the first to harness kefir grains to ferment milk. They kept the milk in goatskin bags hung in doorways, so that everyone passing by would knock the bag and keep the fermentation going. Kefir grains were considered a precious asset – a symbol of wealth – and tribe members were forbidden from giving them to outsiders. They called them the "Grains of the Prophet", believing that the Prophet Muhammad had given them to the people of the Caucasus and warned that they would lose their power if shared with outsiders. The secret was kept for centuries.

The most colorful episode in kefir's history came at the start of the 20th century. The All-Russian Physicians' Society wanted to make kefir available to the general public, after Russian doctors had begun prescribing it for digestive diseases and tuberculosis back in the 19th century. The Blandov brothers, owners of a Moscow dairy who also ran cheese factories in the Caucasus, were given the task of obtaining grains. When ordinary attempts failed, they sent a beautiful young employee named Irina Sakharova to the court of a local prince, Bek-Mirza Barchorov, in the hope that she would charm him into giving her some grains. The prince was captivated but refused to part with the grains. As Irina set off home, local tribesmen kidnapped her and brought her back to the prince, intending for her to marry him. After her employers organized a rescue, the affair reached Tsar Nicholas II, who ruled that the prince must compensate Irina with ten pounds of kefir grains. In September 1908, the first commercial bottles of kefir went on sale in Moscow. In 1973, at the age of 85, Irina received an official letter of appreciation from the Soviet Minister of Industry.

### "Biological immortality": the mystery of kefir grains

One of the most remarkable things about kefir grains is that, for all the scientific knowledge we have accumulated, we still have not managed to create them from scratch in the lab. Scientists have identified more than 50 species of microorganisms living inside the grains, but attempts to rebuild the structure by mixing isolated strains of bacteria and yeasts have failed. The microorganisms did grow in milk, but they did not form the stable grain structure. The key bacterium *L. kefiranofaciens* produces the polysaccharide kefiran, which acts as the "glue" of the microbial community, but we still lack a full understanding of how dozens of species together build this organized three-dimensional structure. The grains are effectively a complete ecosystem in miniature – a tiny symbiotic city that has built itself layer by layer over hundreds, perhaps thousands, of years.

Beyond that, kefir grains are biologically "immortal": unlike most living things, they have no programmed cell death mechanism (apoptosis). As long as they are fed fresh milk, they keep growing, dividing and multiplying without limit. In practical terms, the kefir grains on your kitchen counter today are not "descendants" of ancient grains – in a certain biological sense, they are the same community of living organisms that fermented milk in the Caucasus Mountains centuries ago. Under optimal conditions, kefir grains can increase their mass by more than 500% in just two weeks.

### Milk kefir vs. water kefir

There are two main types of kefir, which differ in the liquid they are fermented in. As a result, their probiotic profiles differ slightly, and so do their nutritional values:
- **Milk kefir:** fermented in milk. This is the classic type and the most widely used, both commercially and traditionally. It contains complete proteins, calcium from the milk, and B vitamins (such as B12 and riboflavin). It tastes tangy, like a drinkable natural yogurt, and often has a light natural fizz from the carbon dioxide released during fermentation. Fermentation also produces a small amount of alcohol – usually less than 1%, so the drink is not considered alcoholic, though prolonged fermentation can raise the level slightly. The milk, combined with bacterial activity, also produces essential vitamin K2.
- **Water kefir:** fermented in a sugar-water solution, [coconut](/en/food/coconut/) water or even natural fruit juices, using "water kefir grains" (which, unlike milk kefir grains, are made of a polysaccharide called dextran). It contains no dairy at all, making it a safe, vegan option for people with severe milk allergies, and it is completely lactose-free. It is usually the base for light, naturally fizzy, fruity drinks, and is sometimes called "probiotic champagne".

### Store-bought vs. homemade kefir – not the same thing

An important difference worth knowing is the gap between supermarket kefir and kefir made at home from live grains. Commercial kefir is usually made with freeze-dried starter cultures rather than real grains, for reasons of product consistency, storage and transport. A typical commercial kefir contains 7–9 defined strains of bacteria, whereas homemade kefir made with original grains can contain anywhere from 30 to more than 60 different strains of microorganisms. Homemade kefir tends to taste sourer and livelier, with stronger fizz, and its probiotic profile is significantly richer.

## A rich nutritional profile

One cup (about 240 ml) of kefir made from fermented cow's milk contains an impressive range of nutrients. Amounts vary slightly with the type of milk and fermentation time, but on average it provides:
- **Calories:** between 100 and 120.
- **Protein:** about 9 grams of complete protein (all essential amino acids).
- **Carbohydrates:** about 7–10 grams (the bacteria consume milk sugar during fermentation, so the longer kefir ferments, the less sugar it contains).
- **Fat:** depends on the milk – whole-milk kefir contains about 5–8 grams of fat, while low-fat kefir contains less than 2 grams. Fat contributes to the creamy texture and to the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins.
- **Calcium:** about 20% of the recommended daily intake.
- **Phosphorus:** about 20% of the daily value – an essential mineral for healthy bones and teeth that works together with calcium.
- **Vitamin B12:** a rich, readily absorbed source providing close to 30% of the daily value (RDI). This makes kefir a particularly important source for vegetarians (not vegans), who may be at risk of B12 deficiency.
- **Riboflavin (vitamin B2):** about 25% of the daily value – a vitamin needed for normal metabolism and enzyme function.
- **Magnesium:** supports nerve signaling and muscle function, and is also important for sleep quality and blood sugar regulation.
- **Tryptophan:** an essential amino acid that serves as a building block for serotonin and melatonin, contributing to relaxation and sleep quality.

## Benefits of kefir

### Uncompromising probiotic power (compared with yogurt)
Kefir is one of the most potent sources of probiotics in the traditional human diet. While an average factory-made yogurt usually contains only about 2 to 5 basic strains of bacteria (mostly Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium), homemade kefir from authentic grains is far more diverse, sometimes ranging from 30 to more than 60 different combinations of probiotic bacteria and yeasts.

The key difference is not just quantity: unlike yogurt, which contains only bacteria, kefir also contains beneficial **yeasts** – a component entirely absent from commercial yogurt. The presence of yeasts adds uniquely to the ecological diversity of the gut flora. This microbial richness helps restore and balance the gut microbiome, supports metabolism and helps reduce systemic inflammation.

### Antibacterial properties (Kefiran)
Beyond their role in digestion, several probiotic strains unique to kefir (such as *Lactobacillus kefiri*) have been shown in lab studies to actively help protect against outside infections. These strains can bind to and secrete substances that inhibit the growth of pathogens such as Salmonella, dangerous E. coli and Helicobacter pylori, the bacterium that damages the stomach.

Fermentation also produces a unique polysaccharide called kefiran – the same substance that forms the grain matrix itself. Isolation studies found that kefiran has strong activity against infectious fungi (such as Candida) and various harmful bacteria. Animal studies have also shown that kefiran has anti-inflammatory properties and can lower blood pressure and cholesterol levels.

### Stronger bones and osteoporosis prevention
Keeping bones intact and dense is a critical part of healthy aging, as osteoporosis is especially common in women after menopause. Milk kefir provides a very effective nutritional platform: it supplies raw calcium on one hand, while fermentation produces good levels of vitamin K2 on the other. This essential vitamin effectively "collects" calcium from the blood and directs it to be deposited in bone tissue, preventing dangerous buildup in the arteries. In addition, fermentation increases calcium bioavailability – meaning the body absorbs more calcium from kefir than from regular, unfermented milk.

### Effects on cholesterol and heart health
Several clinical studies have found that regular kefir consumption is linked to lower LDL ("bad") cholesterol and an improved LDL-to-HDL ratio. The proposed mechanism involves kefir bacteria breaking down bile salts, which forces the liver to produce new bile salts from cholesterol already in the blood. In addition, kefiran itself has shown an ability to suppress rising blood pressure in animal studies.

### Preliminary anti-cancer potential
Although the topic needs follow-up studies in humans, many lab studies suggest that various kefir extracts can measurably inhibit the growth of cancer cells. Scientists hypothesize that the breakdown of proteins into effective peptide complexes, together with the immune stimulation the cultures provide, induces apoptosis – programmed self-destruction of cancer cells (such as breast and colon cancer) – in several models. It is important to stress that these findings come mainly from lab and animal studies, and no clinical treatment recommendation can be drawn from them.

### Effects on asthma, allergies and the respiratory system
Common allergic conditions usually result from the immune system mistakenly identifying and attacking fairly harmless things in the environment, triggering a violent inflammatory response in the body. In several studies in lab animals, systematic administration of kefir showed an ability to regulate asthma and allergy responses – by reducing lung infiltrates in the mucous membranes and lowering IgE levels, the main markers of allergy.

### Effects on the gut-brain axis and psychobiotics
A groundbreaking area of nutrition research focuses on "psychobiotics" – live microorganisms that influence neurological responses. Our gut microbiome clearly affects the rate and volume of neurotransmitter production, including serotonin, which plays a central role in mood – no less than 90% of it is produced in the digestive system. Regular consumption of fresh probiotics such as kefir has been linked to reduced anxiety symptoms and relief from prolonged stress, by modulating the communication pathway between the gut and the brain. In addition, kefir naturally contains tryptophan – an amino acid that is a biological precursor of serotonin, which may strengthen its mood-improving effect.

### Potential effect on blood sugar
Several clinical studies (mostly small-scale) have suggested that regular kefir consumption may help improve blood sugar control and [insulin](/en/food/supplements/insulin/) sensitivity. The bioactive peptides produced during fermentation, combined with the effect of probiotics on systemic inflammation, may be a possible mechanism. These findings are promising but still need confirmation in larger studies.

### Help digesting lactose when lactase is lacking
Most people with lactose intolerance who give up dairy find that drinking kefir does not trigger troublesome digestive symptoms at all. The many lactic acid bacteria process and break lactose down into lactic acid before human digestion begins. As a result, the total lactose remaining is much lower than in comparable dairy products. Kefir also contains live lactase enzymes that join in the stomach and help break down whatever is left.

An interesting fact: a clinical study found that drinking kefir reduced breath hydrogen (a marker of undigested lactose fermenting in the gut) threefold compared with regular milk, and gas symptoms dropped by 50%. This is also why ancient kefir allowed lactose-intolerant populations – such as the people of Xiaohe in the Bronze Age – to consume dairy long before the era of pasteurization and refrigeration.

## Downsides and side effects of kefir

Alongside its benefits, kefir has real downsides – some temporary, and some relevant only to certain groups. Here is the full breakdown.

### Downsides that affect almost everyone who starts drinking it

- **Bloating, gas and stomach pain at first (the "adjustment period"):** this is the most common downside, and it is almost unavoidable. The amount of probiotics in kefir is far higher than most people are used to, and the gut needs time to rebalance. The symptoms – bloating, gas, rumbling, sometimes mild diarrhea – usually last from a few days to two weeks and go away on their own. **The practical fix:** start with a quarter cup a day, stay at that amount for a week, and only then double it. People who jump straight to a full cup almost always experience discomfort. Some attribute this to a "Herxheimer reaction" – rapid die-off of bacteria releasing breakdown products – but the simple explanation of a changing gut flora is enough to account for it, and the Herxheimer explanation is scientifically disputed.
- **A tangy taste not everyone likes:** real kefir is sour, slightly fizzy, and has a faint yeasty aroma reminiscent of beer or [sourdough bread](/en/food/sourdough/). Anyone expecting the taste of a sweet drinking yogurt will be unpleasantly surprised. Homemade kefir that has fermented for a long time becomes significantly sourer.
- **Added sugar in commercial versions:** this downside comes from the industry, not the drink itself. Flavored kefir (strawberry, vanilla, peach) often contains 12–20 grams of sugar per cup – enough to cancel out much of the metabolic benefit. Read the label and choose plain kefir with no added sugar.

### Downsides that affect certain groups

- **High histamine content:** like all fermented foods, kefir is rich in histamine and other biogenic amines (including tyramine), which form when bacteria break down amino acids. In people with histamine sensitivity – usually caused by low activity of the DAO enzyme – this can cause headaches and migraines, itching and skin flushing, nasal congestion, palpitations or a drop in blood pressure. This downside is rarely discussed, and it is the most common explanation for why someone "just doesn't get along" with kefir even after a long adjustment period. The longer the fermentation, the higher the histamine content.
- **Tyramine and MAOI medications:** the tyramine produced during fermentation is a real problem for people taking MAO inhibitors (a class of antidepressants). The combination can cause a sharp, dangerous rise in blood pressure. People taking these medications should avoid fermented foods, including kefir, unless their doctor says otherwise.
- **Milk protein allergy:** it is important to separate two conditions that are often confused. Kefir is **safe and recommended** for most people with lactose intolerance, because fermentation breaks down most of the lactose. However, it is **not safe** for people allergic to the milk protein itself (casein or [whey](/en/food/supplements/whey-protein/) proteins) – fermentation does not break the proteins down enough to prevent an allergic reaction. In that case, choose water kefir or kefir made from a plant-based drink.
- **A weakened immune system:** for people whose immunity is severely compromised – untreated HIV patients, people in active chemotherapy, bone marrow and organ transplant recipients, people taking immunosuppressive drugs – consuming large amounts of live bacteria and yeasts is considered a risk that can, in rare cases, develop into a bloodstream infection. People in this group should consult a doctor, and it is usually better to avoid it.
- **Chronic kidney disease:** kefir is rich in [phosphorus](/en/food/supplements/phosphorus/), [potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/) and protein. For people with kidney failure, all three are closely monitored, and a daily cup of kefir may exceed the permitted allowance. Consult a renal dietitian.
- **Babies under one year:** like any cow's milk product, kefir is not intended for babies under one year old.

### Alcohol in kefir – the downside people know least about

Kefir fermentation is a double fermentation: bacteria produce lactic acid, and yeasts produce carbon dioxide **and alcohol**. This is a fundamental difference from yogurt, which contains no yeasts and therefore no alcohol at all.

In commercial kefir the concentration is very low – usually around 0.05%–0.5%, a range similar to fresh orange juice or fresh bread, and of no practical significance. In homemade kefir, however, the concentration depends entirely on fermentation conditions: a long fermentation, at a high temperature, in a sealed container and with a high ratio of grains can raise it to about 1%–2%, and sometimes higher.

Who this actually matters for:
- **People avoiding alcohol for religious reasons, addiction or personal belief** – it is worth knowing that the drink is not entirely alcohol-free.
- **Pregnancy** – there is no known safe threshold for alcohol during pregnancy. The amounts in kefir are tiny, but anyone who prefers absolute caution should choose pasteurized commercial kefir over homemade. Just as important: homemade kefir is not pasteurized, which is a separate and more significant consideration in pregnancy.
- **Children** – in normal amounts this is not a problem, but commercial kefir is preferable to homemade kefir that has fermented for days.
- **Driving or operating machinery** – a cup of kefir does not affect fitness to drive, and this is not a realistic concern.

To minimize alcohol in homemade kefir: shorten the fermentation time, ferment at a lower temperature, and use an open container covered with cloth instead of an airtight lid.

### Drug interactions

- **Antibiotics:** antibiotics will harm kefir's bacteria, and kefir may affect the absorption of some medications. Separate them by at least two hours. While taking antibiotics there is actually good reason to keep drinking kefir, to help restore the gut flora – just spaced apart in time.
- **Immunosuppressive drugs:** see the section on weakened immune systems above – medical advice is needed.
- **MAO inhibitors:** see the section on tyramine above.

### What isn't a downside, despite popular belief

- **Lactose:** not a problem for most lactose-sensitive people. See the section on lactose digestion.
- **Saturated fat:** whole-milk kefir has about 5–8 grams of fat per cup. The effects of fermented milk fat on heart health are not the same as those of saturated fat from other sources, and research on fermented dairy tends toward neutral to positive.
- **"Too many bacteria":** there is no documented harm from consuming kefir in normal food amounts in healthy people.

## Culinary uses and adding it to your diet

Kefir is most commonly drunk fresh (especially in the morning to get digestion going), or after a workout as a protein drink naturally rich in electrolytes.

In Eastern Europe, kefir is a completely everyday staple – served at breakfast, in school meals, and even used as the base for cold soups. In Russia and Poland you will find it on almost every table, much like regular milk in Western countries.

Ways to use it in the kitchen:
- **A smooth smoothie base:** blending kefir with banana and berries makes a nutrient-dense snack with an especially creamy taste. Kefir gives a richer texture than regular milk thanks to its partially broken-down proteins.
- **Baking:** you can treat kefir exactly like buttermilk. It works as a perfect leavening partner – its acidity reacts with baking soda to make light, moist baked goods. However, **an important point:** baking above 45°C (113°F) destroys most or all of the probiotic effect. The basic nutritional values (protein, calcium, vitamins) are largely preserved.
- **Salad dressings and marinades:** it can replace yogurt in a wide range of dressings or meat marinades. Kefir's acidity tenderizes meat proteins very effectively – a mechanism similar to lemon or vinegar, but with added depth of flavor and juiciness.
- **Homemade kefir cheese:** draining kefir through cheesecloth for 12–24 hours makes a soft, labneh-like cheese with a rich, tangy flavor – a healthier, probiotic-rich substitute for cream cheese (as long as it isn't heated).
- **Skin and hair care:** a non-culinary but traditional use – women in the Caucasus and Eastern Europe used kefir as a face and hair mask. The lactic acid acts as a gentle exfoliant, and the probiotics may support the skin's flora.

## Storing and caring for kefir grains

Kefir grains are living things that need care, but they are also fairly forgiving. Normally, they are given fresh milk every 24 hours at room temperature (about 20–25°C, or 68–77°F). If you need to go on vacation, you can put the grains in a cup of fresh milk and store them in the fridge for up to three weeks – the cold slows fermentation significantly without killing the organisms. For long-term storage, the grains can be dried or frozen, although some of the microbial diversity may be harmed and need a recovery period.

## Summary

Kefir stands out as one of nature's most restorative foods in the modern era, thanks to its considerable health benefits and a history spanning at least 3,600 years of documented use. Traditional fermentation preserves the basic nutrients of milk but turns it into a product with dozens of probiotic strains in stable symbiosis – including beneficial yeasts not found in yogurt. Beyond being easier to digest for people sensitive to lactose, it contributes significantly to bone strength by combining calcium with vitamin K2, curbs inflammation, shows mood-improving effects through the gut-brain axis, and may help balance seasonal allergies and blood sugar control.

On the other hand, the downsides are not negligible and are not the same for everyone: an adjustment period with bloating and gas for almost everyone who starts, histamine content that bothers people sensitive to it, a small amount of alcohol produced during fermentation, unsuitability for people allergic to milk protein, and a real risk for the severely immunocompromised. For most of the healthy population these are temporary or irrelevant obstacles – but they are the reason to start with a quarter cup a day rather than a full cup.

And perhaps the most amazing thing of all: the grains waiting on your counter are effectively "immortal" – the same community of living organisms that developed centuries ago, which cannot be produced artificially even in an advanced lab, and all it asks for is a cup of fresh milk a day.

## Frequently asked questions

### Question: What is kefir?
Answer: A fermented milk drink from the Caucasus Mountains, made by adding "kefir grains" to milk. The grains are not cereal grains but a symbiotic colony of dozens of species of bacteria and yeasts (a SCOBY). The result is a tangy, slightly fizzy drink with far greater probiotic diversity than yogurt — including yeasts, which yogurt does not contain at all.

### Question: What are the downsides of kefir?
Answer: The most common downside is bloating, gas and stomach discomfort in the first week or two, until the gut adjusts to the amount of probiotics – a temporary effect that is largely avoided by starting with a quarter cup a day. Beyond that: kefir is rich in histamine and so bothers people with histamine sensitivity (headaches, itching, nasal congestion); it contains a small amount of alcohol produced during fermentation, mainly in the homemade version; it is not suitable for people allergic to milk protein; it poses a risk for the severely immunocompromised; and flavored commercial versions often contain 12–20 grams of added sugar per cup. Full details in the section [Downsides and side effects of kefir](#downsides-and-side-effects-of-kefir).

### Question: Is there alcohol in kefir?
Answer: Yes, a small amount. Unlike yogurt, kefir grains also contain yeasts, and the yeasts produce alcohol during fermentation. Commercial kefir usually contains 0.05%–0.5%, similar to fresh orange juice. Homemade kefir fermented for a long time at room temperature can reach about 1%–2%. For most people this has no practical significance, but it matters for those who avoid alcohol for religious reasons or addiction, and for pregnant women who prefer absolute caution.

### Question: Why does kefir give me gas and bloating?
Answer: This is a normal adjustment reaction, not a sign that kefir "doesn't suit you". The variety of bacteria in kefir is much greater than what your gut is used to, and the microbial balance shifts within a few days. Symptoms usually pass within one to two weeks. If you started with a full cup, go back to a quarter cup a day, wait a week, and only then increase gradually. If the bloating lasts beyond three weeks, or headaches and skin itching appear, it may be histamine sensitivity rather than adjustment.

### Question: What is the main difference between kefir and yogurt?
Answer: Both are fermented dairy products containing probiotics, but they differ in the fermentation culture and its richness. Kefir is fermented with "kefir grains" that contain both bacteria and beneficial **yeasts**, which are entirely absent from yogurt, which relies on only a few dairy bacteria. Homemade kefir from authentic grains can contain more than 60 strains of active microorganisms (compared with 2 to 5 in standard commercial yogurt). In texture it tends to be thinner, made for drinking rather than eating with a spoon, and it is sometimes fizzy and refreshing. The production process differs too: yogurt requires heating and controlled temperatures (usually 40–45°C), whereas kefir ferments at room temperature.

### Question: Can I drink kefir if I have mild lactose sensitivity?
Answer: In the vast majority of cases – yes. Kefir's long fermentation breaks down much of the lactose into lactic acid. In addition, the many bacteria in kefir help the gut break down the remaining lactose as it moves through the digestive tract. Studies have shown that kefir significantly reduces lactose intolerance symptoms compared with regular milk. For people with a severe allergy to milk **protein** (not lactose), milk-based kefir will always trigger a reaction, so water kefir or kefir based on plant drinks is preferable.

### Question: Can kefir be heated for cooking?
Answer: You can certainly heat and cook kefir, like sour cream or yogurt, to take advantage of its rich flavor and velvety texture in sauces and stews. But remember that any heating at high temperatures (above 40–50°C, or 104–122°F) will kill most or all of its probiotic bacteria and yeasts. The food keeps nutritional values such as protein and calcium but loses the probiotic effect. It is best to add kefir to dishes only after taking them off the heat, or to use it in cold dressings and marinades.

### Question: How much kefir should I drink a day?
Answer: According to most expert recommendations, a moderate and optimal intake is one cup a day (about 240 ml). The most important advice for anyone starting kefir is to begin with small amounts of a quarter cup a day during the first week and increase gradually. A gradual increase spares you adjustment symptoms such as bloating and gas, which come from the dramatic shift in the balance of the gut microbiome.

### Question: How long does kefir keep in the fridge?
Answer: Ready fermented kefir keeps well in the fridge for at least two weeks, although its taste will keep changing and souring over time because fermentation continues slowly even in the cold. Unopened commercial kefir keeps until the expiry date on the package. Over time you may notice a clear liquid (whey) separating – this is completely normal; just stir before drinking. The whey itself is rich in protein and minerals, and there is no reason to pour it away.

### Question: Is kefir suitable for children?
Answer: Kefir is considered safe for children from one year old (like any dairy product). Because of its enormous probiotic diversity, it is best to start with smaller amounts than for adults – one or two teaspoons a day for one-year-olds, increasing gradually. In Eastern Europe, kefir is routinely given to children as part of the daily menu, including in kindergartens and schools.
