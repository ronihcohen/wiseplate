## Teff (*Eragrostis tef*)

Teff is the smallest grain in the world: a single grain weighs about a hundredth as much as a grain of wheat, and one tablespoon holds thousands of them. This tininess isn't a curiosity — it is the main reason for its nutritional value.

**Teff can't be refined.** With wheat or [rice](/en/food/rice/), refining removes the bran and germ and leaves the starchy endosperm — where most of the calories are, and where there is almost none of the fiber and minerals. With teff, the grain is too small for separating its parts to be worthwhile or even possible, so **all teff flour is necessarily whole-grain flour**. This is a built-in advantage that no other common grain has.

Teff is also the basis of **injera** — the fermented flatbread of Ethiopian and Eritrean cuisine, and a staple in the diet of tens of millions of people.

Teff contains an unusual amount of [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) for a grain — 180 mg per 100 grams, several times more than [quinoa](/en/food/quinoa/) or whole wheat. This is because the whole grain is milled, including the outer layers where the mineral is concentrated.

### Iron — a caveat worth knowing

Teff is famous as an iron-rich grain, and studies have indeed found relatively low rates of anemia in regions where it is eaten. But the picture is more complicated than it is usually presented:

* **Some of the measured iron doesn't come from the plant.** In traditional processing, the tiny grains are threshed and dried on the ground, and iron-rich soil particles remain in the flour. Measurements of carefully cleaned teff show lower iron content than the "classic" values. Iron from soil is also absorbed particularly poorly.
* **It is non-heme iron.** As with any plant source, the absorption rate is significantly lower than for iron from animal sources, and it is strongly affected by what you eat alongside it.
* **Phytate inhibits it.** Teff, as a whole grain, contains phytic acid, which binds [iron](/en/food/iron/), [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/) and [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) and reduces their absorption.

**Hence the importance of fermentation.** Traditional injera is fermented for 2-3 days with a starter ("ersho"). Fermentation activates phytase enzymes that break down a good share of the phytic acid and release the minerals for absorption. This isn't just a culinary tradition — it is nutritional technology. **Teff baked directly without fermentation provides fewer available minerals than the same amount of teff in injera.**

### Benefits

* **[Gluten-free](/en/food/gluten/):** suitable for people with celiac disease and non-celiac gluten sensitivity — subject to watching for cross-contamination (see below).
* **Always whole grain:** because of the grain's size, teff flour is necessarily whole-grain flour, with all its fiber and minerals.
* **Fiber:** 8 grams per 100 grams dry — almost a third of the daily intake, and more than most grains.
* **Plenty of [magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) and [manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/)** — 44% and 400% of the daily intake respectively per 100 grams dry.
* **Protein with a good profile for a grain:** as with most grains, lysine is the limiting amino acid — but teff's content is relatively high compared with wheat and barley. Combining it with [legumes](/en/food/lentil/), as happens naturally in Ethiopian cuisine (injera with misir and shiro — lentil and chickpea-flour stews), completes the profile.
* **Unusual [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) for a grain:** 180 mg per 100 grams.
* **Agricultural resilience:** it grows in drought conditions and at high altitudes, so it is being studied as a climate-resilient grain.

### Downsides and considerations

* **The iron content is exaggerated in marketing** — as detailed above.
* **Phytate.** It reduces mineral absorption. Fermenting, soaking or sprouting reduce it significantly.
* **Gluten cross-contamination.** Teff itself is gluten-free, but it is sometimes threshed, milled and packed in facilities shared with wheat. People with celiac disease need a product explicitly labeled "gluten-free." Commercial injera, in Israel and abroad, is commonly mixed with wheat or barley flour to cut costs — **injera at a restaurant is not gluten-free unless explicitly stated otherwise**.
* **Price and availability.** Teff is relatively expensive compared with other grains, and in Israel it is available mainly in health food stores and specialty shops.
* **Taste and texture.** A distinct earthy, nutty taste and a relatively sticky flour. It is hard to bake with on its own; it is usually mixed with other gluten-free flours.
* **Short shelf life.** The fat content of the germ makes teff flour prone to oxidation. Store it in an airtight container, preferably in the fridge or freezer.
* **A grain, not a vegetable.** 367 calories per 100 grams dry. It is a concentrated source of carbohydrate that should be measured, not a free add-on.

### Practical use

* **Cooking ratio:** one part teff to three parts water, about 15-20 minutes at a low simmer, like porridge. The result is thick — teff is a creamy grain that doesn't separate like [quinoa](/en/food/quinoa/).
* **Soak or ferment** when using the flour for baking — overnight in water with a little lemon or a sourdough starter breaks down phytate and improves mineral absorption.
* **Eat it with [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/):** [bell pepper](/en/food/red-pepper/), [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) or [tomato](/en/food/tomato/) in the same meal significantly improve absorption of non-heme iron.
* **Avoid [coffee](/en/food/coffee/) and [tea](/en/food/green-tea/) close to the meal** — the [tannins](/en/food/tannins/) in them markedly inhibit absorption of plant iron.
* **Combine with legumes:** [lentils](/en/food/lentil/), [black beans](/en/food/black-beans/) or [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/) complete the amino acid profile.
* **In baking:** start by replacing only 25-30% of the flour; beyond that the texture becomes heavy and dense.
* **When buying:** look for an explicit "gluten-free" label if that is why you're using it, and keep the flour refrigerated.

### Summary

Teff is a truly whole grain — not because of a manufacturer's choice but because its size makes it impossible to refine. It provides good amounts of fiber, magnesium, manganese, zinc and calcium, and it is gluten-free. Two points call for caution: **its iron content is exaggerated in marketing** — part of it is attributed to soil particles from traditional processing, and plant iron is poorly absorbed anyway; and **"injera" is not necessarily gluten-free**, because it is usually mixed with wheat flour. The solution to both is the same ancient one: fermentation, and combining it with [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) and legumes.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is the nutritional value of teff?**
Answer: 100 grams of dry teff provide 367 calories, 13.3 grams of protein, 8 grams of fiber, 184 mg of magnesium (44% of the daily intake), 3.6 mg of zinc (33%) and 180 mg of calcium — an unusual amount for a grain. Once cooked, the values drop to about a third: about 101 calories and 2.8 grams of fiber per 100 grams.

**Question: Does teff contain gluten?**
Answer: The grain itself is completely gluten-free. The caveats are cross-contamination during processing and packing, and above all the fact that commercial injera is often mixed with wheat or barley flour to cut costs. A person with celiac disease needs a product explicitly labeled "gluten-free," and shouldn't assume that injera at a restaurant is safe.

**Question: Is teff really rich in iron?**
Answer: It contains iron, but the picture is less impressive than it is usually presented. Some of the iron measured in traditional teff comes from soil particles left over from processing on the ground, and carefully cleaned teff shows lower values. In addition, it is non-heme iron, which is absorbed less well, and the phytate in the grain inhibits it further. To improve absorption: ferment or soak, and eat it with vitamin C.

**Question: Why is injera fermented?**
Answer: The 2-3 day fermentation isn't just a matter of taste. It activates phytase enzymes that break down the grain's phytic acid, releasing iron, zinc and calcium for absorption. This is traditional nutritional technology — teff baked without fermentation provides fewer available minerals than the same amount of teff in injera.

**Question: Why is teff flour always whole-grain flour?**
Answer: Because the teff grain is too small to separate the bran and germ from the endosperm — it weighs about a hundredth as much as a grain of wheat. In wheat and rice, refining removes the parts rich in fiber and minerals; with teff it simply isn't practical, so all teff flour contains the whole grain.

**Question: How do you cook teff?**
Answer: One part teff to three parts water, simmered gently for 15-20 minutes, covered. The result is creamy and thick, more like porridge than like quinoa or rice. You can serve it as a breakfast porridge with fruit or as a savory side with vegetables and legumes.

**Question: Is teff a complete protein?**
Answer: As with most grains, lysine is the limiting amino acid in teff — but its content is relatively high compared with wheat and barley, and its amino acid profile is considered good for a grain. Combining it with legumes completes the profile, which is exactly what happens in Ethiopian cuisine when injera is served with lentil and chickpea stews.

**Question: How should you store teff flour?**
Answer: In an airtight container, preferably in the fridge or freezer. Because the flour contains the germ with its fats, it oxidizes and spoils faster than white flour. A sharp smell or bitterness is a sign the flour has oxidized and should be thrown out.
