## Kimchi

**Kimchi is a traditional Korean dish of vegetables pickled in salt and fermented in a spicy seasoning mix.** In Korea it is eaten at almost every meal, including breakfast, and it is considered the national dish. The best-known version — *baechu kimchi* — is based on napa [cabbage](/en/food/cabbage/), but there are more than 180 documented variations, based on white radish (*kkakdugi*), [cucumber](/en/food/cucumber/) (*oi sobagi*), green [onion](/en/food/onion/) and more.

The communal tradition of making kimchi ahead of winter, **Kimjang**, was inscribed in 2013 on UNESCO's list of intangible cultural heritage. Whole families prepare hundreds of heads of cabbage together in late autumn — a remnant of a time when this was the only way to survive with a supply of vegetables through the Korean winter.

### How kimchi is made

The process explains both the benefits and the downsides:

1. **Salting:** The cabbage leaves are pickled in salt for several hours. The salt draws water out of the leaves, softens them and prevents the growth of unwanted bacteria. **This is the source of the dish's high sodium content.**
2. **Seasoning:** A paste is added of *gochugaru* (Korean red pepper flakes), [garlic](/en/food/garlic/), [ginger](/en/food/ginger/), green onion, and often ingredients from the sea as well — fish sauce, salted [shrimp](/en/food/shrimp/) (*jeotgal*) or seaweed.
3. **Fermentation:** The mixture is packed into a sealed container and fermented at room temperature for anywhere from a day to several days, then moved to the refrigerator. Lactic acid bacteria found naturally on the vegetables — mainly *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* in the first stage and *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum* later — consume the sugars and produce lactic acid. The acidity preserves the food and gives it its characteristic tangy taste.

Kimchi keeps fermenting in the refrigerator too, so its taste becomes sourer over time. "Mature", sour kimchi is considered especially suited to cooking — in soups and stews.

Kimchi is extremely low in calories — 15 calories per 100 grams — so it adds volume, flavor and acidity to a meal at almost no calorie cost. **The standout figure in the table is the sodium**, and it is the dish's main downside.

### Benefits

- **Plant-based probiotics:** Kimchi provides live lactic acid bacteria, mainly strains of *Lactiplantibacillus* and *Leuconostoc*. Unlike [yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/) and [kefir](/en/food/kefir/), it is completely free of [milk](/en/food/milk/) — an advantage for people with lactose intolerance or a milk protein allergy.
- **Prebiotic fiber alongside the probiotics:** The vegetables themselves provide fiber that feeds gut bacteria — a combination of probiotics and prebiotics in the same food.
- **Nutrient density relative to calories:** 37% of the daily intake of vitamin K and 17% of vitamin C, in only 15 calories.
- **Compounds from cruciferous cabbage:** Napa cabbage contains glucosinolates, which are being studied in the context of anti-inflammatory activity and cellular defense mechanisms.
- **Capsaicin from the pepper:** The compound that gives the heat has been studied for a mild effect on metabolism and satiety.
- **A side dish that encourages eating vegetables:** A practical advantage that is easy to miss — a spoonful of kimchi turns a bowl of rice or a plain egg into a dish with bold flavor, and it is an effective way to increase vegetable intake.
- **Preliminary metabolic studies:** Small studies in Korea found slight improvements in lipid profile and insulin sensitivity following regular intake. These are limited studies, mainly in Korean populations, and no strong conclusions should be drawn from them.

### Downsides

- **Sodium — the main downside:** A 100-gram serving of kimchi can provide 500–1,100 mg of sodium, that is, up to about half of the recommended daily limit (2,300 mg). Anyone managing high blood pressure, heart failure or kidney disease must take this into account. A realistic side-dish portion is about 30–50 grams, and at that amount the sodium is reasonable.
- **Link to stomach cancer — a link worth understanding correctly:** Epidemiological studies in Korea found an association between high intake of salt-pickled vegetables and an increased risk of stomach cancer, and **salt-preserved food** is classified by international research bodies as a possible risk factor. It is important to keep this in proportion: the link is attributed to the **salt** and the damage it does to the stomach lining, not to fermentation itself — and it was observed at typical Korean intake levels, which are much higher than what is common in the West. As an occasional side dish, there is no real concern.
- **Histamine and biogenic amines:** As with any fermented food, fermentation produces histamine and tyramine. People with histamine sensitivity may experience headaches, itching or nasal congestion. People taking MAOI medications should avoid it.
- **FODMAPs and irritable bowel:** Kimchi contains significant amounts of garlic and onion, so it is high in FODMAPs and may trigger symptoms in irritable bowel syndrome.
- **Spiciness, heartburn and reflux:** The *gochugaru* may worsen heartburn and reflux symptoms in people who are sensitive to them.
- **Not vegetarian or vegan by default:** Most traditional kimchi recipes contain fish sauce or salted shrimp. Vegan versions exist, but check the label — this is a common mistake.
- **Immunocompromised people:** As with any food containing live cultures, medical consultation is needed.
- **Pasteurized products:** Canned kimchi or kimchi pasteurized at high temperature loses its live bacteria. It remains a tasty fermented vegetable food, but without the probiotic benefit. Look for a refrigerated product in the chilled section.

### Culinary use

- **As a side dish (*banchan*)** — the traditional form: a spoonful or two alongside rice and a main course.
- **Kimchi jjigae** — a sour kimchi stew with tofu and meat. The classic use for kimchi that has become too sour.
- **Kimchi fried rice** — one of the most popular Korean dishes.
- **In eggs, on toast and in sandwiches** — a modern combination that works well thanks to the acidity that cuts through fat.
- **Note:** Heating above 45–50 degrees Celsius kills the probiotic bacteria. The basic nutritional value and the flavor are preserved, but the probiotic benefit is lost. If you are after the probiotics, eat it cold.

### Summary

Kimchi is Korean fermented vegetables that provide entirely plant-based probiotics, fiber, vitamin K and vitamin C — all at 15 calories per 100 grams. It is a real advantage for anyone looking for a dairy-free fermented food.

The main downside is **sodium**: 500–1,100 mg per 100 grams. The practical bottom line is to eat it the way it is eaten at its source — **as a 30–50 gram side, not as a main portion** — to eat it cold to preserve the bacteria, and to check the label if you are vegetarian, since most traditional versions contain fish sauce.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is kimchi?**
Answer: A traditional Korean dish of vegetables — usually napa cabbage — that are pickled in salt and fermented with hot red pepper flakes, garlic, ginger and green onion. Fermentation is carried out by natural lactic acid bacteria that produce lactic acid, which gives kimchi its tangy taste and its keeping quality. In Korea it is eaten at almost every meal, and there are more than 180 versions of it.

**Question: Is kimchi healthy?**
Answer: Yes, in reasonable amounts. It provides probiotics, fiber, vitamin K and vitamin C at only 15 calories per 100 grams, and it is a dairy-free probiotic source. The main downside is its sodium content — 500 to 1,100 mg per 100 grams — so it is suited to being a side dish rather than a main portion, and it calls for caution in people managing high blood pressure.

**Question: How much kimchi can you eat a day?**
Answer: A reasonable portion is 30–50 grams a day, the way it is eaten at its source — as a side alongside the meal. At that amount the sodium comes to 150–550 mg, which is reasonable. Anyone managing high blood pressure or kidney failure should stay at the lower end and consult a professional.

**Question: Is kimchi vegetarian?**
Answer: Usually not. Most traditional recipes include fish sauce or salted shrimp, used to deepen the flavor and speed up fermentation. There are vegan versions that use miso or seaweed instead, but you must check the ingredient list — this is a very common mistake.

**Question: Can you heat kimchi?**
Answer: You can, and it is very common in Korean cooking — in soups, stews and fried rice. But heating above 45–50 degrees Celsius kills the probiotic bacteria. The flavor and basic nutritional values are preserved; only the probiotic benefit is lost. If you want the probiotics, eat it cold.
