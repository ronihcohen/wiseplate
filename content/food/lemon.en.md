## Lemon

**A medium lemon (about 60 g of flesh) provides roughly 17 calories and about 30 mg of vitamin C, but in most uses you eat only a tablespoon or two of juice, at about 6 mg of vitamin C per tablespoon. Its practical value: it improves iron absorption from a plant-based meal, its citrate may help prevent kidney stones, and it lets you use less salt. "Alkalizing" and "detox" are not among its benefits.**

The lemon (*Citrus limon*, in Hebrew "לימון") is a citrus fruit that probably originated in South Asia. Hardly anyone eats a whole lemon, so it matters to distinguish the flesh, the juice and the peel, each of which has a different makeup. The juice holds most of the citric acid and vitamin C; the peel holds essential oils (mainly limonene) and flavonoids such as hesperidin.

### Nutrition facts

Per 100 g, and for common portions. Values are rounded.

| Nutrient | Lemon flesh without peel (100 g) | Fresh lemon juice (1 tbsp, 15 ml) |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 29 | about 3 |
| Carbohydrate | 9.3 g | about 1 g |
| of which sugar | 2.5 g | about 0.4 g |
| Dietary fiber | 2.8 g | almost none |
| [Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) | 53 mg | about 6 mg |
| [Potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/) | 138 mg | about 15 mg |
| Citric acid | high | about 0.7-0.8 g |

The juice of one lemon (about 45 ml) supplies roughly 18-20 mg of vitamin C. That is a useful contribution, but not the "daily dose of vitamin C" sometimes claimed.

### Benefits

* **Iron absorption from plant foods.** Vitamin C and organic acids improve the absorption of non-heme (non-animal) iron when eaten in the same meal. In single meals the effect is clear; when a whole diet is measured over several days, the difference is much smaller. [Cook & Reddy, 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11124756/). In practice: a squeeze of lemon over a [lentil](/en/food/lentil/) or [spinach](/en/food/spinach/) salad helps, but will not fix [iron](/en/food/iron/) deficiency on its own.

* **Citrate and kidney stones.** Urinary citrate inhibits the formation of calcium oxalate stones. In a retrospective study of patients with recurrent stones, "lemonade therapy" (lemon juice diluted in water, with almost no sugar) raised urinary citrate and urine volume. [Penniston et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17919696/). It is not a substitute for potassium citrate when a doctor prescribes it, but it is a reasonable addition that also encourages drinking.

* **Less salt, more flavor.** Sourness intensifies flavor and lets you use less [sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/), especially with vegetables, fish and legumes.

* **Vitamin C and colds: the facts.** A Cochrane review found that regular vitamin C (at least 200 mg a day) did not reduce the chance of catching a cold in the general population, but slightly shortened colds: by about 8% in adults and about 14% in children. Starting vitamin C only once a cold begins showed no consistent benefit. [Hemilä & Chalker, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23440782/). A cup of tea with lemon is soothing during a cold, but its vitamin C content is far below the doses tested.

* **Collagen.** Vitamin C is needed to make [collagen](/en/food/collagen/) in the body. People who eat enough fruit and vegetables get the amount needed, and there is no evidence that lemon "prevents wrinkles".

### Downsides

* **Teeth.** Lemon juice is very acidic (pH around 2-2.5). Sipping lemon water throughout the day, or sucking on lemon wedges, erodes enamel. Drink it in one go or with a straw, rinse with water, and do not brush right afterwards.

* **Heartburn and reflux.** In many people acid worsens symptoms. The claim that lemon "becomes alkaline in the stomach" and soothes heartburn is not supported.

* **The alkaline myth.** Food does not change the pH of the blood, which the body keeps in a very narrow range. Lemon does not "balance acidity" in the body and does not "flush out toxins".

* **Peel and coatings.** Commercial lemons are sometimes coated with wax and anti-mold agents. When using zest, wash well in hot water or choose unwaxed lemons.

* **Skin and sun.** Lemon juice on the skin followed by sun exposure can cause burns and dark patches (phytophotodermatitis). Wash your hands after squeezing lemons outdoors.

### Practical use

* **On legumes and leafy greens:** to make use of vitamin C's effect on iron absorption.
* **In place of some salt:** in salads, on fish and on roasted vegetables.
* **Homemade lemonade:** lemon juice in water with little or no sugar. A sweetened commercial drink cancels the benefit.
* **Against browning:** a few drops on cut [apple](/en/food/apple/) or [avocado](/en/food/avocado/).
* **Zest:** the peel adds strong aroma without sourness, for cakes, yogurt and sauces.
* **With green tea:** a few drops of lemon in [green tea](/en/food/green-tea/) is a nice flavor addition; do not expect a meaningful health change from it.
* **Storage:** lemons keep for weeks in the fridge in a closed bag; fresh juice can be frozen in ice-cube trays.
* **Getting more juice:** roll the lemon firmly on the counter or warm it briefly before squeezing; a room-temperature lemon gives noticeably more juice than a cold one.

### Summary

The lemon is more a seasoning than a fruit: a tablespoon of juice gives flavor, a little vitamin C, and acid that helps absorb iron and lets you use less salt. Its citrate may have a role in preventing kidney stones. On the other hand, the acid harms teeth with frequent use, and the claims about alkalizing, detox and weight loss are unfounded.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Does morning lemon water help you lose weight?**
Answer: No. Nothing in lemon burns fat. A glass of water in the morning, with or without lemon, is a good habit, and lemon can help people who find plain water hard to drink.

**Question: How much vitamin C is in a lemon?**
Answer: About 30 mg in the flesh of a medium lemon, and about 18-20 mg in the juice of one lemon. A tablespoon of juice provides about 6 mg.

**Question: Does lemon damage your teeth?**
Answer: With frequent use, yes. The acid erodes enamel. Drink it in one go, rinse with water, and wait before brushing.

**Question: Does lemon help with kidney stones?**
Answer: The citrate in lemon juice raises urinary citrate, which inhibits calcium oxalate stones. It is a reasonable addition for people advised to drink more, not a substitute for treatment.

**Question: Is lemon good for heartburn?**
Answer: For most people the acid actually worsens heartburn. The claim that it is "alkaline in the stomach" is not true.

**Question: Is the peel edible?**
Answer: Yes. Grated zest adds aroma and essential oils. Wash it well, and preferably choose unwaxed lemons.

**Question: Does tea with lemon help a cold?**
Answer: It is pleasant and soothing, but its vitamin C content is small. Even at high doses, vitamin C started once a cold begins has shown no consistent benefit.
