## Asparagus (*Asparagus officinalis*)

Asparagus is the young shoot of a perennial plant, and 100 grams of it provide **35% of the daily value of [vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) and 13% of [folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/) — for just 20 calories.**

It is also the vegetable behind one of the most common questions in nutrition: **why does your urine smell?** The familiar answer — asparagusic acid — is only half right, and **the interesting half is that the real question is not who produces the smell, but who is able to smell it.**

### The urine smell — the full story

Asparagus contains **asparagusic acid**, a sulfur compound that does not exist in any other common food. The body breaks it down into volatile compounds — mainly **methanethiol and dimethyl sulfide** — that are excreted in urine. **They are highly volatile, so the smell appears within 15-30 minutes** of eating.

**And here is the part most explanations miss.** It is commonly said that "it only happens to some people". Studies that examined this systematically found a different picture: **the vast majority of people produce the compounds. What varies is the ability to smell them.**

This is **specific anosmia** — smell blindness to a particular compound, linked to genetic variants in a cluster of olfactory receptor genes. **A substantial part of the population simply cannot detect the smell**, and so is convinced the phenomenon does not happen to them.

**The phenomenon is completely safe** and does not indicate anything.

### Green, white and purple

* **Green** — the shoot grows above ground, is exposed to light and produces chlorophyll. It is also richer in carotenoids and vitamin C.
* **White** — exactly the same plant, but **the shoot is covered with soil or a sheet and never sees light**, so it has no chlorophyll. The same principle that makes [cauliflower](/en/food/cauliflower/) white. The flavor is milder and more bitter, the texture more fibrous, and it must be peeled.
* **Purple** — a separate variety, rich in anthocyanins and sweeter. The color mostly disappears with cooking.

**Nutritionally, green is better** — more carotenoids, more [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) and more antioxidants.

### Benefits

* **[Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/):** 35% of the daily value per 100 grams — for blood clotting and for directing [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) to bone.
* **[Folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/):** 13% — significant during the reproductive years and pregnancy.
* **High protein for a vegetable** — 2.2 grams in 20 calories.
* **Inulin and prebiotics.** Asparagus is rich in **inulin**, a soluble fiber that is not digested in the small intestine and serves as **food for beneficial gut bacteria** — mainly bifidobacteria. It is one of the best prebiotic sources among vegetables.
* **Glutathione and saponins.** Asparagus stands out for its glutathione content and contains saponins (mainly protodioscin) that are being studied for anti-inflammatory effects.
* **Asparagine and a mild diuretic effect.** The compound the vegetable is named after moderately increases urine output.
* **[Iron](/en/food/iron/) and [vitamin B1](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b1/)** in reasonable amounts for a vegetable.
* **Low in calories and high in volume** — 93% water.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Purines and gout.** Asparagus is considered a **moderately high purine** vegetable and traditionally appears on restriction lists for people with gout. **The important caveat:** recent studies found that **plant purines do not raise the risk of gout attacks** the way purines from meat, seafood and alcohol do. People with active gout should get advice, but there is no justification for blanket avoidance.
* **[Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) and anticoagulants.** 35% per 100 grams is a significant amount. People taking Coumadin (warfarin) need **steady, consistent intake**, not avoidance.
* **FODMAPs.** The inulin and fructans that make it an excellent prebiotic are also what cause gas and bloating in sensitive people. **Asparagus is considered high-FODMAP** and is restricted in the elimination phase of the diet.
* **The urine smell** — as described above. Bothersome, not harmful.
* **Price and seasonality.** One of the more expensive vegetables, with a short season (spring). Out of season it is usually imported and especially expensive.
* **Spoils quickly.** Asparagus keeps "breathing" after harvest and loses sugars rapidly — two-day-old shoots are already more fibrous and bitter.
* **Canned asparagus** loses texture and color and contains added [sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/).

### Practical use

* **Store it standing in water**, like a bouquet of flowers — trim a centimeter off the base, stand it in a glass with 2-3 cm of water and cover with a bag in the refrigerator. **This extends freshness from two days to more than a week.**
* **Don't cut — snap.** Hold the shoot at both ends and bend it; it snaps on its own exactly at the border between the woody and the tender part.
* **Roast at 220°C (428°F)** with [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) and salt, 10-12 minutes. Caramelization brings out a sweetness that boiling misses.
* **Or steam for 3-5 minutes** — no more. Overcooked asparagus turns limp and gray.
* **Always with fat.** [Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/), [vitamin E](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-e/) and the carotenoids are all fat-soluble.
* **Peel only white asparagus.** Green asparagus needs no peeling, except for especially thick shoots.
* **Don't throw away the woody ends** — they are excellent for vegetable stock.
* **Raw in a salad** — thin shoots, sliced thinly on the diagonal, with [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) and [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/). Preserves all the folate and vitamin C.

### Summary

Asparagus provides 35% of the daily value of vitamin K, folate, and an unusual level of protein for a vegetable — in just 20 calories — and it is also one of the best prebiotic sources in the vegetable aisle. **Two things worth knowing:** the urine smell is almost universal, and **what varies between people is the ability to smell it**, not the production; and **white and green asparagus are the same plant** — the white one simply grows without light, so it is lower in carotenoids and vitamin C. And in the kitchen, the tip that changes the most: **store it standing in water**, like a bouquet of flowers.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is the nutritional value of asparagus?**
Answer: 100 grams of raw asparagus provide just 20 calories, 2.2 grams of protein, 2.1 grams of fiber, 41.6 mcg of vitamin K (35% of the daily value), 52 mcg of folate (13%), 2.14 mg of iron and 1.13 mg of vitamin E. Its protein-to-calorie ratio is unusual for a vegetable.

**Question: What is the difference between white and green asparagus?**
Answer: It is exactly the same plant. White asparagus grows covered with soil or a sheet and never sees light, so it does not produce chlorophyll — the same principle that makes cauliflower white. Nutritionally, green is better: more carotenoids, more vitamin C and more antioxidants. White is milder and more bitter, more fibrous, and must be peeled.

**Question: How do you prepare asparagus?**
Answer: Hold the shoot at both ends and bend it — it snaps on its own exactly at the border between the woody and the tender part, so there is no need to cut it with a knife. Then roast it in the oven at 220 degrees Celsius with olive oil for 10-12 minutes, which caramelizes it and brings out sweetness, or steam it for just 3-5 minutes. Long cooking makes it limp and gray.

**Question: Is asparagus good for gut bacteria?**
Answer: Yes, and it is one of the best prebiotic sources among vegetables. It is rich in inulin — a soluble fiber that is not digested in the small intestine and serves as food for beneficial bacteria, mainly bifidobacteria. The flip side of the same coin is that inulin and fructans put it in the high-FODMAP category, so it may cause bloating in people with irritable bowel syndrome.

**Question: Can you eat asparagus raw?**
Answer: Yes, and thin, fresh shoots are excellent for it — sliced thinly on the diagonal with lemon and olive oil. This is the way to preserve all the folate and vitamin C, which are heat-sensitive. It is important to eat it with fat, because its vitamin K, vitamin E and carotenoids are fat-soluble.
