## Polyphenols

**Polyphenols are a group of thousands of plant compounds, including flavonoids, phenolic acids and tannins, found mainly in coffee, tea, cocoa, berries, olive oil, nuts and legumes; diets rich in them are linked to better heart health, but the effect of any single compound in people is small and not always proven.** The best way to get them is a variety of plant foods, not supplements. In Hebrew they are called "פוליפנולים".

In the plant, polyphenols protect against UV radiation, insects, fungi and stress. They account for much of the color, bitterness and astringency of plant foods. More than 8,000 different structures are known.

### The main families

| Family | Examples | Main sources |
|---|---|---|
| Flavonoids | catechins and [EGCG](/en/food/egcg/), quercetin, anthocyanins, cocoa flavanols | tea, cocoa, apples, onions, berries |
| Phenolic acids | chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid | coffee, whole grains, fruit |
| Tannins | proanthocyanidins, ellagitannins | tea, red wine, pomegranate, nuts (see [tannins](/en/food/tannins/)) |
| Stilbenes | resveratrol | grapes, red wine (small amounts) |
| Lignans | secoisolariciresinol | [flax seeds](/en/food/flax-seeds/), sesame, grains |

Flavonoids make up about two-thirds of the polyphenols in the diet, and phenolic acids about one-third [(Manach et al., 2004)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15113710/).

### Which foods have the most polyphenols

The table is ranked by concentration, per 100 g or 100 ml. Spices lead it by a wide margin, but they are eaten a few grams at a time, so **the column that really matters is "practical contribution"**, which takes into account the amount actually eaten.

| Food | Relative concentration | Practical contribution to the diet |
|---|---|---|
| Cloves, dried mint, star anise | Highest | Low, eaten in tiny amounts |
| [Cacao](/en/food/cacao-nibs/) and [dark chocolate](/en/food/dark-chocolate/) | Very high | High |
| Berries: [blueberries](/en/food/blueberries/), [raspberries](/en/food/raspberry/), black currants | Very high | High |
| [Coffee](/en/food/coffee/) | High | Highest in the West, because of the amount drunk |
| [Green tea](/en/food/green-tea/) and black tea | High | High |
| Flax seeds | High | Medium |
| [Olives and olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) | Medium-high | High |
| Nuts: [walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/), [pecans](/en/food/pecans/) | Medium-high | Medium |
| Red onion, artichoke, spinach | Medium | Medium |
| [Pomegranate](/en/food/pomegranate/), dark grapes, [apples](/en/food/apple/) | Medium | High |
| Red wine | Medium | Variable |
| Whole grains and legumes | Low-medium | Medium, thanks to the amount eaten |

In Western countries **coffee is the largest source of polyphenols in the diet**, not because of its concentration but because of how much is drunk every day. Tea and fruit come next.

**Rule of thumb:** strong color, bitterness and astringency are signs of polyphenols. That is why dark chocolate has more than milk chocolate, and fruit skin has more than the peeled fruit.

### How they work in the body

Polyphenols used to be seen as "antioxidants" that mop up free radicals. Today it is clear the picture is more complex: most polyphenols are absorbed in small amounts, are modified in the liver, and what reaches the tissues is mainly breakdown products at low concentrations. Bioavailability varies greatly between compounds, and the most abundant polyphenols in the diet are not necessarily the best absorbed [(Manach et al., 2004)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15113710/).

Most of the intake reaches the colon, where gut bacteria break it down into smaller compounds, some of which are absorbed. That is how polyphenols affect, and are affected by, the microbiome, and it is one reason different people respond differently to the same food. Lab studies also suggest they switch on cellular defense pathways (such as Nrf2), but it is unclear how much this happens at the concentrations that reach tissues after a meal.

### Benefits

**Blood pressure and blood vessels.** A meta-analysis of 133 trials found that cocoa and chocolate improved blood vessel function and lowered blood pressure, and green tea lowered LDL [(Hooper et al., 2008)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18614722/). A Cochrane review of flavanol-rich cocoa found a small average drop in blood pressure, about 1.8 mmHg [(Ried et al., Cochrane 2017)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28439881/).

**Links with mortality and chronic disease.** In observational studies, drinking 3–4 cups of coffee a day, the main source of polyphenols, was associated with a lower risk of death and heart disease [(Poole et al., BMJ 2017)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29167102/). It is hard to separate the polyphenols from the rest of the food and the lifestyle here.

**Foods that are good anyway.** Polyphenol-rich foods, such as fruit, vegetables, legumes, nuts and olive oil, are the same foods the Mediterranean diet is built on. That is probably the big practical benefit: not one compound, but a dietary pattern.

### Downsides

**Limited evidence for single compounds.** Much of what is claimed for polyphenols comes from test-tube and animal studies, at concentrations far higher than what reaches the blood after a meal. Human trials usually show small effects.

**Concentrated supplements are not risk-free.** Concentrated green tea extract has been linked to cases of liver injury, mainly at high doses of EGCG taken on an empty stomach [(USP, Oketch-Rabah et al., 2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32140423/). What is safe in a cup of tea is not necessarily safe in a capsule.

**Iron absorption.** Polyphenols, especially the tannins in tea and coffee, reduce absorption of plant [iron](/en/food/iron/) when consumed with a meal. If you are at risk of iron deficiency, keep them away from meals.

**The list does not excuse every food.** Red wine and chocolate contain polyphenols, but also alcohol, sugar and fat. The polyphenol benefit does not cancel those downsides.

### Practical use

- **Variety:** several different colorful plant foods each day beat one "superfood".
- **Drinks:** coffee and tea without sugar are a convenient, large source.
- **Extra virgin olive oil:** contains more polyphenols than refined oil.
- **Skins:** eat apples, pears and vegetables with the skin where possible.
- **Cooking:** some polyphenols are sensitive to heat and water and leach into cooking water; steaming and short cooking keep more than long boiling. Eat both raw and cooked.

### Summary

Polyphenols are thousands of plant compounds that give color and bitterness to coffee, tea, cocoa, berries and olive oil. Diets rich in these foods are linked to better heart health, and trials show small effects on blood pressure and blood lipids. The effect of single compounds and concentrated supplements is less clear, and sometimes carries risk. The simple approach: a wide variety of plant foods.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What are polyphenols in simple terms?**
Answer: Compounds that plants make to protect themselves, and that give foods color, bitterness and astringency. They are found in coffee, tea, cocoa, fruit, vegetables, nuts and olive oil.

**Question: Which foods have the most polyphenols?**
Answer: By concentration: spices such as cloves and dried mint, cocoa, berries, coffee and tea. By actual contribution to the diet: coffee, tea, fruit, olive oil and dark chocolate.

**Question: Does cooking destroy polyphenols?**
Answer: It depends on the compound and the method. Some break down with heat or leach into cooking water, while others are released from the food better after cooking. Combine raw and cooked foods.

**Question: Should I take a polyphenol supplement?**
Answer: Usually there is no need. Supplements isolate one compound at a high dose, and some, such as green tea extract, have been linked to liver injury. A varied diet provides hundreds of compounds at safe doses.

**Question: Is dark chocolate healthy because of its polyphenols?**
Answer: Chocolate with 70% cocoa or more is rich in flavanols, and trials show a small drop in blood pressure. It also contains sugar, saturated fat and calories, so a sensible amount is a few squares a day.

**Question: Is coffee a good source of polyphenols?**
Answer: Yes, for most people in the West it is the largest source, mainly thanks to chlorogenic acid. Without lots of sugar and cream, it is a good drink in this respect.
