Polyphenols are the umbrella name for a huge group of more than 8,000 natural chemical compounds produced by plants. They are effectively the plant's "immune system": they protect it from the sun's deadly UV radiation, ward off insects and pests, and help it survive drought and stress. When we eat the plant, that protection passes on to us.

### The main families
1.  **Flavonoids:** the largest and best-known group (about 60% of polyphenols).
    *   *Examples:* quercetin (onions and apples), catechins, led by [EGCG](/en/food/egcg/) (green tea), anthocyanins (the purple color in berries).
2.  **Phenolic acids:** found mainly in seeds, fruit skins and vegetables.
    *   *Examples:* chlorogenic acid (coffee).
3.  **Stilbenes:** a small but famous group.
    *   *Example:* resveratrol (red wine, grapes).
4.  **Lignans:** found in seeds (such as [flax seeds](/en/food/flax-seeds/)), grains and legumes.

### How do they work in the body? The hormesis effect
It used to be thought that they were simply "antioxidants" that soak up free radicals like a sponge. Today science understands that it is more complicated than that.
Polyphenols work through a mechanism called **hormesis** – positive stress.
The body recognizes them as very weak toxins (xenobiotics). In response, it switches on powerful defense mechanisms of its own (such as the Nrf2 gene), which increase the body's production of its own internal antioxidants, improve mitochondrial function and clear out damaged cells. In other words, they don't "give" us health — they "force" the body to become healthier.

### Effect on the microbiome
Most polyphenols are not absorbed in the stomach at all. They reach the large intestine, where they serve as food (a prebiotic) for the good gut bacteria. The bacteria break them down into active compounds that are absorbed into the body and affect the brain, the immune system and metabolism.

### Which foods have the most polyphenols

The table is ranked by total polyphenol concentration. Note that the units are per 100 grams or per 100 ml, which is why spices lead it by a huge margin — but they are consumed a few grams at a time. **The column that really matters is "practical contribution"**, which takes into account the amount actually eaten.

| Food | Relative concentration | Practical contribution to the diet |
|---|---|---|
| Cloves, dried mint, star anise | Highest | Low — consumed in tiny amounts |
| [Cacao](/en/food/cacao-nibs/) and [dark chocolate](/en/food/dark-chocolate/) | Very high | **High** |
| Berries: [blueberries](/en/food/blueberries/), [raspberries](/en/food/raspberry/), black currants | Very high | **High** |
| [Coffee](/en/food/coffee/) | High | **Highest in the West** — because of the amount consumed |
| [Green tea](/en/food/green-tea/) and black tea | High | **High** |
| [Flax seeds](/en/food/flax-seeds/) | High | Medium |
| [Olives and olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) | Medium-high | **High** |
| Nuts: [walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/), [pecans](/en/food/pecans/) | Medium-high | Medium |
| Red onion, artichoke, spinach | Medium | Medium |
| [Pomegranate](/en/food/pomegranate/), dark grapes, [apples](/en/food/apple/) | Medium | **High** |
| Red wine | Medium | Variable |
| Whole grains and legumes | Low-medium | Medium — thanks to the amount eaten |

**The practical conclusion that surprises many people:** in Western countries **coffee is the number one source of polyphenols in the diet** — not because it has the highest concentration, but because it is consumed in large amounts every day. Tea and fruit come next.

**A simple rule of thumb:** strong color, bitterness and astringency are the signs that polyphenols are present. The more colorful, bitter or astringent a food is, the richer it is in them. That is why dark chocolate beats milk chocolate, why skins beat peeled fruit, and why strong tea is richer than weak tea.

**Tip:** variety beats quantity. The polyphenol families work through different mechanisms, so five different foods in moderate amounts are better than one food in a large amount.

### Frequently asked questions about polyphenols

**Question: Does cooking destroy them?**
Answer: **It depends.** Some are heat-sensitive, but others actually become more available. **[Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) and fruit:** usually destroyed by cooking. **Tomatoes (lycopene):** availability rises significantly when cooked with oil. **Tea:** boiling water extracts them well, but prolonged boiling on the stove can break them down. **Rule of thumb:** mix it up. Eat both fresh and cooked.

**Question: Is taking a "grape seed extract" or curcumin supplement enough?**
Answer: Supplements can help in acute situations (such as inflammation), but they isolate one substance out of thousands. In nature, polyphenols work in synergy. An [apple](/en/food/apple/) contains hundreds of substances that work together. A supplement contains one. In the long run, a plant-rich diet always beats the single supplement.

**Question: Is [dark chocolate](/en/food/dark-chocolate/) really healthy because of them?**
Answer: **Yes, but carefully.** Cacao beans are very rich in flavonoids. Good-quality [dark chocolate](/en/food/dark-chocolate/) (over 70% cacao solids) is an excellent source. However, it also contains a lot of sugar and saturated fat. **Recommendation:** 2 squares a day is health. A whole bar is already a fattening dessert.

**Question: Is coffee a good source?**
Answer: Surprisingly, for the average Westerner, **coffee is the main source of polyphenols in the diet!** It is very rich in chlorogenic acids. As long as you don't load it with sugar and cream, it is considered a health drink rich in antioxidants.
