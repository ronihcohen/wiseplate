### Introduction: the most vulnerable customer
Children are the perfect target audience for the food industry: they have no developed critical faculty, they directly influence their parents' spending ("pester power"), and the eating habits they acquire now will stay with them — and with the companies — for life. But what looks like an innocent colorful wrapper is actually the tip of the iceberg of a sophisticated, aggressive psychological marketing operation.

### The psychology of the shelf
Food companies invest millions in behavioral research to crack children's minds:
*   **Characters and brands:** Using familiar cartoon characters (licensed characters) on the packaging of harmful foods creates an immediate emotional connection. Studies have shown that children will prefer the taste of a completely identical snack just because of the character on the package.
*   **Eye level:** In supermarkets, the most heavily [sweetened breakfast cereals](/en/food/sweetened-cereals/) and the most attractive [snacks](/en/food/gummy-candies/) are placed precisely at the eye level of a child sitting in the cart or walking beside their parents, making them impossible to ignore.
*   **Games and gifts:** Adding toys or creating an "experience" around the food (tongue-coloring, special shapes) shifts the focus from the food itself to the thrill of play.

### The data: a growing generation
The impact of aggressive marketing is evident in childhood obesity data in Israel:
*   **First place in Europe:** Israel stands out for the worse in childhood obesity rates compared with European countries.
*   **Screens and food:** The correlation between screen hours and obesity is clear, not only because of lack of movement but because of the massive exposure to hidden and overt advertising on social networks and YouTube.

### The regulatory loophole
While countries such as Chile and Britain have taken drastic steps (banning cartoon characters on high-sugar products, restricting advertising hours), regulation in Israel still lags behind:
1.  **Poor labeling:** Despite the red-label reform, many products aimed at children are still marketed as "healthy" (added vitamins, "no food coloring") even though they are all processed sugar — a direct continuation of the patterns described on the [sugar industry](/en/food/sugar-industry-tactics/) page.
2.  **Social networks:** Regulation barely exists in the digital space, where online influencers promote snacks and [sugary drinks](/en/food/sugary-soft-drinks/) directly to their young followers, without filters.

### Summary
We must not put all the blame on parents. When the entire environment — from the screen to the shelf — is cleverly designed to make children want [ultra-processed food](/en/food/ultra-processed-foods/), "educating for smart nutrition" becomes an almost impossible task. Systemic intervention is needed to protect young minds from marketing manipulations whose cost is measured in their health.
