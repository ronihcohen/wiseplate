## Food Marketing to Children

**Food marketing to children measurably affects what they eat: in a meta-analysis of experiments, children who watched an advert for unhealthy food ate more immediately afterwards, while no effect was found in adults.** On the strength of findings like these, the World Health Organization recommended in 2023 that countries adopt mandatory policies restricting the marketing of foods high in sugar, salt and saturated fat to children. In Hebrew the topic is called "שיווק מזון לילדים".

Children are a convenient audience for advertisers: they struggle to tell content from advertising until a relatively late age, they influence their parents' shopping, and the eating habits they pick up stay with them for years. Parents remain the central influence, but they operate within an environment that is deliberately designed.

### What the research shows

* **Adverts increase eating in children:** a meta-analysis of 18 experimental studies found that exposure to a TV or online advert for unhealthy food increased how much children ate straight afterwards, with a moderate effect. No such effect was found in adults. [Boyland 2016, Am J Clin Nutr](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26791177/)
* **Cartoon characters change taste:** 40 children aged 4–6 tasted pairs of identical foods — crackers, [gummy fruit snacks](/en/food/gummy-candies/) and carrots — in packaging with and without a familiar cartoon character. The children said the food with the character tasted better and chose it as their snack. The effect was weaker for carrots. [Roberto 2010, Pediatrics](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20566614/)
* **Policy changes purchases:** in Chile, a law that combined warning labels, a ban on marketing "high-in" products to children and a ban on selling them in schools reduced purchases of high-sugar drinks by about 24%. [Taillie 2020, PLoS Med](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32045424/)

### Common marketing tools

* **Characters and licensed brands:** TV heroes on boxes of [sweetened breakfast cereals](/en/food/sweetened-cereals/), snacks and [argaliot](/en/food/argaliot/).
* **Shelf placement:** children's products are often placed at their eye level and at the checkout.
* **Gifts and games:** a toy in the box, an app or a competition turns the product into an experience.
* **Digital marketing:** influencers, videos and games on social media, where it is harder to recognise that something is an advert.
* **Health claims:** "fortified with vitamins" or "no artificial colours" on a high-sugar product creates a healthy impression — a pattern also described on the [sugar industry](/en/food/sugar-industry-tactics/) page.

### The WHO recommendation

The 2023 guideline recommends mandatory (not voluntary) policies that protect children of all ages from the marketing of foods high in saturated fat, trans fat, free sugars or salt, across all media, with a government-led definition of which products are covered. [WHO — Policies to protect children from food marketing](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240075412)

### Counterarguments and caveats

* **Short-term effects:** most experiments measure eating right after the advert, not obesity over years. The link to obesity rests mainly on observational studies.
* **Self-regulation:** the industry argues that voluntary pledges are enough. The WHO found them less effective and therefore recommends legislation.
* **Parents' role:** the environment matters, but choices at home — what is bought, served and drunk — remain very important.
* **Economic trade-offs:** restricting adverts hits the revenue of children's channels and content creators; that is a legitimate policy consideration to weigh.

### Practical tips for parents

* **Don't shop with a hungry child:** shop after a meal, with a list prepared in advance.
* **Talk about adverts:** school-age children can understand "who paid for this video, and why".
* **Fewer screens with adverts:** ad-free streaming services cut exposure.
* **A character doesn't make it children's food:** check the nutrition table, not the packaging.
* **Drinks:** water as the default, and [sugary soft drinks](/en/food/sugary-soft-drinks/) only on special occasions.
* **Get them in the kitchen:** children who help cook tend to try more vegetables and new foods.

### Summary

The evidence shows that food marketing affects children more than adults: adverts increase how much they eat, and cartoon characters change how food tastes to them. The WHO recommends mandatory legislation, and Chile has shown that such a policy can change purchases. Meanwhile, parents can cut exposure, talk with their children about advertising and choose by the nutrition table rather than the packaging.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Do food adverts really make children eat more?**
Answer: Yes, in the short term. A meta-analysis of experiments found that children who watched an advert for unhealthy food ate more right afterwards, while no effect was found in adults.

**Question: Why do cartoon characters on packaging work?**
Answer: In an experiment with 4–6-year-olds, the same food tasted better when it came in packaging with a familiar character. The effect was strongest for sweet snacks and weakest for carrots.

**Question: What does the WHO recommend?**
Answer: Mandatory policies restricting the marketing of foods high in sugar, salt, saturated fat and trans fat to children of all ages, across all media.

**Question: Do marketing restrictions work?**
Answer: In Chile, a package of laws that included restrictions on marketing to children, warning labels and a school sales ban cut purchases of high-sugar drinks by about 24%.

**Question: At what age do children understand what an advert is?**
Answer: Young children find it hard to tell content from advertising and to understand its intent. By school age you can talk with them about who funds content and why.

**Question: What can parents do?**
Answer: Limit viewing of content with adverts, shop from a list, check the nutrition table instead of the packaging, and involve children in cooking.
