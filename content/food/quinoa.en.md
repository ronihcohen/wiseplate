## Quinoa

**Quinoa is the seed of a plant in the amaranth family that is cooked and eaten like a grain; one cup of cooked quinoa (about 185 g) provides roughly 220 calories, 8 g of complete protein and 5 g of fiber, and it is naturally gluten-free.** It is more nutritious than white rice, but it is not a "superfood": its strength is a good mix of protein, fiber and minerals in one serving. In Hebrew it is called "קינואה".

Quinoa has been grown for thousands of years in the Andean highlands of Peru and Bolivia. Botanically it is not a cereal but a "pseudo-grain", like [amaranth](/en/food/amaranth/) and [buckwheat](/en/food/buckwheat/), which is why it contains no gluten. Shops sell white, red and black quinoa; the nutritional differences between them are small, and the main difference is texture and cooking time.

### Nutrition facts

Values are for **cooked** quinoa (boiled in water, no salt), approximate, based on USDA data. 100 g of dry quinoa swells to about 270 g cooked.

| Nutrient | Per 100 g cooked | Per cup cooked (about 185 g) |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | about 120 | about 222 |
| Protein | about 4.4 g | about 8.1 g |
| Carbohydrates | about 21 g | about 39 g |
| Dietary fiber | about 2.8 g | about 5.2 g |
| Fat | about 1.9 g | about 3.6 g |
| Magnesium | about 64 mg | about 118 mg |
| Phosphorus | about 152 mg | about 281 mg |
| Iron | about 1.5 mg | about 2.8 mg |
| Zinc | about 1.1 mg | about 2 mg |
| Folate | about 42 mcg | about 78 mcg |
| Potassium | about 172 mg | about 318 mg |

A cooked cup covers roughly a third of an adult's daily [magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) needs and about 15% of the [iron](/en/food/iron/) needs of a woman of reproductive age [(NIH: magnesium)](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/).

### Benefits

**A complete plant protein.** Most grains are low in the amino acid lysine; quinoa contains all the essential amino acids in reasonable amounts, lysine included. That does not make it a large protein source (8 g per cup, less than half a serving of [lentils](/en/food/lentil/) or chicken), but it does make its protein better quality than that of rice or wheat, which is useful in a vegetarian diet.

**Good amounts of fiber and minerals.** Compared with cooked white rice, a cup of quinoa has roughly three to five times the fiber, and more magnesium, iron, zinc and folate. Many people fall short on magnesium, and quinoa is an easy way to raise it.

**A modest effect on blood sugar.** In a controlled trial, 37 overweight men ate bread enriched with 20 g of quinoa flour a day for four weeks. On continuous glucose monitoring their blood sugar curve was lower than with white wheat bread, but cholesterol and other heart-risk markers did not differ [(Li et al., Nutrients 2018)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29914146/). In other words, a small benefit for blood sugar, not a medicine. Another crossover trial in older adults found a modest improvement in cardiovascular risk markers [(Pourshahidi et al., 2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31919583/). Human evidence is still limited and short-term.

**Gluten-free.** Quinoa suits people with [celiac disease](/en/food/gluten/) and anyone avoiding gluten, and it adds fiber and protein that processed gluten-free products often lack. Choose a pack labeled "gluten-free", because cross-contamination in factories that also handle wheat is possible.

**Antioxidants.** The seeds contain [polyphenols](/en/food/polyphenols/) and flavonoids such as quercetin and kaempferol, more so in the darker varieties. That is a real plus, but it has not been shown to change health outcomes in people, so it is best not to overstate it.

### Downsides

**Saponins and bitterness.** The seed coat carries saponins, bitter, soapy compounds that protect the plant from birds and insects [(review of quinoa saponins, Molecules 2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32120971/). Most commercial quinoa is already washed, but an extra rinse in a fine sieve removes leftover bitterness and the mild stomach irritation some people notice.

**A carbohydrate, not a protein food.** Quinoa is mostly carbohydrate: about 39 g of carbohydrate per cup against 8 g of protein. If you swap it for chicken or beans thinking it is a "protein source", you will eat less protein than you think. If you track carbohydrates or calories, measure a portion as you would with rice.

**Oxalates.** Quinoa contains a moderate amount of oxalate. For most people this does not matter, but anyone with calcium-oxalate kidney stones who has been told to limit oxalates should check with their dietitian.

**Phytates and iron absorption.** As in grains and legumes, the phytates in quinoa reduce how much of its iron and zinc you absorb. Eating it with foods rich in [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) (red pepper, tomato, lemon) improves iron absorption.

**Price.** Quinoa costs much more than rice, buckwheat or bulgur, while the nutritional gap between them is small. Buckwheat and [barley](/en/food/barley/) are cheaper alternatives with similar strengths (barley contains gluten).

### Practical use

- **Rinsing:** rinse in a fine sieve under running water for about half a minute, until the water stops foaming.
- **Cooking:** use 1 cup of quinoa to 1½–2 cups of water. Bring to a boil, cover and simmer on low heat for about 15 minutes, then leave covered for 5 minutes. White quinoa cooks faster than red or black.
- **Ideas:** a salad base with vegetables, lentils and olive oil; a side dish instead of rice; a breakfast porridge with milk and fruit; a filling for stuffed vegetables.
- **Smart pairings:** adding legumes or [eggs](/en/food/eggs/) raises the protein in the meal; vitamin C-rich vegetables improve iron absorption.
- **Storage:** cooked quinoa keeps 3–4 days in a closed container in the fridge and freezes well in portions.

### Summary

Quinoa is a nutritious carbohydrate side: complete protein, more fiber and minerals than white rice, and no gluten. Human trials show a small benefit for blood sugar response, not a dramatic effect on the heart or on weight. Rinse it, combine it with legumes and vegetables, and treat it as a good alternative to rice rather than a main protein source.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much protein is in a cup of quinoa?**
Answer: A cup of cooked quinoa (about 185 g) has about 8 g of protein. 100 g of dry quinoa has about 14 g, but cooking adds water and dilutes it.

**Question: Is quinoa a complete protein?**
Answer: Yes. It contains all the essential amino acids, including lysine, which most grains lack. The total amount is still modest, so it is worth pairing with other protein sources.

**Question: Do you need to rinse quinoa before cooking?**
Answer: It is recommended. Rinsing removes leftover saponins, which taste bitter and soapy. Even if the pack says "pre-washed", half a minute under running water does no harm.

**Question: Is quinoa safe for people with celiac disease?**
Answer: Yes, it is naturally gluten-free. Choose a product labeled "gluten-free" to avoid cross-contamination in plants that also process wheat.

**Question: Quinoa or brown rice: which is better?**
Answer: Quinoa is slightly richer in protein, magnesium and folate, and brown rice is cheaper. Both are good choices; the gap between them is much smaller than the gap between either one and white rice.

**Question: Is quinoa fattening?**
Answer: No more than any other carbohydrate in the same amount. A cooked cup is about 220 calories. The fiber and protein help with fullness, but portion size is what decides.
