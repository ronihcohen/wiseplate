## Peanut Butter

**One tablespoon of natural peanut butter (16 g) contains about 4 g of protein, 8 g of mostly unsaturated fat and about 94 calories. It is a nutritious spread if the ingredient list says just "peanuts", and in observational studies eating peanuts is associated with a lower risk of heart disease.** The main downsides are calories, allergy, and added sugar and palm oil.

Peanut butter (in Hebrew "חמאת בוטנים") is made by grinding roasted peanuts. One point worth knowing: **the peanut is not a nut but a legume** — a relative of [lentils](/en/food/lentil/) and [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/) that grows underground. That also explains its high protein profile compared with nuts.

### Nutrition facts

|---|---|---|---|

| Protein | 25 g | about 4 g | about 8 g |
| Total fat | 50 g | about 8 g | about 16 g |
| of which monounsaturated | 24 g | about 3.8 g | about 7.7 g |
| of which saturated | 10 g | about 1.6 g | about 3.2 g |

### What separates the products — the ingredient list

This is the factor that matters more than anything else, and the difference between products on the market is large.

**Natural peanut butter** contains one or two ingredients: peanuts, and sometimes a little salt. The oil separates and rises to the top — that is a good sign, not a defect. Just stir it in.

**Processed commercial peanut butter** also contains:
* **Sugar** — often 8-10 grams per 100 grams.
* **Partially or fully hydrogenated palm oil** — added to prevent oil separation. It adds saturated fat, and palm cultivation has a significant environmental impact.
* **Salt** in larger amounts, and sometimes stabilizers.

What this means: two products on the same shelf may be fundamentally different. **Checking the ingredient list is the most meaningful thing you can do** when buying peanut butter.

### Benefits

* **Especially high protein for a plant spread:** 25 grams per 100 grams — among the highest in the category, and above most nuts. Its limiting amino acid is methionine, so combining it with grains ([whole-grain bread](/en/food/rye-bread/), [oats](/en/food/oatmeal/)) creates a complementary profile.

* **Fat profile:** about half the fat is monounsaturated (oleic acid, the same acid that dominates [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/)). In a meta-analysis of cohort studies, every 28 g of nuts a day was associated with about a 21% lower risk of cardiovascular disease and about 22% lower mortality, and the results were similar for peanuts and tree nuts. These are observational studies, not trials. [Aune et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27916000/).

* **Vitamin E:** about 9 mg per 100 grams — about 60% of the recommended 15 mg a day. [NIH ODS: Vitamin E](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-HealthProfessional/).

* **Niacin (B3):** 100 grams provides about 13 mg, close to the full daily value.

* **Magnesium, [manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/) and [copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/):** in significant amounts — 154 mg of magnesium per 100 grams.

* **Resveratrol and phytosterols:** peanuts contain resveratrol (familiar from red wine) and phytosterols, which compete with cholesterol for absorption in the gut.

* **Satiety:** the combination of protein, fat and fiber provides high satiety. As part of breakfast it may keep you full for longer, but that does not cancel out its calorie density.

### Peanut allergy — what has changed

Peanut allergy is one of the most severe and common allergies, and unlike many others it usually does not go away with age.

But the guidelines on **prevention** have completely reversed in the past decade. For years, the recommendation was to delay exposure to peanuts in at-risk infants. **The LEAP study** (Learning Early About Peanut Allergy), a randomized controlled trial published in 2015, found the opposite: early introduction of peanuts into the diet of high-risk infants, starting at 4-11 months of age, **sharply reduced the rate of allergy**: at age 5, among infants with a negative skin-prick test, 1.9% of the group that ate peanuts developed an allergy, compared with 13.7% of the group that avoided them. [Du Toit et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25705822/).

Following it, the official guidelines in the US and other countries were changed, and early introduction is now recommended — in a safe form, meaning a thinned spread rather than whole peanuts (a choking hazard), and for especially high-risk infants after medical consultation.

In Israel, the early consumption of Bamba (a popular peanut-puff snack) was cited as one of the clues that led to the study in the first place, because of the relatively low allergy rates observed here.

### Downsides

* **Calorie density:** 588 calories per 100 grams, and about 94 per tablespoon. It is a food that is very easy to eat much more of than planned, especially straight from the jar.

* **Allergy:** as noted — severe, common and usually lifelong.

* **Aflatoxins:** peanuts are susceptible to contamination by the *Aspergillus* mold, which produces aflatoxin — a known liver toxin and carcinogen. The risk is higher in peanuts stored in humid conditions. In countries with regulatory monitoring, levels in commercial products are usually low, and the problem is worse in unregulated markets. [Front Microbiol, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31636616/).

* **Palm oil:** adds saturated fat, and its cultivation has a considerable environmental impact, including rainforest clearing.

* **Added sugar:** in many products, and sometimes not obvious from the front of the package.

* **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/):** in branded products, about 400-500 mg per 100 grams.

* **Phytic acid:** somewhat reduces absorption of [iron](/en/food/iron/) and [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/).

* **Omega-6:** peanut butter is relatively rich in linoleic acid and low in omega-3. In a Western diet that already tends toward a high ratio, it's worth balancing with omega-3 sources such as [walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/) or [flaxseeds](/en/food/flax-seeds/).

### Practical use

**What to buy:** an ingredient list of one line — "peanuts" (or at most "peanuts, salt"). Avoid products that contain palm oil, sugar or stabilizers.

**How much:** 1-2 tablespoons a day (16-32 grams), about 94-190 calories. Measure with a spoon rather than eating from the jar — that's the practical tip that saves the most calories.

**Oil separation:** a natural and desirable phenomenon in a natural product. Stir well the first time and then store in the refrigerator — that slows the separation.

**Best combinations:** on [whole-grain bread](/en/food/rye-bread/) or in [oatmeal](/en/food/oatmeal/) — the grain completes the missing methionine. With a sliced [apple](/en/food/apple/) — a combination of fiber and protein with high satiety.

**For infants:** according to current guidelines, early introduction (from 4-6 months of age) reduces the risk of allergy. You must thin the spread with water or a puree — thick peanut butter is a choking hazard. For infants with severe eczema or egg allergy — consult a doctor before the first exposure.

**See also:** [Cashews](/en/food/cashews/), [Tahini](/en/food/tahini/)

### Summary

Peanut butter is an excellent source of plant protein (25 grams per 100 grams), rich in monounsaturated fat, vitamin E and magnesium, and cohort studies link eating peanuts to reduced cardiovascular risk. The factor that matters most is **the ingredient list** — the gap between a natural product and one with palm oil and sugar is substantial. On allergy, the guidelines have reversed: early introduction for infants reduces risk rather than increasing it.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much protein is in peanut butter?**
Answer: About 25 grams per 100 grams, and about 4 grams per tablespoon. This is one of the highest concentrations among plant spreads — higher than most nuts, because the peanut is actually a legume and not a nut.

**Question: Which peanut butter is the healthiest?**
Answer: The one whose ingredient list is "peanuts" only, or at most "peanuts and salt". Avoid products with palm oil (adds saturated fat), added sugar or stabilizers.

**Question: Does peanut butter make you gain weight?**
Answer: It is high in calories — 588 per 100 grams, about 94 per tablespoon — but it is also filling, and in observational studies people who eat nuts and peanuts did not tend to gain more weight. The key is measuring with a spoon rather than eating straight from the jar.

**Question: When should peanuts be introduced into a baby's diet?**
Answer: The guidelines reversed following the LEAP study — early introduction, around 4-6 months of age, cut the rate of allergy in at-risk infants from 13.7% to 1.9% in the trial. You must thin the spread (choking hazard), and for infants with severe eczema or egg allergy, consult a doctor first.

**Question: Why does the oil separate in natural peanut butter?**
Answer: It's a natural and desirable phenomenon — the absence of stabilizers and palm oil. Just stir it and store it in the refrigerator, where separation slows down. Oil that doesn't separate usually indicates added hydrogenated oil.

**Question: Are there aflatoxins in peanut butter?**
Answer: There is a theoretical risk from fungal contamination in peanuts stored in humidity, but in countries with regulatory monitoring, levels in commercial products are usually low. Throw out a jar that smells or tastes musty.
