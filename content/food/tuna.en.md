## Tuna

Tuna (in Hebrew "דג טונה") is a large predatory fish of the mackerel family (Scombridae), and one of the most widely eaten fish in the world. Nutritionally, it provides protein at a very high density for a low calorie cost — but two key figures about it, its [omega-3](/en/food/supplements/omega-3/) content and its mercury content, **vary dramatically between species** and between forms of serving.

### Tuna nutrition facts per 100 grams

100 grams of light tuna canned in water and drained provide **116 calories, 25.5 grams of protein, less than a gram of fat and zero carbohydrates** — one of the best protein-to-calorie ratios of any food. The version in oil has nearly twice the calories, and fresh tuna is similar to light tuna in protein but much lower in sodium:

| Nutrient (per 100 g) | Light in water, drained | In oil, drained | Fresh (yellowfin) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calories | 116 | 198 | 109 |
| Protein | 25.5 g | 29.1 g | 24.4 g |
| Fat | 0.8 g | 8.2 g | 0.5 g |
| Of which EPA+DHA | ~270 mg | ~150 mg | ~270 mg |
| Carbohydrates | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| [Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) | 320 mg | ~350 mg | 45 mg |
| [Selenium](/en/food/supplements/selenium/) | ~90 mcg | ~66 mcg | ~90 mcg |
| [Vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/) | 2.2 mcg | 2.5 mcg | 2.1 mcg |
| [Vitamin D](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-d/) | ~270 IU | ~230 IU | ~70 IU |

One can of tuna (about 140 grams drained) provides about 33 grams of protein for roughly 160 calories. The two figures that don't fit in a single table and vary dramatically between species — omega-3 and mercury — are the ones that determine which tuna is worth buying and how often.

### The figure that varies: omega-3 by species

The basic assumption that "tuna is an oily fish rich in omega-3" is only partly true:

| Type | Omega-3 (EPA+DHA) per 100 g |
|---|---|
| Bluefin tuna, fresh | 1,300-1,500 mg |
| Albacore (white), canned | 700-860 mg |
| Yellowfin tuna, fresh | 250-300 mg |
| **Light tuna (skipjack), canned in water** | **250-270 mg** |
| Tuna canned in oil (drained) | 130-200 mg |

The difference between the most available and cheapest version (canned light) and fatty tuna is fivefold or more. The common canned tuna is **not** as strong an omega-3 source as [salmon](/en/food/salmon/) (2,000-2,500 mg) or [sardines](/en/food/sardines/) (1,400-1,500 mg).

An important point about tuna canned in oil: the vegetable oil in the can draws in some of the fish's omega-3, and draining the oil pours it down the sink. Tuna in water preserves omega-3 better.

### Mercury — the difference that sets the frequency

Tuna is a predatory fish that lives many years, so it accumulates methylmercury up the food chain. The concentration depends on the size of the species and its lifespan:

* **Light tuna (skipjack / "light")** — a small, short-lived fish. Relatively low average mercury, about 0.12 ppm.
* **White tuna (albacore)** — larger. Average mercury about 0.35 ppm — **almost three times as much**.
* **Bluefin and bigeye tuna** — the highest.

The guidelines of the US FDA and EPA for sensitive populations — **pregnant women, breastfeeding women, women planning a pregnancy and young children**:
* Canned **light** tuna: up to 2-3 servings a week.
* **White** tuna (albacore): up to one serving a week.
* Bluefin or bigeye tuna: avoid.

For a healthy adult outside these groups, eating 2-3 servings a week of light tuna is considered safe. Mercury mainly harms the developing nervous system, hence the emphasis on pregnancy and childhood.

### Benefits

* **Exceptionally dense protein:** 25.5 grams per 100 grams for only 116 calories. A complete protein of high biological value, supplying all the essential amino acids. One can of tuna (about 140 grams drained) provides about 33 grams of protein — more than two servings of meat.

* **A high amount of [selenium](/en/food/supplements/selenium/):** 100 grams provide about 90 mcg — over 100% of the daily intake. Selenium plays an interesting role here: it binds to mercury and reduces its bioavailability, which partly moderates the risk (a measure called the "Selenium Health Benefit Value").

* **[Vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/):** 100 grams provide about 2.2 mcg, close to the full daily intake.

* **[Vitamin D](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-d/):** one of the few food sources — about 270 IU per 100 grams in canned tuna.

* **Convenience and cost:** canned tuna is one of the cheapest and most convenient sources of quality protein. Long shelf life, no need for refrigeration or cooking — a real practical advantage.

* **Low in saturated fat:** unlike other animal protein sources.

### Downsides and risks

* **Mercury:** as described above. The main consideration, and it depends entirely on the species and the population.

* **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) in canned tuna:** regular canned tuna contains 300-400 mg of sodium per 100 grams. "No salt added" or "reduced sodium" versions exist. Briefly rinsing the tuna in water removes some of the sodium.

* **Histamine (scombroid poisoning):** fish of the mackerel family, including tuna, produce histamine if not kept properly chilled after being caught. The result is an allergy-like reaction — flushing, rash, headache and palpitations. Histamine is **heat-stable and is not destroyed by cooking**. Avoid fresh tuna whose smell or appearance is suspicious.

* **Tuna in oil vs. in water:** the version in oil contains nearly twice the calories (about 200 per 100 grams), and it also loses omega-3 when drained. The version in water is almost always the better choice.

* **Fish allergy:** one of the major allergens, and it can be severe.

* **Sustainability:** bluefin tuna stocks are in critical condition in some regions. Fishing methods using fish aggregating devices cause bycatch of sea turtles and dolphins. "Pole and line" labels or the MSC label indicate more sustainable methods.

### Practical use

**What to buy:** **light tuna in water**, preferably reduced-sodium. This is the best balance between low mercury, preserved omega-3 and calories.

**Frequency:** 2-3 servings a week for a healthy adult. One serving a week of albacore for pregnant women and children.

**How to make up the omega-3:** since light tuna provides only about 270 mg, it is worth also including [salmon](/en/food/salmon/), [sardines](/en/food/sardines/) or [mackerel](/en/food/mackerel/) in your weekly menu — smaller fish, richer in omega-3 and lower in mercury.

**Draining:** drain the liquid well from the version canned in oil. With the version in water you can use the liquid.

**Best pairing:** tuna with [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/), [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) and vegetables — the lemon contributes [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/), which improves [iron](/en/food/iron/) absorption, and the olive oil supplies the fat missing from the version in water.

### Summary

Canned tuna is an excellent, cheap and convenient protein source — 25.5 grams of protein for 116 calories — and it provides selenium, B12 and vitamin D in significant amounts. The two figures that really matter are the species and the canning method: the common light tuna contains about a quarter of the omega-3 of salmon, but also about a third of the mercury of white tuna. For most people the right choice is **light tuna in water, 2-3 times a week**, alongside other oily fish that will actually supply the omega-3.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is the nutritional value of 100 grams of tuna?**
Answer: Light tuna canned in water and drained: 116 calories, 25.5 grams of protein, 0.8 grams of fat, zero carbohydrates, 320 mg of sodium, about 90 mcg of selenium and 2.2 mcg of vitamin B12. In oil and drained: 198 calories and 29.1 grams of protein. Fresh: 109 calories and 24.4 grams of protein.

**Question: How much omega-3 is in tuna?**
Answer: It depends a lot on the type. Canned light tuna provides about 270 mg per 100 grams, albacore about 700-860 mg, and fresh bluefin tuna 1,300-1,500 mg. For comparison, [salmon](/en/food/salmon/) has 2,000-2,500 mg.

**Question: How much tuna can you eat per week?**
Answer: For a healthy adult — 2-3 servings of light tuna. Pregnant women, breastfeeding women and young children: up to 2-3 servings of light tuna, or **only one serving** of white tuna (albacore), which has almost three times as much mercury.

**Question: Tuna in water or in oil?**
Answer: In water, almost always. The version in oil contains nearly twice the calories, and the oil draws in some of the fish's omega-3 — which gets poured away when draining.

**Question: Which tuna is lowest in mercury?**
Answer: Light tuna (skipjack) — about 0.12 ppm on average, compared with about 0.35 ppm in albacore. The smaller and shorter-lived the fish, the less mercury it accumulates.

**Question: Does tuna contain vitamin D?**
Answer: Yes, and it is one of the few food sources — about 270 IU per 100 grams in canned tuna, along with about 90 mcg of selenium and 2.2 mcg of vitamin B12.

**Question: What is scombroid poisoning?**
Answer: An allergy-like reaction (flushing, rash, headache) caused by histamine that builds up in fish of the mackerel family that were not properly chilled after being caught. Histamine is heat-stable and is not destroyed by cooking.
