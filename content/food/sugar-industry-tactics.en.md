## The Sugar Industry and Science

**Internal documents uncovered in 2016 show that the US sugar industry's research foundation (the Sugar Research Foundation) funded a 1960s scientific review that played down the link between sugar and heart disease and pointed the finger at fat and cholesterol instead — without disclosing the funding.** The story does not prove that sugar is the sole cause of heart disease, but it shows how commercial interest can shape scientific debate, which is why it still matters. In Hebrew the topic is known as "תעשיית הסוכר".

### What was actually uncovered

**The NEJM review (1967):** researchers at the University of California, San Francisco found in internal documents that the foundation initiated, in 1965, a literature review on diet and heart disease that was published in the New England Journal of Medicine. The foundation set the review's objective, supplied articles for inclusion and received drafts before publication. The review singled out fat and cholesterol as the main dietary culprits and downplayed the evidence on sucrose. The foundation's funding and role were not disclosed. [Kearns 2016, JAMA Intern Med](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27617709/)

**A halted rat study (Project 259):** the same group found that between 1967 and 1971 the foundation funded a rat study at the University of Birmingham. When early results hinted at a link between sucrose and blood lipids, and with an enzyme previously associated with bladder cancer, the foundation ended the funding and the results were never published. [Kearns 2017, PLoS Biol](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29161267/)

### What we know today about sugar and health

* **The WHO recommendation:** limit free sugars (added sugar, [honey](/en/food/honey/), syrups and fruit juices) to less than 10% of daily calories, and ideally less than 5%. On a 2,000-calorie diet that is under 50 g a day, and ideally about 25 g (about 6 teaspoons). [WHO — Guideline: sugars intake](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241549028)
* **Tooth decay:** the systematic review prepared for that guideline found moderate-quality evidence that caries is lower when free-sugar intake is below 10% of calories. [Moynihan 2014, J Dent Res](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24323509/)
* **Diabetes and weight:** in a meta-analysis of cohort studies, people who drank 1–2 servings a day of [sugary soft drinks](/en/food/sugary-soft-drinks/) had a 26% higher risk of type 2 diabetes than those who rarely drank them. [Malik 2010, Diabetes Care](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20693348/)
* **Liver and blood lipids:** excess calories from fructose raise liver fat in short trials; the difference between [corn syrup](/en/food/corn-syrup/) and ordinary sugar is small.

### Tactics that are still around

1. **Many names for sugar:** on an ingredient list, sugar appears as dextrose, glucose syrup, maltose, agave syrup, fruit juice concentrate and more. Splitting it into several types pushes each one further down the list.
2. **"Low fat" with added sugar:** some reduced-fat products, such as yogurts and [energy bars](/en/food/processed-energy-bars/), add sugar to make up for the lost flavour.
3. **Funding research:** industry-funded research is not invalid by definition, but it is worth checking who paid, whether the funding was disclosed, and what independent reviews say.

### Benefits (the other side of sugar)

Sugar itself has legitimate roles: it is a fast energy source during long exercise, it improves flavour and texture, and it works as a preservative in jams. The problem is the amount, not its presence in the diet.

### Counterarguments and caveats

* **Not only sugar:** the documents show an influence on the debate, but they do not prove that sugar is the main cause of heart disease. Saturated fat, smoking, blood pressure and inactivity also play a part.
* **"Sugar addiction":** the claim that sugar is addictive like a drug is popular, but a review of the literature found little evidence for it in humans; addiction-like behaviour in animals appeared only with intermittent access to sugar. [Westwater 2016, Eur J Nutr](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27372453/)
* **The debate has moved on:** since the 2000s, official guidelines, including the WHO's, address added sugar explicitly.

### Practical use

* **Read the "of which sugars" line:** 5 g of sugar is roughly one teaspoon.
* **Look for every name:** add up all the types of sugar on the ingredient list.
* **Start with drinks:** for many people they are the largest source of added sugar.
* **Ask who paid:** when a study makes headlines, check the conflict-of-interest statement at the end of the paper.

### Summary

Documented internal papers show that in the 1960s the sugar industry funded a review that shifted the blame to fat, and stopped a study whose results were inconvenient for it. Today's evidence supports keeping free sugar below 10% of calories because of tooth decay, weight and diabetes — without needing exaggerated claims such as "addiction". The main lessons are to check who funds research, and to cut added sugar, especially in drinks.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What exactly did the sugar industry do in the 1960s?**
Answer: The industry's research foundation funded a review published in the NEJM in 1967, set its objective and received drafts. The review downplayed the link between sugar and heart disease and emphasised fat, and the funding was not disclosed.

**Question: Does that mean fat is harmless?**
Answer: No. The revelation shows that the debate on sugar was skewed, not that saturated fat is safe. Current guidelines recommend limiting both added sugar and saturated fat.

**Question: How much sugar is OK per day?**
Answer: The WHO recommends less than 10% of calories from free sugars, and ideally less than 5%. On a 2,000-calorie diet that is under 50 g, and ideally about 25 g.

**Question: Is sugar addictive like a drug?**
Answer: The evidence in humans is weak. A 2016 review found that addiction-like behaviour in animals appeared only with intermittent access to sugar, and is probably driven by sweet taste rather than a chemical effect of sugar.

**Question: How do I spot hidden sugar on a label?**
Answer: Look for dextrose, glucose syrup, corn syrup, maltose, agave syrup and fruit juice concentrate. Add them all up, and check the "of which sugars" line in the nutrition table.

**Question: Is industry-funded research always biased?**
Answer: Not always, but the risk of bias is higher. Check whether the funding was disclosed and whether the conclusions match independent reviews.
