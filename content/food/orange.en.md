## Orange

The orange (*Citrus sinensis*), in Hebrew "תפוז", is a familiar citrus fruit rich in [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/), unique flavonoids and dietary fiber. For decades it has been considered a symbol of healthy eating — largely with good reason, although the difference between eating the whole fruit and drinking its juice is nutritionally significant.

The orange originated in Southeast Asia and comes in dozens of varieties — navel, Valencia, blood orange and more. All varieties share the same basic nutritional profile, with slight differences in sugar and antioxidant levels.

### Research-based health benefits

* **[Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) — a classic source:** a medium orange (about 130 grams) provides about 70 mg of vitamin C — about 78% of the recommended daily intake. The vitamin supports [collagen](/en/food/collagen/) production, absorption of [iron](/en/food/iron/) from plant sources, immunity and the maintenance of blood vessels.

* **Hesperidin:** a flavanone unique to citrus, concentrated in the white layer between the peel and the fruit (the albedo). Studies link it to improved flexibility and endothelial function of blood vessels, a reduction in mild high blood pressure and anti-inflammatory effects.

* **Naringenin:** another flavanone with the ability to improve [insulin](/en/food/supplements/insulin/) sensitivity and support the expression of anti-inflammatory genes — studied in the context of preventing metabolic syndrome.

* **Folate:** a medium orange provides about 40 mcg of [folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/). This is important for women of reproductive age and during pregnancy to support normal neural development of the fetus.

* **Soluble fiber (pectin):** pectin slows the absorption of sugar into the blood, helps balance cholesterol and acts as a prebiotic for gut bacteria.

* **[Potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/):** a medium orange provides about 230 mg of potassium, contributing to blood pressure regulation and cardiovascular function.

* **Thiamine (vitamin B1):** supports nervous system function and the metabolism of carbohydrates into energy.

### Downsides and health considerations

* **Prefer the whole fruit over juice:** orange juice, even freshly squeezed, is concentrated in sugar and depleted of fiber. A glass of juice (240 ml) is equivalent to the sugar of 3-4 oranges with almost no fiber. The whole fruit is always better in terms of glycemic load.

* **Acidity and reflux:** the orange's low pH may irritate the lining of the esophagus in people who suffer from gastroesophageal reflux. Eat it with a meal and not on an empty stomach.

* **Drug interactions:** furanocoumarins in citrus fruits (more in [grapefruit](/en/food/grapefruit/) than in oranges, but present in them too) inhibit the CYP3A4 enzyme in the liver, which affects the metabolism of many drugs — statins, benzodiazepines and more. People taking prescription medication should check with a doctor.

* **Teeth:** the acidity may damage enamel with very frequent consumption — rinse your mouth with water after eating.

### Culinary and practical use

* **Raw with all its fiber:** the best way — eat it with the white layer (the albedo), don't throw it away
* **Grated peel (zest):** very rich in flavonoids and essential oils — adds to baking, sauces and marinades
* **In a salad with olive oil:** the flavanones are partly fat-soluble; combining with [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) increases absorption
* **As a marinade ingredient:** the acidity and rich aroma tenderize protein and add depth of flavor

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Is freshly squeezed orange juice healthy?**
Answer: Better than pasteurized juice, but it still lacks the fiber and is concentrated in sugar. It's better to eat a whole orange.

**Question: Are oranges good for a cold?**
Answer: Vitamin C may shorten the duration of a cold and ease symptoms, but it does not prevent it. That said, the orange is a good source of vitamin C for everyday intake.

**Question: Which is better — an orange or a clementine?**
Answer: A clementine contains less acidity and fewer flavanones. The orange is nutritionally preferable, although both are healthy.

**Question: Is the white pith of the orange edible?**
Answer: Yes — the albedo (the white pith) is rich in pectin and hesperidin. Its slight bitterness is harmless, and it's worth eating.

### Summary

A whole orange is one of the simplest entry points to a diet rich in antioxidants. It's better to eat the fruit than to drink juice, to keep the fiber and not throw away the white pith — that's where a significant share of the health-promoting flavanones is concentrated.
