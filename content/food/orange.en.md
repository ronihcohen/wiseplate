## Orange

**A medium orange (about 130 g) provides roughly 62 calories, 3 g of fiber, about 70 mg of vitamin C (most of the daily requirement) and about 40 mcg of folate. The whole fruit is an excellent choice; a glass of orange juice has almost twice the calories and sugar and hardly any fiber, and in cohort studies fruit juice was linked to a higher risk of diabetes.**

The orange (*Citrus sinensis*, in Hebrew "תפוז") is the most widely eaten citrus fruit, in varieties such as navel, Valencia and blood orange. All share a similar nutritional profile: vitamin C, soluble fiber, potassium, folate and flavanones, chiefly **hesperidin**, which is concentrated in the white membranes and the albedo (the white layer under the peel).

### Nutrition facts

For a medium whole orange and a glass of orange juice (240 ml). Values are rounded; vitamin C in juice varies with type and storage.

| Nutrient | Whole orange (130 g) | Orange juice (240 ml) |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | about 62 | about 110 |
| Carbohydrate | about 15 g | about 26 g |
| Sugar | about 12 g | about 21 g |
| Dietary fiber | about 3.1 g | about 0.5 g |
| [Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) | about 70 mg | about 80-120 mg |
| [Folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/) | about 40 mcg | about 45-75 mcg |
| [Potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/) | about 235 mg | about 450-500 mg |
| Glycemic index | about 43 | about 50 |

Per 100 g of orange: about 47 calories, 9.4 g of sugar, 2.4 g of fiber and 53 mg of vitamin C.

### Benefits

* **Vitamin C.** One orange supplies about 70 mg, close to the recommended intake for adults (75 mg for women, 90 for men). Vitamin C is needed to make [collagen](/en/food/collagen/), supports immune function and improves iron absorption from plants. [NIH ODS: Vitamin C](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminC-HealthProfessional/). Having an orange with a legume meal is a simple way to use this for better [iron](/en/food/iron/) absorption.

* **Folate.** A medium orange provides about a tenth of the recommended intake (400 mcg dietary folate equivalents). Folate matters especially for women who may become pregnant, although the recommendation for them is a supplement in addition to food. [NIH ODS: Folate](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Folate-HealthProfessional/).

* **Citrus, heart and mortality.** A meta-analysis of cohort studies found an inverse association between eating citrus fruit and both cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality. [Aune et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28338764/). That is observational, and fits the general advice to eat fruit and vegetables.

* **Soluble fiber.** The pectin in oranges, mostly in the membranes, slows sugar absorption and adds to fullness. That is why a whole orange raises blood sugar more slowly than juice.

* **Hesperidin: less than promised.** Hesperidin has been studied for blood vessels. In a controlled trial, a glass of orange juice raised blood levels of flavanone metabolites but did not change cardiovascular risk markers over the following hours. [Schär et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25788001/). There is no basis for claims that oranges "lower blood pressure".

### Downsides

* **Juice is not fruit.** In three large cohort studies, whole oranges were not associated with diabetes risk, but every three glasses of fruit juice a week was associated with an 8% higher risk. [Muraki et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23990623/). A glass of juice contains the sugar of two to three oranges without the fiber, and goes down fast.

* **Acidity and reflux.** In people with reflux, oranges, and especially juice on an empty stomach, can worsen heartburn.

* **Teeth.** Sipping juice through the day erodes enamel. Better to drink it with a meal and rinse with water.

* **Medications: only in specific cases.** Ordinary oranges are not considered a CYP3A4 problem the way [grapefruit](/en/food/grapefruit/) is. The exceptions: Seville (bitter) oranges, used for marmalade, can act like grapefruit, and orange juice, like apple juice, reduces absorption of the antihistamine fexofenadine (Allegra). [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/grapefruit-juice-and-some-drugs-dont-mix).

* **Potassium in kidney disease.** A glass of juice supplies a substantial amount of potassium; anyone on a potassium restriction needs to count it.

### Practical use

* **Eat the membranes:** peeling by hand, which leaves some of the white layer, keeps more fiber and hesperidin than cutting clean segments.
* **If you drink juice:** a small glass (about 150 ml) with a meal, not as a thirst quencher. Water with orange slices is a good alternative.
* **In salads:** orange segments with beets, fennel or leafy greens and a little [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/).
* **With a plant-based meal:** an orange as dessert after a legume or whole-grain meal improves iron absorption.
* **Zest:** washed orange peel adds aroma to baking and sauces.
* **Storage:** about a week at room temperature, several weeks in the fridge.
* **For children:** orange segments make a better snack than juice boxes, which deliver the sugar without the fiber and are easy to over-drink.

### Summary

The orange is one of the most accessible and cheapest sources of vitamin C, and also provides fiber, folate and potassium for under 65 calories. The important decision is whole fruit or juice: the fruit wins on almost every measure. Most people can eat oranges without worrying about medication; the exceptions are Seville oranges and fexofenadine. One or two whole oranges a day is a simple, cheap habit that covers most of your vitamin C needs.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in an orange?**
Answer: About 47 calories per 100 g, so roughly 62 for a medium 130 g orange. A large orange reaches about 85 calories.

**Question: How much vitamin C is in an orange?**
Answer: About 70 mg in a medium orange, close to the whole recommended daily intake.

**Question: Is natural orange juice as healthy as an orange?**
Answer: No. Even freshly squeezed juice loses most of the fiber, contains almost twice the sugar per glass, and in cohort studies fruit juice was linked to a higher risk of diabetes.

**Question: Are oranges suitable for people with diabetes?**
Answer: Yes, as whole fruit within carbohydrate counting. Their glycemic index is low (about 43). Juice raises blood sugar faster.

**Question: Do oranges affect medications like grapefruit?**
Answer: Ordinary oranges usually do not. Seville oranges can, and orange juice reduces absorption of fexofenadine. Check your drug's leaflet.

**Question: Do oranges help against colds?**
Answer: Regular vitamin C slightly shortens colds but does not prevent them. Oranges are a good way to get vitamin C, not a medicine.

**Question: Should I eat the white part?**
Answer: Yes. The membranes and white layer contain fiber and hesperidin. They are slightly bitter, but there is no reason to remove them.
