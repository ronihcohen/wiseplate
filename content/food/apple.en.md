## Apple

**A medium apple (about 180 g, with the peel) provides roughly 95 calories, 25 g of carbohydrate and 4.4 g of fiber, with a low glycemic index of about 36. Its value lies less in vitamins than in soluble fiber (pectin) and the polyphenols in the peel; in a controlled trial, two apples a day modestly lowered total and LDL cholesterol.**

The apple (*Malus domestica*, in Hebrew "תפוח") is one of the most widely eaten fruits in the world and is grown commercially in thousands of varieties. Nutritionally it is not a "vitamin bomb": its vitamin C content is modest next to citrus fruit or [strawberries](/en/food/strawberry/). What sets it apart are two other components, **pectin** and **polyphenols concentrated in the peel**, and those two explain most of what research has found about it.

### Nutrition facts

Per 100 g of raw apple with the peel, and in the second column for a medium apple (about 180 g). Values are rounded and vary a little between varieties.

| Nutrient | Per 100 g | Medium apple (180 g) |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 52 | about 95 |
| Carbohydrate | 13.8 g | about 25 g |
| Sugars | 10.4 g | about 19 g |
| Dietary fiber | 2.4 g | about 4.4 g |
| Protein | 0.3 g | about 0.5 g |
| Fat | 0.2 g | about 0.3 g |
| [Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) | 4.6 mg | about 8 mg |
| [Potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/) | 107 mg | about 195 mg |
| Water | 86% | 86% |

The peel is the part that matters most: it holds roughly half the fiber and most of the [polyphenols](/en/food/polyphenols/), including almost all of the quercetin. A peeled apple loses much of the value described below.

### Benefits

* **Cholesterol: there is a controlled trial, and the effect is modest.** In a randomized crossover trial, 40 adults with mildly raised cholesterol ate two apples a day for 8 weeks and, in the other period, drank an apple beverage matched for sugar and calories. After the apple period total cholesterol was 5.89 versus 6.11 mmol/L, LDL 3.72 versus 3.86 mmol/L, and triglycerides were lower; blood pressure did not change. [Koutsos et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31840162/). The accepted mechanism: pectin binds bile acids in the gut, and the liver pulls cholesterol from the blood to make new ones. It is the same mechanism behind the beta-glucan in [oatmeal](/en/food/oatmeal/) and [barley](/en/food/barley/).

* **Type 2 diabetes: whole fruit yes, juice no.** In an analysis of three large US cohort studies (about 187,000 participants), every three servings a week of apples and pears was associated with a 7% lower risk of type 2 diabetes, whereas the same increase in fruit juice was associated with an 8% higher risk. [Muraki et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23990623/). This is observational research, so it shows an association rather than proof of cause.

* **Heart and blood vessels.** A meta-analysis of cohort studies found an inverse association between eating apples and pears and both cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality. [Aune et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28338764/). It is hard to separate the apple from an overall healthy diet, so read this as support for eating fruit rather than as a "medicine".

* **Prebiotic fiber.** Pectin is not digested in the small intestine. In the colon, bacteria ferment it into short-chain fatty acids, mainly butyrate, which serves as a fuel for the cells lining the colon.

* **Fullness for fewer calories.** Eighty-six percent water, plenty of chewing and few calories: a whole apple is more filling than the same calories as juice or a processed snack.

### Downsides

* **Apple juice is not an apple.** Juicing removes almost all the fiber and most of the peel's polyphenols. A glass of juice contains about as much sugar as a sweetened drink, is absorbed faster, and juice is what was linked to a higher diabetes risk in the Muraki analysis above.

* **FODMAPs and bloating.** Apples are rich in fructose and sorbitol. In people with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) these are common triggers of gas and abdominal pain, and a low-FODMAP diet, which temporarily limits such foods, reduced symptoms in a controlled trial. [Staudacher et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28625832/).

* **Mouth itching in people allergic to birch pollen.** Some people with pollen allergy feel tingling or itching of the lips and mouth from fresh apple. The reaction usually disappears when the apple is cooked or baked, because the protein responsible is sensitive to heat.

* **Pesticide residues.** Apples are tested often and frequently appear on residue lists. Thorough washing under running water removes part of the residue. Since the peel is the point, wash well rather than peel.

* **Acidity and teeth.** Malic acid, especially in juice or when sipped through the day, contributes to enamel erosion. Rinse with water and avoid brushing right after acidic fruit.

* **Seeds.** Apple seeds contain amygdalin, which releases cyanide when chewed. A seed or two swallowed by accident is not a problem; there is no reason to grind seeds and eat them.

### Practical use

* **Choosing a variety:** red varieties (Pink Lady, Red Delicious) carry more anthocyanins in the peel; tart varieties such as Granny Smith contain slightly less sugar. The differences are small: the variety you enjoy and actually eat is the best one.
* **Storage:** in the fridge (0-4°C) an apple keeps its texture for weeks. Keep it away from leafy greens, because it releases ethylene, which speeds ripening.
* **Browning:** the enzyme polyphenol oxidase browns cut fruit. A few drops of [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) slow the process. Browning is harmless.
* **Cooking:** baking and stewing reduce some of the vitamin C but keep most of the fiber. A baked apple with cinnamon and no added sugar makes a good dessert.
* **Pairings:** sliced apple with [peanut butter](/en/food/peanut-butter/) or yogurt adds protein and fat and keeps you full for longer.
* **Lunchbox and snack use:** a whole apple travels well without refrigeration for a day, which makes it one of the easiest fruits to have on hand instead of a packaged snack.

### Summary

The apple is a cheap, widely available fruit that stores well, and its value comes from fiber and polyphenols rather than vitamins. The best evidence about it concerns a small drop in cholesterol and an observational link with a lower risk of diabetes. Two practical rules: eat it with the peel, and prefer the whole fruit to juice.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in an apple?**
Answer: About 52 calories per 100 g, so roughly 95 calories for a medium 180 g apple. A small apple (about 150 g) provides about 78 calories.

**Question: Are green or red apples better?**
Answer: The difference is small. Tart green apples have slightly less sugar, and red ones have more anthocyanins in the peel. Choose by taste.

**Question: Are apples suitable for people with diabetes?**
Answer: Yes, as part of carbohydrate counting. A whole apple has a low glycemic index (about 36), and the fiber slows sugar absorption. Apple juice raises blood sugar faster and is better avoided.

**Question: Do I have to eat the peel?**
Answer: You don't have to, but the peel holds about half the fiber and most of the antioxidants. Wash it well and eat it, unless you find it hard to digest.

**Question: Does "an apple a day keep the doctor away"?**
Answer: Not literally. A study of nearly 8,400 US adults found that daily apple eaters did not visit the doctor less often once other factors were accounted for, although they used slightly fewer prescription medications. [Davis et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25822137/).

**Question: Why do apples make me bloated?**
Answer: Most likely because of the fructose and sorbitol, which are FODMAPs. This is common in people with IBS and is not an allergy. Cooked apple or a smaller portion is often tolerated better.

**Question: Do apples help with weight loss?**
Answer: They can help as a replacement for a high-calorie snack, thanks to their volume, water and fiber. There is no "fat-burning" ingredient in them.
