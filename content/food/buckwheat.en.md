## Buckwheat (*Fagopyrum esculentum*)

The name is misleading in two languages. In English it is called "buckwheat" and in Hebrew "כוסמת", and both hint at wheat or spelt — **but buckwheat is not related to them at all.** It is not a grain and does not belong to the grass family; botanically it is a relative of rhubarb and sorrel (the knotweed family, Polygonaceae), and what we eat is its seed. Hence the nickname **pseudo-grain**, together with [quinoa](/en/food/quinoa/), [amaranth](/en/food/amaranth/) and [teff](/en/food/teff/).

The immediate implication: **buckwheat is completely [gluten-free](/en/food/gluten/).** The second implication is more interesting — its protein profile is exceptional in the plant world.

**Don't confuse buckwheat with spelt.** Buckwheat is the seed of a plant from the knotweed family and is not a relative of wheat; spelt is a species of wheat in every respect, and contains [gluten](/en/food/gluten/). Their Hebrew names are confusingly similar — "כוסמת" (buckwheat) and "כוסמין" (spelt) — which trips up many people, and for people with celiac disease the difference is critical.

### The protein — and why it is exceptional

Most plant proteins are low in **lysine**, which is why grains and legumes "complement" each other. Buckwheat breaks the pattern: it is **unusually rich in lysine for a plant**, and its amino acid profile is relatively balanced — including tryptophan and threonine.

The fair caveat: **digestibility**. Buckwheat contains protease inhibitors and [tannins](/en/food/tannins/) that lower the digestibility of its protein, so its PDCAAS score is lower than you would expect from its amino acid composition alone. In other words: **the nutrients are there, but not all of them are absorbed.** Good cooking and rinsing beforehand improve the situation.

### Rutin — the compound buckwheat is known for

Buckwheat is one of the richest dietary sources of **rutin** — a flavonoid from the quercetin family. Rutin has been studied mainly in the context of **the integrity of capillary walls**, and in Europe it was used for years as a drug for chronic venous disease and hemorrhoids.

Two important things to know:
* **Bitter buckwheat (Tartary, *Fagopyrum tataricum*)** contains dozens of times more rutin than regular buckwheat. It is common in China and the Himalayas and is marketed as a tea.
* **Rutin is sensitive to processing.** Roasting the groats (kasha) and soaking flour damage some of it. The amount you get from a regular serving of buckwheat is much smaller than the dose in supplements and drugs.

### Benefits

* **Gluten-free:** Suitable for celiac disease and gluten sensitivity — subject to cross-contamination (see below).
* **Lysine and protein with a good profile** — as detailed above.
* **Fiber:** 10 grams per 100 grams dry, including soluble fiber; cooling after cooking also creates **resistant starch**, which serves as a substrate for gut bacteria.
* **[Magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/):** 231 mg per 100 grams dry — 55% of daily intake, one of the best plant sources.
* **[Copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/) and [manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/)** in very high amounts.
* **A relatively low glycemic index (around 45-55)** compared with grains; small intervention studies showed improved glycemic response when refined grain was replaced with buckwheat.
* **D-chiro-inositol:** A compound buckwheat is relatively rich in, studied in the context of [insulin](/en/food/supplements/insulin/) sensitivity. The evidence is early.
* **Buckwheat [honey](/en/food/honey/)** — especially dark and richer in polyphenols than most types of honey, comes from the same plant.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Allergy — the real risk, not "natural toxins."** Buckwheat allergy exists, and unlike most allergies to plant foods it can be **severe and even anaphylactic**. It is especially common in Japan and Korea, where buckwheat is eaten in large amounts (soba noodles). Anyone who experiences symptoms after eating it needs an allergy workup, not to "try again."
* **Fagopyrin.** A photosensitizing compound that causes increased sensitivity to the sun. It is concentrated in the **leaves, flowers and sprouts** — not in the groats. Drinking buckwheat leaf tea or eating large amounts of sprouts may cause the effect; the cooked groat is not a problem.
* **Gluten cross-contamination.** Buckwheat is gluten-free, but commercial soba noodles almost always contain wheat flour (sometimes 60-80%!), and buckwheat flour products are sometimes milled in shared facilities. People with celiac disease need explicit labeling.
* **A dominant flavor.** Roasted buckwheat (kasha) has a strong earthy, nutty flavor that is not for everyone. Unroasted buckwheat (greenish) is much milder — if you were disappointed by one, try the other.
* **Protease inhibitors and tannins** — reduce the digestibility of the protein.
* **A concentrated carbohydrate food.** 343 calories per 100 grams dry. Measure it like any grain, and don't treat it like a vegetable.
* **Shelf life.** Its fat and germ content make the groats, and especially the flour, prone to oxidation. Store in an airtight container, preferably refrigerated.

### Practical use

* **Rinse before cooking** — removes dust, tannins and excess bitterness.
* **A 1:2 cooking ratio** (one cup of buckwheat to two cups of water), 12-15 minutes at a low simmer, covered, then 5 minutes of rest. Buckwheat cooks faster than brown rice and tends to fall apart if overcooked.
* **Toast it first:** A minute or two in a dry pan strengthens the flavor and keeps the groats separate.
* **Cool it and use it tomorrow.** Cooking and cooling create resistant starch — an improved glycemic response and a substrate for gut bacteria.
* **Roasted or unroasted?** For kasha — roasted. For breakfast porridge or a mild side dish — the greenish unroasted version.
* **In baking:** Gluten-free buckwheat flour needs a binder. Start by replacing 25-30% of the flour, or combine with other flours and with egg or [chia seeds](/en/food/chia-seeds/).
* **Combine with [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/)** to improve absorption of non-heme iron — [lemon](/en/food/lemon/), [tomato](/en/food/tomato/) or [bell pepper](/en/food/red-pepper/) in the same meal.
* **When buying soba:** Read the ingredients. "100% buckwheat" (juwari soba) exists but is expensive; most products contain wheat.

### Summary

Buckwheat is not wheat and not a relative of it — it is a gluten-free seed from the rhubarb family, and that changes almost everything. It provides 10 grams of fiber, 231 mg of magnesium and 13.3 grams of lysine-rich protein per 100 grams dry, and a relatively low glycemic index. Two caveats are worth keeping in mind: **buckwheat allergy can be severe** — that is the real risk, not vague "natural toxins"; and **soba noodles almost always contain wheat**, so they are not a solution for celiac disease unless explicitly stated otherwise.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Does buckwheat contain gluten?**
Answer: No. Buckwheat is not a grain and is not related to wheat — it is a seed from the knotweed family, a relative of rhubarb and sorrel, and therefore completely gluten-free. The caveats are cross-contamination during milling and packaging, and above all commercial soba noodles, which almost always contain wheat flour at a rate of 60-80%.

**Question: What are the nutrition facts of buckwheat?**
Answer: 100 grams of dry buckwheat provide 343 calories, 13.3 grams of protein, 10 grams of fiber, 231 mg of magnesium (55% of daily intake), 1.1 mg of copper (122%) and 1.3 mg of manganese (57%). Cooked, it is about 92 calories and 2.7 grams of fiber per 100 grams.

**Question: Is buckwheat a complete protein?**
Answer: Its amino acid profile is relatively balanced and exceptional in the plant world for its richness in lysine — the amino acid missing from most grains. The caveat is digestibility: its protease inhibitors and tannins lower absorption, so its protein score is lower than the amino acid composition alone promises. Rinsing and good cooking improve this.

**Question: What is rutin and why is it important?**
Answer: A flavonoid from the quercetin family, of which buckwheat is one of the richest dietary sources. It has been studied mainly in the context of capillary wall integrity, and in Europe it was used as a drug for chronic venous disease. An important caveat: the amount you get from a serving of buckwheat is significantly smaller than the medicinal dose, and roasting the groats damages some of it.

**Question: Is buckwheat dangerous for anyone?**
Answer: Yes — for people allergic to it. Buckwheat allergy can be severe and even anaphylactic, and it is especially common in places where it is eaten in quantity, such as Japan and Korea. In addition, the plant's leaves, flowers and sprouts contain fagopyrin, a compound that causes sun sensitivity — but the groats themselves do not contain it in significant amounts.

**Question: How do you cook buckwheat?**
Answer: Rinse, toast for a minute or two in a dry pan, then cook at a ratio of one cup of buckwheat to two cups of water — 12-15 minutes at a low simmer, covered, followed by 5 minutes of rest. It cooks faster than brown rice, and cooking it too long turns it into mush.

**Question: What is the difference between roasted and unroasted buckwheat?**
Answer: Roasted buckwheat (kasha) is reddish-brown with a strong, dominant earthy-nutty flavor. Unroasted buckwheat is light greenish and its flavor is much milder and more neutral. Anyone who tried buckwheat and didn't like the taste almost always tried the roasted kind, and should try the other.

**Question: Is buckwheat suitable for people with diabetes?**
Answer: It is one of the more suitable grains. Its glycemic index is relatively low (45-55), it is rich in fiber and magnesium, and small intervention studies showed improved glycemic response when refined grain was replaced with buckwheat. Cooking and cooling before eating creates resistant starch and further improves the response.
