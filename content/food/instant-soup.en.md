## Instant Cup Soup

Instant cup soup is an ultra-processed food by definition: a powder of salt, flavor enhancers, hydrogenated fat, maltodextrin and flavorings, with a marginal amount of dried vegetable pieces. It is sold as "soup," but nutritionally it is closer to **a very salty clear broth** than to a meal.

Its main problem is not calories — it is low in them — but **sodium**, and its presentation as a meal when it provides almost no protein or fiber.

### Nutrition facts — prepared serving (a cup, about 200-250 ml)

| Nutrient | Clear / vegetable soup | Soup with noodles |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 40-80 | 190-350 |
| Protein | 1-2 grams | 4-7 grams |
| Fat | 1-3 grams | 8-16 grams |
| Of which saturated | 0.5-1.5 grams | 4-8 grams |
| Carbohydrates | 6-12 grams | 25-45 grams |
| Dietary fiber | 0-1 grams | 1-2 grams |
| **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/)** | **700-1,200 mg** | **1,200-1,900 mg** |

**This is the only figure that really matters:** one cup provides **30%-50% of the entire recommended daily sodium intake** (2,300 mg), and in noodle versions sometimes more than 80%. For comparison — that is the amount of sodium in three servings of [fries](/en/food/fries/), in a small serving of [sausages](/en/food/sausages/), or in more than five slices of bread.

### What's in the ingredient list

* **Salt** — usually the first or second ingredient.
* **Flavor enhancer (MSG, E621) and ribonucleotides (E627, E631)** — not as dangerous as commonly thought; the evidence for "Chinese restaurant syndrome" has not been replicated in controlled studies. The relevant point is a different one: they create a sense of depth of flavor that the product itself does not provide.
* **Palm oil or hydrogenated vegetable fat** — the source of the saturated fat, mainly in the noodle versions.
* **Maltodextrin** — a processed carbohydrate with a higher glycemic index than sugar.
* **Dried vegetables** — usually less than 2% of the product.
* **Preservatives and antioxidants** (TBHQ, BHA) in some of the fried noodles.

**The noodles in "ramen" versions are deep-fried in oil before drying** — that is the source of most of the fat and calories there.

### Benefits

* **Real convenience:** a minute of boiling water, no dishes and no refrigeration. There are situations where this is what you have — at work, on a hike, when you are sick.
* **Low in calories (in the clear version):** 40-80 calories per serving. A hot, salty liquid does provide a temporary feeling of fullness.
* **Long shelf life** and low cost.
* **Fluids and electrolytes:** in the context of illness with fever, vomiting or diarrhea, the sodium it provides is actually an advantage rather than a disadvantage.

### Downsides

* **Sodium — the main problem.** The link between high sodium intake and high blood pressure and cardiovascular risk is one of the best established in nutrition. A daily serving of instant soup adds about a third of the allowance without registering as "I ate something salty."
* **Almost no protein or fiber** in the clear version — so the fullness passes in less than an hour, and often leads to more eating.
* **Low in nutrients:** dried vegetables at a few percent of the product do not provide meaningful amounts of vitamins.
* **An ultra-processed food:** large cohort studies link high intake of ultra-processed food to higher mortality and cardiometabolic disease, beyond the effect of any single ingredient. See [ultra-processed foods](/en/food/ultra-processed-foods/).
* **Saturated fat in the noodle versions:** 4-8 grams per serving, most of it from palm oil.
* **Misleading presentation:** the package shows fresh vegetables and homemade soup. The product inside is not that.

### How to turn it into a reasonable meal

This is the practical approach — not giving it up, but using it as a base:

1. **Use only half a packet.** The effect on flavor is much smaller than the effect on sodium, which immediately drops by 50%.
2. **Add protein:** an [egg](/en/food/eggs/) beaten into the boiling water, a spoonful of cubed [tofu](/en/food/tofu/), cooked [lentils](/en/food/lentil/) or canned [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/). This is the difference between a drink and a meal.
3. **Add vegetables:** ready-to-use frozen ones, or fresh [spinach](/en/food/spinach/) that wilts in seconds.
4. **Add a good fat:** a teaspoon of [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) or half an [avocado](/en/food/avocado/) — slows absorption and adds fullness.
5. **Choose reduced-sodium versions** — the gaps between brands are as much as twofold.
6. **A homemade alternative:** [bone broth](/en/food/bone-broth/) or homemade stock frozen in cubes — a minute in the microwave, and without the sodium.

### Summary

Instant cup soup provides 40-80 calories and 700-1,200 mg of sodium — that is, up to half the daily allowance — with almost no protein or fiber. It is not a meal, and when treated as one it leaves you hungry within an hour. As an occasional emergency solution it does no real harm; as a daily habit it contributes a significant share of your sodium intake. The most significant upgrade is simple: **half a packet, plus an egg and vegetables**.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much sodium is in instant cup soup?**
Answer: 700-1,200 mg in clear soup, and 1,200-1,900 mg in noodle versions — that is, 30%-80% of the recommended daily intake (2,300 mg) in a single cup. This is the main problematic figure in the product.

**Question: Is instant cup soup filling?**
Answer: Only briefly. The clear version provides 1-2 grams of protein and less than a gram of fiber, so the fullness passes in less than an hour. Adding a beaten egg, lentils or tofu turns it into a meal that lasts.

**Question: Is the MSG in instant soup harmful?**
Answer: The evidence does not support this. "Chinese restaurant syndrome" has not been replicated in double-blind controlled studies, and safety authorities classify MSG as safe. The problem with instant soup is not the MSG but the sodium, the saturated fat in the noodle versions, and the lack of nutritional value.

**Question: How can you improve instant cup soup?**
Answer: Use only half a packet (immediately cuts sodium by 50%), add a protein source — a beaten egg, tofu, lentils or chickpeas — and add frozen vegetables or fresh spinach. A teaspoon of olive oil adds fullness. This turns a salty drink into a meal.

**Question: How many calories are in instant soup?**
Answer: 40-80 calories in clear or vegetable soup, and 190-350 calories in noodle versions. The difference comes from the noodles being deep-fried in oil before drying.
