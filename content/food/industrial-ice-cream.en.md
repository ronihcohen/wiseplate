## Commercial Ice Cream

Ice cream is one of the only products sold **by volume and eaten by weight** — and the difference between the two is the most important thing to understand about it. Two one-liter tubs, side by side on the same shelf, can contain 400 grams of ice cream or 700 grams of ice cream. **The difference is air.**

This is neither a scam nor a secret — it is the property that makes ice cream ice cream. But it completely changes the meaning of the numbers on the label, and what you are actually buying.

### Nutrition facts per 100 grams (typical commercial vanilla ice cream)

| Nutrient | Amount | % daily value |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 207 | — |
| Protein | 3.5 grams | 7% |
| Carbohydrates | 23.6 grams | — |
| **Of which sugars** | **21.2 grams** | — |
| Fat | 11.0 grams | — |
| **Of which saturated fat** | **6.8 grams** | **34%** |
| [Calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) | 128 mg | 10% |
| Cholesterol | 44 mg | — |
| [Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) | 80 mg | 3% |

**21 grams of sugar per 100 grams** is about five teaspoons. The recommended maximum of added sugar for an adult is about 25-50 grams a day — meaning **a medium serving of ice cream can use up most of it**.

### The air in the tub — "overrun"

In ice cream production, air is whipped into the mix while it freezes. The measure is called **overrun**, and it is the percentage of volume added:

* **100% overrun** — the mix has doubled in volume. Half the tub, by volume, is air. This is the standard for cheap ice creams.
* **20-40% overrun** — premium ice creams and gelato. High density, high weight, more concentrated flavor.

**What this means in practice:**

| | Cheap tub (1 liter) | Premium tub (0.5 liter) |
|---|---|---|
| Weight | ~450 grams | ~450 grams |
| Calories per 100 ml | ~95 | ~190 |
| Calories per tub | ~930 | ~855 |

The large tub looks like twice as much ice cream, but in fact contains a very similar amount. **A label that states values "per 100 ml" will always look better for an ice cream with a lot of air** — not because it is healthier, but because there is less of it.

**What is worth knowing:** air is not bad. Ice cream with high overrun is simply "diluted" — and in the amount a person eats by the spoonful, that does in practice mean fewer calories. The problem starts when you compare by percentages on the label rather than by the spoonful.

### "Ice cream", "frozen dessert" and "parve" — not the same thing

Standards in Israel and Europe distinguish between categories, and the differences are significant:

* **Dairy ice cream** — based on milk fat. Its saturated fat comes from the milk.
* **Frozen dessert / frozen dairy product** — the milk fat content is lower than required, and is sometimes replaced with vegetable fat.
* **Parve ice cream** (non-dairy under kosher rules) — dairy-free, and based mostly on **coconut fat or palm fat**. These are vegetable fats, but **highly saturated** — sometimes more saturated than milk fat. "Parve" and "plant-based" are not synonyms for "healthy," and this is a common mistake.

### Benefits

* **[Calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) and protein** in dairy-based versions: 128 mg of calcium and 3.5 grams of protein per 100 grams. Not a reason to eat ice cream, but not zero.
* **A cultural and social role.** Dessert is not a nutritional failure — it is part of life. A diet with no room for occasional enjoyment tends to fail in the long run.
* **An easy portion to measure.** Unlike snacks out of a bag, ice cream is eaten in scoops and cones — units that are easy to count.
* **High-protein versions** (based on [Greek yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/) or [whey protein](/en/food/supplements/whey-protein/)) offer 8-12 grams of protein and less sugar per 100 grams.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Sugar density.** 21 grams per 100 grams, almost all of it added. A medium 150-gram serving is about 32 grams of sugar — above the entire daily recommendation.
* **Saturated fat.** 6.8 grams per 100 grams, 34% of the recommended daily intake. A medium serving reaches about 50%.
* **Cold hides the sweetness.** Sweet taste is perceived as weaker at low temperatures, so ice cream contains more sugar than the palate detects. Melted ice cream mix tastes so sweet it is almost impossible to drink.
* **The "serving" on the label.** The label usually shows 100 ml or "one scoop" (65 ml). A 500 ml individual tub looks reasonable until you multiply by 7.5.
* **Additives.** Stabilizers (guar, carob, carrageenan) and emulsifiers (mono- and diglycerides) are approved ingredients and usually not a concern; alongside them, cheap versions contain food colorings and artificial flavors. A product with a one-line ingredient list is almost always better.
* **Toppings are a serving in their own right.** Chocolate sauce, cookie crumbs, wafers and syrup can double the serving without a word about them in the ice cream's nutrition table.
* **Protein and calcium are not a justification.** You can get both from [Greek yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/) at 59 calories per 100 grams, instead of 207.

### Practical use

* **Always compare per 100 grams, not per 100 ml.** It is the only comparison that cancels out the air.
* **Buy an individual cup instead of a tub.** A pre-portioned serving sidesteps the self-control question entirely.
* **Put it in a small bowl and return the tub to the freezer** — don't eat from the tub in front of a screen.
* **Read the ingredient list.** [Milk](/en/food/milk/), cream, sugar, eggs, vanilla — the shorter, the better.
* **"Parve" is not "light."** Parve ice creams are usually based on coconut or palm fat, and are highly saturated. Choose them only if the reason is kashrut or allergy.
* **A homemade alternative that works:** frozen [banana](/en/food/banana/) blended in a food processor with cocoa or [peanut butter](/en/food/peanut-butter/) — creamy texture, no added sugar.
* **A high-protein version:** frozen [Greek yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/) with [berries](/en/food/blueberries/) — about 70 calories per 100 grams and 8 grams of protein.
* **Time it.** Dessert after a balanced meal creates a much milder glycemic load than ice cream on an empty stomach.

### Summary

Commercial ice cream is a dessert: 207 calories, 21 grams of sugar and 6.8 grams of saturated fat per 100 grams. That is no problem when it is eaten as an occasional dessert, and it is a problem when it is eaten out of the tub several times a week. Two practical things are worth more than any general recommendation: **compare products by weight, not by volume** — because half the cheap tub is air; and **don't confuse "parve" with "healthy"** — the plant-based versions are usually based on coconut or palm fat, which are among the most saturated fats there are.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in commercial ice cream?**
Answer: A typical vanilla ice cream provides about 207 calories per 100 grams, with 21.2 grams of sugar and 6.8 grams of saturated fat. A medium 150-gram serving is about 310 calories and 32 grams of sugar — above the entire daily recommendation for added sugar. Chocolate ice creams and ones loaded with mix-ins come out higher.

**Question: Why do two ice cream tubs of the same volume weigh different amounts?**
Answer: Because of the air whipped into the mix during production, a measure called overrun. In cheap ice creams it can reach 100% — meaning half the tub, by volume, is air. In premium ice creams and gelato it is only 20-40%. So a cheap one-liter tub and a premium half-liter tub may contain a similar amount of ice cream.

**Question: Is parve (non-dairy) ice cream healthier than dairy ice cream?**
Answer: Usually not. Parve ice creams are mostly based on coconut fat or palm fat, which are vegetable fats but highly saturated — sometimes more saturated than milk fat. They are also low in protein and calcium. "Parve" is a consideration of kashrut or allergy, not of health.

**Question: How do you compare ice creams at the supermarket?**
Answer: Always compare per 100 grams and not per 100 ml. A label given by volume will always look better for an ice cream with more air — not because it is healthier, but because there is less of it. It is also worth reading the ingredient list and preferring a short one.

**Question: Why does ice cream contain so much sugar?**
Answer: Partly because cold suppresses the perception of sweetness. The same mix at room temperature would taste so sweet it would be almost impossible to drink, but frozen it is perceived as balanced. Sugar also plays a technical role — it lowers the freezing point and keeps the ice cream from turning into a hard block of ice.

**Question: What are the stabilizers and emulsifiers in ice cream?**
Answer: Stabilizers (guar, carob, carrageenan) and emulsifiers (mono- and diglycerides) prevent ice crystals from forming and keep the texture smooth, especially after thawing and refreezing. They are approved ingredients and not the main concern with ice cream — sugar and saturated fat are. Still, a short ingredient list usually indicates a better-quality product.

**Question: Is there a healthy alternative to ice cream?**
Answer: Two that work. Frozen banana blended in a food processor takes on a surprisingly creamy texture without any added sugar, and you can add cocoa or peanut butter. Alternatively, frozen Greek yogurt with berries provides about 70 calories and 8 grams of protein per 100 grams, compared with 207 calories and 3.5 grams of protein in ice cream.

**Question: How much ice cream is OK to eat?**
Answer: There is no magic number, but a useful frame: a 100-150 gram serving, as dessert after a meal, a few times a month — fits easily into a balanced diet. The problem is not the serving but the frequency and the manner: a tub eaten in front of a screen is easily 900 calories and 90 grams of sugar.
