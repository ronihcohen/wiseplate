## Artificial Sweeteners

**Artificial (non-sugar) sweeteners such as aspartame, sucralose and saccharin are 200–700 times sweeter than sugar and add almost no calories, but the evidence that they help with long-term weight loss is weak.** In 2023 the World Health Organization advised against using them as a weight-control tool — and yet swapping a sugar-sweetened drink for a diet one does cut calories in practice. In Hebrew they are called "ממתיקים מלאכותיים".

They are found in "diet" and "zero" drinks, yogurts, chewing gum, protein bars and coffee sachets. The interesting question is not whether they are poison — they have been through many safety assessments — but what they do to appetite, blood sugar and gut bacteria.

### Common sweeteners

| Sweetener | Sweetness vs. sugar | Acceptable daily intake (FDA) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aspartame | 200 times | 50 mg per kg of body weight |
| Acesulfame K | 200 times | 15 mg per kg |
| Sucralose | 600 times | 5 mg per kg |
| Saccharin | 200–700 times | 15 mg per kg |
| Steviol glycosides (stevia) | 200–400 times | 4 mg per kg (as steviol) |

[FDA — Aspartame and Other Sweeteners in Food](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food)

The joint WHO/FAO expert committee (JECFA) sets aspartame's limit at 40 mg per kg a day. For a 70 kg person that is 2,800 mg — far more than a few cans a day contain.

### Benefits

* **Less sugar and fewer calories:** a can of sugary soft drink holds about 35 g of sugar; the diet version close to none.
* **Evidence from a controlled trial:** in an 18-month trial of 641 children in the Netherlands, children given a sweetener-sweetened drink every day gained less weight and fat than children given an identical drink with sugar. [de Ruyter 2012, NEJM](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22998340/)
* **Teeth:** sweeteners do not feed the mouth bacteria that cause tooth decay.
* **People with diabetes:** they allow a sweet taste without the immediate rise in blood sugar that ordinary sugar causes.

### Downsides and open questions

* **A modest effect on weight:** a large systematic review found no differences in most health outcomes between users and non-users, and most studies were small and short. [Toews 2019, BMJ](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30602577/)
* **The WHO guideline (2023):** advises against using sweeteners for weight control, because no long-term benefit has been shown and observational studies linked long-term use to diabetes and heart disease. The recommendation is "conditional" — based on evidence that is not strong. [WHO — Use of non-sugar sweeteners](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240073616)
* **Observational studies and reverse causation:** in a meta-analysis, diet-drink consumption was linked to a higher risk of diabetes, but the link shrank sharply after accounting for body weight, and the authors found signs of bias. [Imamura 2015, BMJ](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26199070/) A plausible explanation is that people already at risk switch to diet drinks.
* **Gut microbiome:** researchers at the Weizmann Institute showed in 2014 that saccharin altered gut bacteria in mice and caused glucose intolerance. [Suez 2014, Nature](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25231862/) In a follow-up trial in 120 healthy adults, saccharin and sucralose altered the microbiome and impaired blood-sugar responses in some participants — the effect was personal and varied from person to person. [Suez 2022, Cell](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35987213/) The long-term clinical meaning is still unknown.
* **Aspartame and cancer:** in 2023 the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified aspartame as "possibly carcinogenic" (Group 2B) on limited evidence, while JECFA left the acceptable intake unchanged. [WHO — aspartame assessment](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-07-2023-aspartame-hazard-and-risk-assessment-results-released)
* **Phenylketonuria:** people with PKU must avoid aspartame, which contains phenylalanine.
* **Digestion:** sugar alcohols such as sorbitol and maltitol, common in "sugar-free" sweets, can cause gas and diarrhoea in large amounts.

### Does sweet taste raise insulin?

The claim that sweet taste alone triggers [insulin](/en/food/supplements/insulin/) release and "metabolic confusion" has been tested in many studies, and the results are inconsistent. Most found no meaningful rise in insulin after a diet drink on its own, so it should not be treated as a proven mechanism.

### Practical use

* **As a swap, not an addition:** if you drink sugar-sweetened drinks, moving to the diet version is an improvement. If you drink water, there is no reason to switch to diet.
* **Water is the goal:** a diet drink can be a stepping stone towards water, soda water or unsweetened [tea](/en/food/green-tea/).
* **Watch the sugar alcohols:** "sugar-free" sweets are not necessarily low in calories and can upset your gut.
* **Children:** it is better to get used to a less sweet taste than to swap one sweetness for another.

### Summary

Artificial sweeteners are approved and safe at normal intakes, and replacing a sugar-sweetened drink with a diet one cuts calories and sugar. But they are not a proven weight-loss tool: the WHO advises against relying on them, and the Weizmann Institute research shows they may affect the microbiome and blood sugar in some people. Water remains the best choice.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Do zero drinks make you gain weight?**
Answer: They are almost calorie-free, and a controlled trial in children found that replacing a sugar-sweetened drink with a diet one reduced weight gain. The link between diet drinks and obesity in observational studies probably reflects, in part, the fact that heavier people tend to choose them.

**Question: Do zero drinks raise insulin?**
Answer: The evidence is inconsistent. Most studies found no meaningful rise in insulin after a diet drink on its own.

**Question: Do sweeteners harm gut bacteria?**
Answer: In the Weizmann Institute trial in 120 adults, saccharin and sucralose altered the microbiome and impaired blood-sugar responses in some participants. The effect was personal, and its long-term meaning is still unknown.

**Question: Does aspartame cause cancer?**
Answer: In 2023 it was classified "possibly carcinogenic" (Group 2B) on limited evidence — the same group that includes, for example, traditional Asian pickled vegetables. The acceptable intake stayed at 40 mg per kg a day, far above normal consumption.

**Question: Is stevia better?**
Answer: Stevia comes from a plant, but there is no strong evidence that it is healthier than other sweeteners. It is also covered by the WHO guideline.

**Question: Has the WHO banned sweeteners?**
Answer: No. The guideline advises against using them as a way to control weight, and does not apply to people with diabetes who already use them. It does not say they are toxic.
