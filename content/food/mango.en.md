## Mango

{{< youtube id="ONkOjTCFWSQ" title="Mango: Too Much Sugar? (video)" loading="lazy" >}}

The mango (*Mangifera indica*) is the most consumed fruit in the world by volume — more than apples, bananas or grapes. In Hebrew it is called "מנגו". It comes from South Asia, where it has been grown for more than 4,000 years, and in India it is the national fruit. It belongs to the cashew family (Anacardiaceae), the same family as cashews and [pistachios](/en/food/pistachios/) — a fact that explains some of the downsides described below.

Nutritionally, the mango sits in an interesting place: it is unusually rich in vitamin C and carotenoids, and at the same time contains more sugar than most common fruits. Both are true at once, which is why the question "is mango healthy" can't be answered with a yes or no.

### Mango nutrition facts

Per 100 grams of fresh mango (about half a medium mango):

| Nutrient | Amount | % of daily intake |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 60 kcal | — |
| Carbohydrate | 15 g | — |
| **of which sugars** | **13.7 g** | — |
| Dietary fiber | 1.6 g | 6% |
| Protein | 0.8 g | 2% |
| Fat | 0.4 g | — |
| **Vitamin C** | **36 mg** | **40%** |
| Vitamin A (as beta-carotene) | 54 mcg RAE | 6% |
| Folate | 43 mcg | 11% |
| Vitamin E | 0.9 mg | 6% |
| Copper | 0.11 mg | 12% |
| Potassium | 168 mg | 4% |

**A whole medium mango** (about 200 grams of flesh) provides about 120 calories and about 27 grams of sugar — roughly the same as two medium bananas.

### Benefits

- **An unusual amount of vitamin C:** half a mango provides about 40% of the recommended daily intake of vitamin C — more than an orange of the same weight. The vitamin is essential for making collagen and for immune function, and it also **significantly improves the absorption of plant iron** when eaten in the same meal.
- **Carotenoids for vision and skin:** the orange color comes from beta-carotene and related compounds, which the body converts to vitamin A. Mango also contains lutein and zeaxanthin, two carotenoids that accumulate in the retina and are linked to protection against macular degeneration.
- **Mangiferin — the unique polyphenol:** mango is one of the main dietary sources of **mangiferin**, a polyphenol widely studied for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity and a possible effect on sugar and fat metabolism. Most of the research is still in the lab and in animals.
- **Digestive enzymes:** mango contains amylases — enzymes that break starches down into simple sugars. Their activity rises as the fruit ripens, which is why a ripe mango is so much sweeter. There is preliminary evidence that eating mango helps with chronic constipation, together with the fiber.
- **Soluble fiber:** the pectin in mango is a soluble fiber that contributes to fullness and slows sugar absorption, and feeds gut bacteria.

### Downsides

This is the side worth knowing before making mango a regular part of your diet.

- **High sugar content — the main downside:** 27 grams of sugar in a medium mango is a significant amount, especially for people managing diabetes, prediabetes or a low-carb diet. It is important to keep perspective: this is natural sugar that comes with fiber, water and vitamins — a mango is not the same as a sweetened drink — but the amount is not negligible.
- **Fructose and irritable bowel symptoms:** mango has excess fructose relative to glucose, so it is classed as a **high-FODMAP** food. In people with irritable bowel or fructose malabsorption, a large serving can cause bloating, gas and diarrhea. A serving of about 40 grams is usually considered tolerable.
- **Contact dermatitis (urushiol):** this is a downside that is almost never mentioned. Mango skin and sap contain **urushiol** — the same compound found in poison ivy. Sensitive people can develop an itchy rash around the mouth and on the hands after peeling or eating fruit with the skin. The fix: peel with gloves or wash well, and eat only the flesh.
- **Oral allergy syndrome:** people with a latex or pollen allergy may experience itching and mild swelling of the lips and throat after eating mango — a cross-reaction. Cooking the fruit usually prevents the reaction.
- **Dried mango and mango juice:** these are no longer the same food. In dried mango the sugar is concentrated several times over and sometimes more sugar is added; in juice the fiber has been almost completely removed, making it a sweetened drink in every respect. **The gap between the whole fruit and its products is the most meaningful nutritional difference in mango.**
- **Ripeness and potassium:** unripe mango is sour, hard to digest and can cause stomach discomfort. In addition, anyone managing kidney disease with a potassium restriction should factor in mango's contribution.

### What is the glycemic index of mango?

The glycemic index of fresh mango is about **51** — considered **low** (below 55). The glycemic index alone is misleading, because it doesn't take the amount into account. The more useful measure is the **glycemic load**: for a 100-gram serving of mango it is about 8, a low load; for a whole mango, about 16 — a medium load.

In practice: fresh mango in a reasonable amount does not cause a sharp spike in blood sugar in a healthy person, thanks to its fiber and water. Dried mango or mango juice is a completely different story.

### How much mango can you eat a day?

There is no official limit, but the practical guideline for a healthy person is **up to one medium mango a day** (about 150–200 grams of flesh), as part of the 2–3 daily servings of fruit. That amount provides the full benefit of vitamin C and carotenoids without excess sugar.

- **With diabetes or prediabetes:** stick to about 100 grams (half a mango), and eat it as part of a meal that includes protein or fat — [yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/), nuts, tahini — which moderates the glycemic response.
- **With irritable bowel:** start at about 40 grams and see how you react.
- **On a weight-loss diet:** mango is no more "fattening" than other fruit; 120 calories for a medium mango is a reasonable serving of fruit. The problem starts when it is eaten on top of a full diet rather than instead of another snack.

### Culinary use

- **Whole fruit, chilled:** the best way to eat it. Chilling brings out the sweetness.
- **Frozen in a smoothie:** frozen mango gives a smoothie a creamy texture without added sugar or ice cream. Combining it with [Greek yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/) balances the sugar with protein.
- **Salsa:** chopped mango with red onion, cilantro, lemon and chili — an excellent side for fish and chicken that uses the acidity to cut through fat.
- **Green (unripe) mango:** in Southeast Asia it is eaten salty and spicy as a salad. Its sugar content is much lower and its vitamin C content is actually higher.
- **Avoid:** mango canned in syrup, sweetened dried mango and concentrated mango juices.

### Summary

Mango is a genuinely nutritious fruit — half a mango provides 40% of the daily intake of vitamin C, alongside carotenoids, folate and mangiferin. The main downside is a relatively high sugar content (27 grams in a medium mango), which matters for people managing diabetes or counting carbs. Less known but relevant downsides: a high FODMAP content that bothers people with irritable bowel, and urushiol in the skin that can cause a rash on contact.

The practical bottom line: one medium mango a day as whole fruit is an excellent choice; mango juice and sweetened dried mango are a completely different food, and those are worth limiting.

## Frequently asked questions

### Question: What are the downsides of mango?
Answer: The main downside is a high sugar content — about 27 grams in a medium mango — which needs to be taken into account by people managing diabetes or a low-carb diet. In addition, mango is high in FODMAPs and can cause bloating and gas in people with irritable bowel; its skin contains urushiol, which can cause an itchy rash in sensitive people; and it can trigger oral allergy syndrome in people allergic to latex or pollen.

### Question: Does mango make you fat?
Answer: No more than other fruit. A medium mango provides about 120 calories, similar to two small bananas or a large apple. Mango does not cause weight gain when it replaces another snack; it does contribute to a calorie surplus when it is added on top of a full diet. Dried mango, on the other hand, is very calorie-dense and it is very easy to eat a lot of it.

### Question: How much mango can you eat a day?
Answer: For a healthy person — up to one medium mango a day (about 150–200 grams), as part of the daily fruit allowance. People managing diabetes are advised to stick to half a mango and eat it in a meal with protein or fat. People with irritable bowel should start at about 40 grams.

### Question: Is mango suitable for people with diabetes?
Answer: Yes, in a controlled amount. The glycemic index of fresh mango is about 51 — low — and the glycemic load of a 100-gram serving is only about 8. Combining it with protein or fat moderates the response further. Mango juice and dried mango should be avoided.

### Question: Which vitamins are in mango?
Answer: Mango stands out especially for vitamin C — about 36 mg per 100 grams, which is 40% of the daily intake. It also provides vitamin A as beta-carotene, folate (11%), vitamin E, vitamin B6 and copper (12%). It also contains lutein and zeaxanthin, which are important for eye health, and the polyphenol mangiferin.

### Question: Why does my mouth itch after eating mango?
Answer: There are two possibilities. The first is urushiol — a compound in mango skin and sap, from the same chemical family as poison ivy, that causes a contact rash. The second is oral allergy syndrome, a cross-reaction in people with a latex or pollen allergy. In both cases, peel with gloves, wash the flesh well and avoid contact with the skin.

### Question: How much sugar is in a mango?
Answer: About 13.7 grams of sugar per 100 grams of fresh mango, so about 27 grams in a whole medium mango (about 200 grams of flesh). That is more than most common fruits, but the sugar comes together with fiber, vitamin C and carotenoids, unlike mango juice or sweetened dried mango.
