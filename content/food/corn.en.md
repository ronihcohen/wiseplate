## Corn / Maize (*Zea mays*)

Corn (in Hebrew "תירס") is one of the most important crops in human history, and it is also one of the hardest foods to talk about in general terms — because "corn" is really four different things: **sweet corn**, eaten as a vegetable; **field corn**, for industry and animal feed; **popcorn** (a separate variety with an especially hard hull); and **processed derivatives** such as starch and [corn syrup](/en/food/corn-syrup/).

The question "is corn healthy?" is meaningless without specifying which of them you mean. A boiled corn cob and a drink made with corn syrup are not the same food in any sense, even though both are "corn".

And corn hides one of the most instructive stories in the history of nutrition: **nixtamalization** — the reason some cultures that lived on corn thrived, while others fell ill with a deadly deficiency disease.

**A medium corn cob** (about 90 grams of kernels) is about 77 calories — a small serving of carbohydrate, not a free vegetable.

### Nixtamalization — the story worth knowing

Corn contains **a reasonable amount of niacin (vitamin B3), but in a bound form** — "niacytin" — that the body cannot absorb. Mesoamerican cultures discovered a process thousands of years ago that solves this: soaking the kernels in an alkaline solution (limewater or ash) and cooking them. The process is called **nixtamalization**, and it:

* **releases the niacin** into an absorbable form;
* **improves the availability of lysine and tryptophan**, and with it the protein quality;
* **adds [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/)** from the lime itself;
* softens the hull and allows the kernel to be ground into a dough (masa) — the basis for tortillas and tamales.

When corn left the Americas in the 16th century, it was taken **without the process**. In regions of Europe, Africa and the southern US where corn became the almost exclusive staple food, **pellagra** broke out — a niacin-deficiency disease characterized by dermatitis, diarrhea, dementia and death. Tens of thousands of people died of it in the US alone in the early 20th century, until the dietary cause was identified.

**The practical conclusion:** a real Mexican tortilla made from **masa** is not nutritionally equivalent to regular cornmeal. And as long as corn is part of a varied diet rather than an exclusive staple food — pellagra is not a practical consideration today.

### Lutein and zeaxanthin — a real distinction

Yellow corn is **the only grain that contains a significant amount of lutein and zeaxanthin** — carotenoids that accumulate in the macula at the center of the retina and filter blue light. In observational studies their intake is linked to a lower risk of age-related macular degeneration.

Like all carotenoids, they are fat-soluble: **corn needs to be eaten with a source of fat** — [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/), [avocado](/en/food/avocado/), a little butter — for them to be absorbed. White corn does not contain them at all.

### Benefits

* **Fiber in popcorn:** 14.5 grams per 100 grams — one of the most fiber-rich snacks there is, when it is air-popped rather than popped in oil.
* **Lutein and zeaxanthin** — as detailed above.
* **[Vitamin B1](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b1/) and [folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/)** in reasonable amounts.
* **[Gluten-free](/en/food/gluten/)** — suitable for celiac disease, subject to cross-contamination.
* **Resistant starch:** corn that has been cooked and cooled develops resistant starch, which serves as food for gut bacteria.
* **[Magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) in popcorn:** 144 mg per 100 grams — 34% of the daily value.
* **Available, cheap and keeps well** — frozen, canned or on the cob, all year round.

### Downsides and considerations

* **The processed derivatives are the problem, not the vegetable.** [High-fructose corn syrup](/en/food/corn-syrup/) is found in [sweetened soft drinks](/en/food/sugary-soft-drinks/), [sweetened breakfast cereals](/en/food/sweetened-cereals/) and [sweetened ketchup](/en/food/sweetened-ketchup/), and cornstarch is a basic ingredient in [ultra-processed food](/en/food/ultra-processed-foods/). Confusing these with corn on the cob mainly serves the industry.
* **A carbohydrate serving, not a free vegetable.** 18.7 grams of carbohydrates per 100 grams — three times as much as [cauliflower](/en/food/cauliflower/) or [broccoli](/en/food/broccoli/). In a salad it counts as a carbohydrate.
* **Sweetened canned corn.** Many products contain added sugar and [sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) ("Whole kernel corn, sweetened"). Look for "no added sugar or salt", or rinse in a strainer.
* **Movie theater and microwave popcorn.** Air-popped popcorn is about 387 calories per 100 grams; popcorn with butter and oil reaches 500 and above, with high [sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/). Microwave packs add fats and additives.
* **Whole kernels in the stool.** A common phenomenon that causes needless worry. The outer hull of the corn kernel is made of **cellulose**, which the body has no enzyme to break down. What's inside — the starch, protein and fat — is digested and absorbed. **Thorough chewing reduces the phenomenon, and it is not a sign of a digestive problem.**
* **GMO.** Most of the world's field corn is genetically engineered and used for animal feed and industry; sweet corn for direct consumption is mostly not engineered. The position of the major health bodies is that the approved varieties are safe to eat. Those who prefer to avoid them can choose organic products.
* **Allergy and sensitivity** to corn exist but are uncommon.

### Practical use

* **Eat it with fat.** A little [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) or butter on the cob is not a sin but a condition for absorbing the lutein.
* **Roast rather than boil.** Grilling or oven-roasting concentrates sugars and caramelizes them; long boiling draws water-soluble vitamins out into the water.
* **Fresh corn — cook it quickly.** As with [peas](/en/food/peas/), the sugars in corn start turning into starch right after harvest. A cob picked today is much sweeter than a two-day-old cob. **Frozen corn, blanched and frozen close to harvest, is usually better than "fresh" corn that has traveled.**
* **Homemade stovetop popcorn:** a tablespoon of oil, a lid, medium heat — about 100 calories for a large 30-gram serving. Much less than ready-made packs. Alternatively, [amaranth popcorn](/en/food/amaranth-popcorn/) provides more protein for the same volume.
* **Count it as a carbohydrate** on the plate, not as a vegetable.
* **When buying canned:** read the ingredients — corn, water, and maybe salt. Sugar on the list is a reason to choose something else.
* **Rinse canned corn** in a strainer — it removes a considerable part of the sodium and sugar in the liquid.

### Summary

Corn itself — the cob, the frozen kernels, air-popped popcorn — is a reasonable to good food: fiber, B vitamins, and a unique status as the only grain source of lutein and zeaxanthin. What gave corn its bad reputation is **what the industry makes from it**: high-fructose syrup and refined starch. Two practical things to remember: **eat it with fat** so the carotenoids are absorbed, and **count it as a carbohydrate, not a vegetable**. And whole kernels in your stool? That is the cellulose hull, and it is completely normal.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Is corn a vegetable or a carbohydrate?**
Answer: Botanically it is a cereal grain, and nutritionally it behaves like a carbohydrate — 18.7 grams per 100 grams, three times as much as broccoli or cauliflower. On the plate it is right to count it as a carbohydrate serving and not as a free vegetable. A medium cob is about 77 calories.

**Question: Why do you see whole corn kernels in your stool?**
Answer: Because the outer hull of the kernel is made of cellulose, and the human body has no enzyme that breaks it down. What is inside the kernel — the starch, protein and fat — is fully digested and absorbed. This is a completely normal phenomenon and does not indicate a digestive problem; thorough chewing reduces it.

**Question: What is nixtamalization?**
Answer: An ancient Mesoamerican process in which corn kernels are soaked and cooked in an alkaline solution of limewater or ash. It releases the niacin in corn from a bound form that is not absorbed, improves the availability of lysine and tryptophan, and adds calcium. When corn left the Americas without the process, populations that lived on it as a staple food fell ill with pellagra — a niacin-deficiency disease.

**Question: Is canned corn healthy?**
Answer: It depends on the product. Canned corn that contains only corn, water and salt is a reasonable choice. Many products on the market are sweetened with added sugar and contain high sodium. It is worth reading the ingredient list and rinsing the corn in a strainer before use — this removes a considerable part of the sodium and sugar.

**Question: Is popcorn healthy?**
Answer: Air-popped popcorn is one of the best snacks there is — 14.5 grams of fiber per 100 grams and 144 mg of magnesium. The problem is everything added to it: popcorn with butter or oil exceeds 500 calories per 100 grams, and microwave packs contain fats and additives. A serving of homemade stovetop popcorn with a tablespoon of oil is about 100 calories.

**Question: Is frozen corn worse than fresh?**
Answer: It is usually actually better. The sugars in corn start turning into starch right after harvest, and frozen corn is blanched and frozen within hours — so it is sweeter and more nutritious than a "fresh" cob that traveled and sat for two days. The exception is corn picked the same day.

**Question: What is the difference between corn and corn syrup?**
Answer: A fundamental difference. Corn is a whole food with fiber, vitamins and carotenoids. High-fructose corn syrup is a refined sweetener produced from corn starch by an enzymatic process, and it contains no fiber or micronutrients. It is a major ingredient in sweetened drinks and ultra-processed food, and the bad reputation of "corn" comes almost entirely from it.

**Question: Why is it important to eat corn with fat?**
Answer: Because the lutein and zeaxanthin in yellow corn are fat-soluble carotenoids. Without fat in the meal they are absorbed at a low rate. A little olive oil, butter or avocado with the corn significantly increases absorption. White corn, by the way, does not contain them at all.
