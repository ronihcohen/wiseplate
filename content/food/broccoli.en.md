## Broccoli (*Brassica oleracea*)

Broccoli (in Hebrew "ברוקולי") is one of the most nutrient-dense vegetables there is: 100 grams provide almost the full daily intake of [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) and 85% of [vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) — in just 34 calories.

But the component that makes it more researched than any other vegetable is not a vitamin: it is **sulforaphane**. And here lies the most important practical point on this page — **the most common cooking method in Israel destroys it almost completely**.

### Sulforaphane — and the mistake almost everyone makes

Broccoli does not contain sulforaphane. It contains two **separate** things, located in different cells:

1. **Glucoraphanin** — a stable, inactive sulfur compound.
2. **Myrosinase** — an enzyme that turns glucoraphanin into sulforaphane.

They only meet when the cell structure breaks — that is, when you cut, chew or crush. **And myrosinase is very sensitive to heat: it is already deactivated at around 60-70 degrees.**

Hence the problem: broccoli thrown into a boiling pot or stir-fried on high heat loses the enzyme before the reaction has time to happen. Glucoraphanin remains, and almost no sulforaphane is formed.

**Four practical ways to solve this:**

* **Cut it and wait 40 minutes before cooking.** Cutting starts the reaction while it is still cold; by the time the heat arrives, the sulforaphane has already formed, and it is itself heat-stable. This is the most effective step, and it costs nothing.
* **Steam for just 3-4 minutes**, don't boil. Brief steaming keeps the internal temperature low enough and preserves some of the enzyme, and it also prevents [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) from leaching into the water.
* **Add an external source of myrosinase** — a pinch of mustard powder, [sesame](/en/food/sesame-seeds/), radish or arugula on the cooked broccoli. These contain the enzyme in active form and "bring back" the reaction. A documented method that is especially effective for frozen broccoli.
* **Eat some of it raw** — in a salad or as a dip.

**Important to know about frozen broccoli:** The blanching process before freezing deactivates myrosinase almost completely. Frozen broccoli retains vitamins well, but produces almost no sulforaphane — unless you add an external enzyme source as described above.

**And why this matters at all:** Sulforaphane activates the Nrf2 pathway, which in turn activates phase 2 detoxification and antioxidant enzymes. It has been studied in the context of protection against cancer, inflammation and cardiometabolic function. The evidence in the lab and in animals is strong; in human studies it is promising but still early.

### Benefits

* **Almost a full day's [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) in 34 calories:** 89 mg per 100 grams — more than an [orange](/en/food/orange/) (53 mg). Anyone who thinks of vitamin C as belonging to citrus fruits is missing the better source.
* **[Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/):** 85% of daily intake, important for clotting and for directing [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) to bone.
* **Fiber:** 2.6 grams per 100 grams, combined with water — contributes to satiety and bowel regularity.
* **[Folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/):** 16% of daily intake, significant in pregnancy.
* **Exceptional nutrient density:** Its nutrient-to-calorie ratio is among the highest of any vegetable.
* **Sulforaphane and glucosinolates**, as detailed above.
* **Lutein and zeaxanthin** for eye health.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Gas and bloating.** Broccoli contains raffinose and fructans — carbohydrates that are not digested in the small intestine and are fermented in the colon. This is the physiological reason for gas, and it is not a sign of a problem. **The stalks are richer in fructans than the florets**, so sensitive people can use only the florets. Longer cooking reduces the effect.
* **FODMAP:** For this reason broccoli is limited on a low-FODMAP diet, but **florets in amounts of up to 75 grams are considered low-FODMAP** — the stalk is the problematic part.
* **Goitrogens and the thyroid.** Glucosinolates may interfere with [iodine](/en/food/supplements/iodine/) uptake by the thyroid. It is very important to keep this in proportion: the effect is significant only with a combination of **existing iodine deficiency** and **very large amounts of raw cruciferous vegetables**. Cooking reduces it significantly, and for anyone with adequate iodine intake it is not a practical consideration.
* **[Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) and anticoagulants.** People taking Coumadin do not need to avoid broccoli — they need to keep their intake **steady and consistent**, so the drug dose can be calibrated to it. Sharp fluctuations are the problem, not the vegetable.
* **Loss of vitamin C when boiled:** Prolonged boiling can remove over 50% of the vitamin, which spills into the cooking water. Steaming preserves most of it.
* **Pesticide residues:** The dense florets make washing difficult. A short soak in water with a little baking soda followed by rinsing is more effective than rinsing alone.
* **Bitter taste for some people:** A genetic sensitivity to sulfur compounds (the TAS2R38 gene). Roasting in the oven with [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) sweetens them through caramelization and noticeably reduces the bitterness.

### Practical use

* **Cut and wait 40 minutes** before cooking — the step with the biggest impact.
* **Steam for 3-4 minutes** until bright green. Broccoli that has turned olive green is overcooked.
* **Roast in the oven at 220°C (428°F)** with olive oil and garlic — the best approach for anyone put off by the taste.
* **Don't throw away the stalk.** It is edible, sweeter than the florets, and excellent peeled and sliced or grated into a salad.
* **A pinch of mustard powder** on the finished broccoli — especially if it is frozen.
* **Combine with [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/)** — it both improves absorption of the carotenoids and softens the taste.

### Summary

Broccoli provides almost 100% of vitamin C and 85% of vitamin K in 34 calories per 100 grams — one of the highest nutrient densities among vegetables. But the one thing really worth taking away from here is how to cook it: **cut it, wait 40 minutes, and steam for just 3-4 minutes.** Broccoli boiled until soft is still a good vegetable, but it has lost most of what makes it special. And for those who buy frozen — a pinch of mustard powder at the end restores what blanching before freezing removed.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What are the nutrition facts of broccoli?**
Answer: 100 grams provide 34 calories, 2.8 grams of protein, 2.6 grams of fiber, 89 mg of vitamin C (99% of daily intake) and 102 mcg of vitamin K (85%). This is one of the best nutrient-to-calorie ratios of any vegetable.

**Question: How do you cook broccoli without losing its nutritional value?**
Answer: Cut it and wait 40 minutes before cooking, then steam for just 3-4 minutes. The wait lets the enzyme myrosinase produce sulforaphane while it is still cold, and the brief steaming prevents both the deactivation of the enzyme and the leaching of vitamin C into the cooking water. Prolonged boiling removes over 50% of the vitamin C.

**Question: What is sulforaphane and why is it important?**
Answer: A compound formed in broccoli when the enzyme myrosinase meets glucoraphanin — that is, only when you cut or chew it. It activates the Nrf2 pathway, which activates detoxification and antioxidant enzymes, and has been studied in the context of protection against cancer and inflammation. The lab evidence is strong; in humans it is still early.

**Question: Is frozen broccoli as good as fresh?**
Answer: In terms of vitamins — yes, sometimes even more so, because it is frozen soon after harvest. But the blanching process before freezing deactivates the enzyme myrosinase, so it produces almost no sulforaphane. The solution: a pinch of mustard powder, sesame or radish on the finished broccoli brings back the enzyme.

**Question: Why does broccoli cause gas?**
Answer: It contains raffinose and fructans — carbohydrates that are not digested in the small intestine and are fermented by bacteria in the colon. The stalks are richer in them than the florets, so sensitive people can use only the florets. Longer cooking reduces the effect.

**Question: Is broccoli bad for the thyroid?**
Answer: Almost always not. Its glucosinolates may interfere with iodine uptake, but the effect is significant only with a combination of existing iodine deficiency and very large intake of raw cruciferous vegetables. Cooking reduces it significantly, and for anyone whose iodine intake is adequate it is not a practical consideration.

**Question: Can you eat the broccoli stalk?**
Answer: Yes, and it is even sweeter than the florets. It is best to peel off the tough outer layer and slice it or grate it into a salad. The only caveat is that it is richer in fructans, so it is less suitable for people with IBS.

**Question: How much vitamin C is in broccoli compared with an orange?**
Answer: More. Broccoli provides 89 mg per 100 grams compared with about 53 mg in an orange — almost twice as much. The caveat is that broccoli is eaten cooked, and cooking damages vitamin C; brief steaming preserves most of it.
