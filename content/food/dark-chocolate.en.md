## Dark Chocolate

Dark chocolate (in Hebrew "שוקולד מריר") is made from fermented, roasted and ground [cacao beans](/en/food/cacao-nibs/), with added sugar and sometimes cocoa butter — and without the [milk](/en/food/milk/) solids that characterize [milk chocolate](/en/food/milk-chocolate/). The cocoa percentage on the wrapper describes the combined weight of cocoa solids and cocoa butter, that is, the part that isn't sugar.

The components behind the research interest are **flavanols** — a group of [polyphenols](/en/food/polyphenols/) led by epicatechin. Their concentration in raw cacao beans is very high, but this is where the field's central problem lies: the industrial process destroys most of them.

### The cocoa percentage is misleading — the point worth understanding

The common assumption is that 85% cocoa guarantees more flavanols than 70%. This is only partly true, and often not at all.

The flavanol content of finished chocolate depends much more on the **processing method** than on the cocoa percentage:

* **Fermenting and roasting** the beans breaks down a considerable part of the flavanols.
* **Alkalization (Dutch processing)** — treatment with an alkali to reduce acidity and achieve a dark color and mild flavor — destroys up to 80-90% of the flavanols. An especially dark, "mild" chocolate may actually be low in them.

The result: two products labeled "70% cocoa" can differ several-fold in their actual flavanol content, and there is no way to tell from the wrapper. Most successful intervention studies used standardized cocoa extracts with a known amount of flavanols — not a chocolate bar off the shelf. This is the central gap between the research and the shelf.

Practical tip: if the ingredient list says "cocoa processed with alkali" or "alkalized cocoa" — the flavanol content is low.

### Research-based health benefits

* **Blood pressure — the best-established benefit:** a Cochrane review of dozens of controlled trials found that flavanol-rich cocoa products lower blood pressure by about 2 mmHg on average. This is a small but statistically significant drop, and meaningful at the population level. The mechanism: epicatechin increases the availability of nitric oxide (NO) in the endothelium, which relaxes the smooth muscle in the blood vessel wall and widens the vessels.

* **Endothelial function:** improvement in FMD (flow-mediated dilation) — an accepted measure of blood vessel wall health — is one of the most consistent findings in cocoa trials.

* **Insulin sensitivity:** several trials show a modest improvement in [insulin](/en/food/supplements/insulin/) sensitivity with flavanol-rich cocoa, although the evidence is weaker than for blood pressure — and in sweetened chocolate the effect is partly offset by the sugar content.

* **[Magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) and minerals:** 70-85% dark chocolate is a significant source of magnesium (about 230 mg per 100 grams, more than 50% of the daily intake), [iron](/en/food/iron/), [copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/) and [manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/). Keep in mind that the iron here is non-heme, and its absorption is lower.

* **Theobromine:** an alkaloid in the caffeine family, found in chocolate in larger amounts than caffeine itself (about 800 mg per 100 grams of dark chocolate). It acts as a mild stimulant and bronchodilator, and its effect is experienced as a smoother, longer-lasting alertness than that of [caffeine](/en/food/supplements/caffeine/).

* **Mood:** chocolate contains anandamide, phenylethylamine and tryptophan — compounds with neurological activity. The amounts are too low to explain the perceived effect, which most likely comes mainly from the sensory experience (cocoa butter melting at body temperature) and reward mechanisms.

### Downsides and health considerations

* **Calorie density and sugar:** 100 grams of 70-85% dark chocolate provide about 600 calories and 43 grams of fat. Dark chocolate also contains sugar — about 24 grams per 100 grams at 70%, less at higher concentrations. The amounts studied are 20-30 grams a day, not a whole bar.

* **Heavy metals — cadmium and lead:** this is the most significant and least known downside. The cacao plant absorbs cadmium from the soil, and cacao beans — especially from South American sources — may contain it in non-negligible concentrations. Lead usually comes from contamination after harvest. Independent tests found that a considerable share of dark chocolate products on the market exceed the exposure thresholds of the State of California (Prop 65), which are especially strict. The European Union has set limits for cadmium in chocolate. **The practical meaning:** a daily intake of 20-30 grams is not a concern for a healthy adult, but large, regular intake — and especially in children — warrants moderation and rotating between brands.

* **[Caffeine](/en/food/supplements/caffeine/) and theobromine:** 100 grams of dark chocolate contain about 80 mg of caffeine — like a cup of [coffee](/en/food/coffee/). In sensitive people, eating it in the evening may disrupt sleep.

* **Oxalates:** the oxalate content of cocoa is relatively high, a consideration for people prone to calcium-oxalate kidney stones.

* **Migraine:** chocolate is often mentioned as a migraine trigger, although the controlled evidence for this is mixed and inconclusive.

* **Tannins and [iron](/en/food/iron/) absorption:** the [tannins](/en/food/tannins/) in cocoa may inhibit the absorption of non-heme iron from a meal. Don't eat chocolate together with a meal meant to provide iron.

### Practical use

**How much:** the amounts tested in studies are 20-30 grams a day — about two squares. This is also the reasonable balance between flavanols on one side and calories and heavy metals on the other.

**What to look for:** 70% cocoa and above; a short ingredient list (cocoa solids, cocoa butter, sugar, lecithin, vanilla); **avoiding "cocoa processed with alkali"**; and no hydrogenated vegetable fat other than cocoa butter.

**When:** because of the caffeine and theobromine, it is better during the day. Combined with coffee in the evening, it can add up to a significant stimulant dose.

**Dark chocolate vs. cocoa:** if the goal is flavanols rather than enjoyment, [cocoa powder](/en/food/raw-cacao-powder/) that hasn't been alkalized provides them at a much higher density and without the sugar and fat.

### Summary

Dark chocolate has a real research basis — mainly in lowering blood pressure and improving endothelial function — but the effect is modest in size and depends on flavanols that many products on the market barely contain. The gap between "flavanol-rich cocoa in research" and "a chocolate bar off the shelf" is the most important point to understand. At 20-30 grams a day, from good-quality chocolate that hasn't been alkalized, this is a rare case of an enjoyable food that can also be justified nutritionally — but not a "superfood", and not a reason to start eating chocolate.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much dark chocolate is OK per day?**
Answer: 20-30 grams, about two squares. This is the amount tested in most studies, and it also balances the benefits against the calories (about 600 per 100 grams) and cadmium exposure.

**Question: Is a higher cocoa percentage always better?**
Answer: Not necessarily. A higher percentage means less sugar, but the flavanol content depends mainly on the processing method. Chocolate that has been alkalized loses up to 90% of its flavanols even at a high cocoa percentage. Check the ingredient list for "cocoa processed with alkali".

**Question: Is it true that chocolate contains heavy metals?**
Answer: Yes. The cacao plant absorbs cadmium from the soil, and tests found non-negligible levels in some products. At 20-30 grams a day this is not a concern for a healthy adult, but it is worth rotating between brands and being careful with high intake and with children.

**Question: Does dark chocolate raise blood sugar?**
Answer: Less than milk chocolate, thanks to less sugar and more fat and fiber that slow absorption. 85% chocolate contains a relatively small amount of sugar. It is still a calorie-dense food that needs to be counted.

**Question: Does dark chocolate contain caffeine?**
Answer: Yes, about 80 mg per 100 grams — similar to a cup of coffee. It also contains a larger amount of theobromine, a mild, long-acting stimulant. Sensitive people may prefer to avoid it in the evening.
