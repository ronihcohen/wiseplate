## Oats

**In Hebrew, oats are called "שיבולת שועל".** In Israel many people also call them **"קוואקר" ("Quaker")** — a name that took root from the American brand Quaker Oats and became generic. "Quaker" is not a different kind of grain but ordinary rolled oats; the distinction that really matters is not the name but the **degree of processing**, discussed below, which has a major effect on the glycemic index.

Oats (*Avena sativa*) are among the most thoroughly researched grains. Unlike most grains, they are almost always eaten in their whole form — the bran and germ are not removed in normal processing — so they keep their fiber and active compounds even in industrial products.

100 grams of dry oats provide about 389 calories, 66 grams of carbohydrates, 10.6 grams of fiber, 17 grams of protein and 7 grams of fat. A typical serving (40-50 grams dry) provides about 160-190 calories and 4-5 grams of fiber.

### Beta-glucan — the key component

About 4-6% of oats is **beta-glucan**, a soluble fiber that forms a viscous solution in the small intestine. The viscosity is the mechanism: the gel binds bile acids and prevents their reabsorption, so the liver has to draw cholesterol from the blood to make new ones — and LDL goes down.

This is one of a small number of food components that have earned an **officially approved health claim**. The FDA and EFSA have both approved stating that oat beta-glucan contributes to lowering cholesterol, at a threshold of **3 grams per day** — an amount reached with about 60-80 grams of dry oats, or about two servings.

Meta-analyses of controlled trials show a consistent 5-10% drop in LDL with regular intake, without harming HDL.

### Avenanthramides — unique to oats

Polyphenolic compounds found **almost exclusively in oats** in the food world. They are being studied for anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity and for effects on endothelial function.

This is also why colloidal oatmeal appears in skincare products for atopic skin — avenanthramides have a recognized local soothing effect, and colloidal oatmeal is approved by the FDA as a skin protectant.

### Gluten — a point that confuses many people

Oats **do not naturally contain gluten**. [Gluten](/en/food/gluten/) is the name of the protein network formed from the prolamins of wheat, rye and barley only; the protein in oats is called avenin, and it is well tolerated by the vast majority of people with celiac disease.

The problem is not the grain but **cross-contamination**: oats are usually grown in fields next to wheat, harvested with the same machinery, transported in the same trucks and processed in the same plants. Measurements have found significant levels of gluten in regular oats.

**The practical conclusion:** people with celiac disease must eat only oats explicitly labeled **"gluten-free"** — that is, oats grown and processed in a separate, controlled supply chain. A small minority of people with celiac disease also react to avenin itself, so the usual recommendation is to introduce them gradually and with monitoring.

### The forms — and their effect on the glycemic index

This is an important practical fact that many people are unaware of. The more mechanical processing, the more exposed the starch is to enzymes and the faster it is absorbed:

| Form | Description | Estimated GI |
|---|---|---|
| Whole grain (groats) | The entire kernel | ~55 |
| Steel-cut | The kernel cut into 2-3 pieces | ~52-55 |
| Regular rolled | Steamed and flattened | ~57-59 |
| Quick / fine | Rolled thinner | ~65-70 |
| Instant | Precooked and dried | ~79-83 |

The difference between steel-cut oats and an "instant" packet is a real difference in glycemic response — even though it is the same grain. Flavored instant packets also add a significant amount of sugar on top of that.

### More health benefits

* **Satiety:** the viscosity slows stomach emptying. Trials have found that oatmeal porridge provides more satiety than ready-to-eat [breakfast cereals](/en/food/sweetened-cereals/) with the same calories.

* **Glycemic regulation:** the gel slows glucose absorption and lowers the post-meal blood sugar peak.

* **Resistant starch:** cooling after cooking turns some of the starch resistant to digestion, so it acts like fiber and feeds gut bacteria. This is the advantage of **overnight oats** — beyond convenience, they have a better glycemic profile than freshly made hot porridge.

* **High protein for a grain:** 17 grams per 100 grams — among the highest of the grains.

* **Minerals:** a good source of [manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/), [phosphorus](/en/food/supplements/phosphorus/), [magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) and non-heme iron.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Gluten contamination:** as detailed above — the critical consideration for people with celiac disease.

* **Sweetened oat products:** flavored "instant" packets, granola and oat-based breakfast cereals usually contain a considerable amount of sugar. Commercial granola may contain 20-30% sugar and be high in fat — it is a completely different product from porridge.

* **Phytic acid:** reduces absorption of [iron](/en/food/iron/) and [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/). Soaking (for example, in overnight oats) partially reduces it.

* **FODMAPs in large amounts:** well tolerated in normal amounts, but large servings may cause symptoms in sensitive people.

* **A sudden jump in fiber:** a sudden increase in soluble fiber intake causes gas and bloating. Increase gradually and drink water.

* **[Oat milk](/en/compare/soy-almond-oat-milk/) is not oats:** the manufacturing process breaks the starch down enzymatically into simple sugars, and most of the fiber is filtered out. Its glycemic index is relatively high and its protein low.

### Practical use

**For cholesterol:** about 70 grams dry per day (two servings) provides the 3 grams of beta-glucan set as the threshold in the health claim.

**For blood sugar control:** prefer steel-cut or regular rolled oats over instant. Add protein and fat — [walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/), [tahini](/en/food/tahini/), yogurt or an [egg](/en/food/eggs/) — which slow absorption further.

**Overnight oats:** 40 grams of oats, 100-150 ml of liquid, in the fridge overnight. Creates resistant starch, reduces phytate and saves cooking.

**Avoid flavored packets:** use plain oats and add fresh fruit, [cinnamon](/en/food/cinnamon/) or a little [honey](/en/food/honey/) — full control over the amount of sugar.

**For celiac disease:** make sure of an explicit "gluten-free" label, and introduce gradually.

### Summary

Oats are one of the few foods with an officially approved health claim — 3 grams of beta-glucan per day to lower cholesterol — alongside avenanthramides, which are almost entirely unique to them. Two practical things change the outcome more than anything else: **the form** (steel-cut or rolled, not instant — a gap of up to 30 points in glycemic index), and avoiding sweetened products, which are a completely different food. For people with celiac disease — the grain itself is gluten-free, but you must choose an explicitly labeled product because of widespread cross-contamination.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Do oats contain gluten?**
Answer: Not naturally — their protein is called avenin and most people with celiac disease tolerate it well. The problem is widespread cross-contamination in the fields, at harvest and in processing alongside wheat. People with celiac disease must choose a product explicitly labeled "gluten-free".

**Question: Which oats are the healthiest?**
Answer: Steel-cut or regular rolled. The more mechanical processing, the faster the starch is absorbed — the glycemic index rises from about 55 for steel-cut to about 79-83 for instant.

**Question: How much oats do you need to lower cholesterol?**
Answer: 3 grams of beta-glucan per day — about 70 grams of dry oats, roughly two servings. This is the threshold set in the approved health claim of the FDA and EFSA.

**Question: Are overnight oats healthier than hot porridge?**
Answer: They have an advantage. Cooling creates resistant starch that acts as fiber and feeds gut bacteria, and soaking reduces phytic acid and improves mineral absorption.

**Question: Is granola the same as oats?**
Answer: No. Commercial granola usually contains 20-30% sugar and added fat, and it is a completely different product nutritionally — even though the base is the same.

**Question: Does oat milk provide the same benefits?**
Answer: No. The manufacturing process breaks the starch down enzymatically into simple sugars and filters out most of the fiber, so its glycemic index is relatively high and its protein and beta-glucan content low.
