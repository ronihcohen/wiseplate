## Commercial Mayonnaise

Mayonnaise (in Hebrew "מיונז") is an **emulsion** — a stable mixture of oil in water, held together by the lecithin in egg yolk. In terms of composition, that is almost the whole story: **about 75-80% of mayonnaise's weight is vegetable oil.** Everything else — egg, vinegar, salt and a pinch of mustard — is less than a quarter.

That is why it has 680 calories per 100 grams, and it is also why it turns any dish it goes into an entirely different dish calorie-wise.

By the way, mayonnaise has a reputation as the main culprit in picnic food poisoning — **a reputation it doesn't deserve**, and we'll explain why below.

### Nutrition facts per 100 grams

| Component | Regular mayonnaise | "Light" mayonnaise |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 680 | 230-330 |
| Fat | 75 g | 22-33 g |
| Of which saturated | 11.8 g (59%) | 3-5 g |
| Of which polyunsaturated | ~41 g | ~12 g |
| Of which monounsaturated | ~18 g | ~6 g |
| Protein | 1.0 g | 0.5 g |
| Carbohydrates | 0.6 g | 8-14 g |
| [Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) | 635 mg (28%) | 700-900 mg |
| Cholesterol | 42 mg | 20 mg |

**In practical terms:** a tablespoon of mayonnaise (14 grams) is **about 95 calories and 10.3 grams of fat**. Two tablespoons in a tuna salad add almost 200 calories — more than the tuna itself.

### "Light" — what actually replaces the oil

When you remove 60% of the oil, you create a gap that has to be filled. In reduced-fat mayonnaise it is filled with **water, modified starches, gums (guar, xanthan) and sugar** — and sometimes more salt, to make up for the lost flavor.

The result is mixed:
* **Calories** — a real and significant improvement, sometimes threefold.
* **Sugar** — light mayonnaise usually contains more sugar and carbohydrates than regular mayonnaise (8-14 grams vs. 0.6).
* **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/)** — often higher.
* **The ingredient list** — much longer.

**The practical conclusion:** if the goal is calories, "light" works. But it is usually better to simply **use less regular mayonnaise** than to switch to a more processed product with sugar and thickeners.

### The fat — less dramatic than people think

The oil in commercial mayonnaise is usually soybean or canola oil, meaning it is **rich in linoleic acid ([omega-6](/en/food/supplements/omega-3/))**. The argument that this fat is "inflammatory" is common online, but the research picture is more complex:

* Large systematic reviews found that replacing saturated fat with linoleic acid **lowers LDL** and improves cardiometabolic risk markers.
* The claim that linoleic acid raises inflammation markers in humans **was not supported** in controlled trials.
* What does deserve attention is the **ratio** of omega-6 to omega-3 in the Western diet — and the fix is to **add [omega-3](/en/food/supplements/omega-3/)** ([salmon](/en/food/salmon/), [sardines](/en/food/sardines/), [flax seeds](/en/food/flax-seeds/)), not necessarily to cut omega-6.

**The real downside of mayonnaise is not the type of fat but the amount:** 680 calories per 100 grams, at a density that is very easy not to notice.

### The myth: mayonnaise and food poisoning

"Don't take a salad with mayonnaise to a picnic" is a rule of thumb almost everyone knows, and it is **the opposite of reality**.

**Commercial** mayonnaise is made from **pasteurized eggs** and is **very acidic** — a pH of around 3.6-4.0, thanks to the vinegar and lemon juice. Such an acidic environment **inhibits** bacterial growth, including Salmonella. In lab studies, adding commercial mayonnaise to a protein salad slowed bacterial growth rather than speeding it up.

**So what does cause poisoning from such salads?** The ingredients the mayonnaise is mixed with — **cooked chicken, hard-boiled eggs, [potatoes](/en/food/baked-potato/), tuna** — and above all **time and temperature**. A salad that has sat in the sun for four hours is dangerous whether or not it contains mayonnaise.

**The important exception:** **homemade mayonnaise** is made from **raw, unpasteurized egg yolk**, and it does pose a Salmonella risk — especially for pregnant women, children, the elderly and the immunocompromised. They should use pasteurized eggs or commercial mayonnaise.

### Benefits

* **Texture and flavor.** Mayonnaise plays a real technological role — it binds salads, adds moisture to dry dishes and improves mouthfeel.
* **[Vitamin E](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-e/) and [vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/)** from the vegetable oil, in reasonable amounts.
* **Fat that aids absorption.** Carotenoids in a vegetable salad — lutein, lycopene, beta-carotene — are fat-soluble, and a tablespoon of mayonnaise increases their absorption significantly. This is true of any fat source, but it is true of mayonnaise too.
* **A reasonable fat profile.** Mostly unsaturated, with only 11.8 grams of saturated fat per 100 grams — less than butter or hard [margarine](/en/food/margarine/).
* **Microbial safety** in the commercial version — as detailed above.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Extreme calorie density.** 680 calories per 100 grams — among the highest of any food product. The problem is that it is spread, not measured.
* **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/).** 635 mg per 100 grams, and sometimes more in light versions.
* **"Mayonnaise" that isn't mayonnaise.** In Israel and Europe there is a minimum oil and egg-yolk requirement for a product to be called "mayonnaise". Cheap products are sold as "mayonnaise sauce" or "mayonnaise-flavored spread" and contain less oil and more starch, sugar and stabilizers.
* **Added sugar.** Present in some brands, and in almost all "light" versions.
* **Homemade mayonnaise and Salmonella** — as detailed above.
* **Shelf life after opening.** An opened jar keeps in the fridge for about two months; homemade mayonnaise — only 3-4 days.

### Practical use

* **Measure, don't spread.** A teaspoon (5 grams) is 34 calories; a tablespoon (14 grams) is 95. When you scoop from the jar by eye, the real number is usually double.
* **Cut it by half.** Half mayonnaise and half [Greek yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/) in a tuna or egg salad — almost the same texture, about half the calories, and a protein bonus.
* **Alternatives that work:** diluted raw [tahini](/en/food/tahini/) (adds [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) and fiber), mashed [avocado](/en/food/avocado/), 5% white cheese, or mustard — which is almost calorie-free.
* **Read the name on the jar.** "Mayonnaise", not "mayonnaise-flavored sauce".
* **Check for sugar in the ingredient list** — especially in reduced-fat versions.
* **Picnic safety:** the real rule is **temperature and time**, not mayonnaise. A salad containing protein should not stay out of the fridge for more than two hours (and only one hour on a hot day).
* **Homemade mayonnaise:** pasteurized eggs, immediate refrigeration, use within 3-4 days.
* **Make use of the fat:** a tablespoon of mayonnaise in a colorful vegetable salad is not just flavor — it improves carotenoid absorption.

### Summary

Commercial mayonnaise is, for practical purposes, **oil in a jar**: 75% of its weight and 680 calories per 100 grams. The type of fat is not the problem — it is mostly unsaturated, and the fear of "inflammatory omega-6" is not well supported by controlled trials. The problem is **the amount spread without measuring**, and the simplest fix is a measured teaspoon or diluting it with yogurt. Finally, a myth worth letting go of: **mayonnaise is not the culprit in picnic food poisoning** — it is acidic and pasteurized, and the real culprits are the chicken, the egg and the potato that sat in the sun for four hours.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in mayonnaise?**
Answer: 680 calories per 100 grams — among the densest of food products. In practical terms: a teaspoon (5 grams) is 34 calories, and a tablespoon (14 grams) is about 95 calories and 10.3 grams of fat. Two tablespoons in a tuna salad add almost 200 calories, more than the tuna itself.

**Question: Is light mayonnaise really better?**
Answer: In calories — yes, sometimes three times fewer. But the oil that was removed is replaced with water, starches, thickeners and sugar, so light mayonnaise usually contains more carbohydrates and sugar (8-14 grams vs. 0.6) and sometimes more sodium. In many cases it is better to simply use less regular mayonnaise than to switch to a more processed product.

**Question: Does mayonnaise cause food poisoning at picnics?**
Answer: That is a myth. Commercial mayonnaise is made from pasteurized eggs and is very acidic (pH 3.6-4.0), so it actually inhibits bacterial growth. The real culprits in salad poisoning are the ingredients it is mixed with — chicken, hard-boiled eggs, potatoes, tuna — together with a long time at high temperature. The right rule is not to leave a protein salad out of the fridge for more than two hours.

**Question: Is homemade mayonnaise safe?**
Answer: Less so than commercial, because it contains raw, unpasteurized egg yolk, so there is a risk of Salmonella. For pregnant women, young children, the elderly and the immunocompromised, this is a real risk. The solution is to use pasteurized eggs, refrigerate immediately and eat it within 3-4 days.

**Question: Is the fat in mayonnaise harmful?**
Answer: Less than people think. It is mostly unsaturated fat from soybean or canola oil, with only 11.8 grams of saturated fat per 100 grams — less than butter. The claim that linoleic acid (omega-6) causes inflammation was not supported in controlled human trials, and reviews found that replacing saturated fat with it lowers LDL. The problem with mayonnaise is the amount, not the type.

**Question: What can you use instead of mayonnaise?**
Answer: Several alternatives work well — Greek yogurt (adds protein and cuts about half the calories when mixed half-and-half), diluted raw tahini (adds calcium and fiber), mashed avocado, 5% white cheese, or mustard, which is almost calorie-free. In a tuna or egg salad, a half-and-half mix with yogurt is barely noticeable.

**Question: What is the difference between "mayonnaise" and "mayonnaise sauce"?**
Answer: A regulatory difference. For a product to be called "mayonnaise", a minimum proportion of oil and egg yolk is required. Products that don't meet it are sold as "mayonnaise sauce" or "mayonnaise-flavored spread", and contain less oil and egg and more water, starch, sugar and stabilizers. It is worth reading the product name, not just the brand.

**Question: How long does mayonnaise keep after opening?**
Answer: Commercial mayonnaise keeps in the fridge for about two months after opening, thanks to its acidity and pasteurization — subject to the date on the package. Homemade mayonnaise is a completely different story: only 3-4 days in the fridge, because it contains raw yolk and no preservatives.
