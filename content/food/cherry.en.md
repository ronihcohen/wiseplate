## Cherry

**100 g of sweet cherries provide about 63 calories, 13 g of sugar and 2 g of fiber. Most research has used tart (Montmorency) cherries, usually as concentrated juice: there is moderate evidence for a small improvement in sleep and in muscle recovery after hard exercise, and an observational link with fewer gout attacks. These are modest effects, not treatments.**

The cherry (in Hebrew "דובדבן") comes in two main types: **sweet cherries** (*Prunus avium*, such as Bing), large, dark and sweet, eaten fresh; and **tart cherries** (*Prunus cerasus*, such as Montmorency and Morello), small and sour, sold mostly frozen, dried or as juice. The difference matters, because almost all the studies on sleep, recovery and gout used tart cherries or cherries in general, not a small handful of fresh fruit.

### Nutrition facts

Per 100 g of raw, pitted fruit. A cup of sweet cherries (about 150 g) provides about 95 calories and 19 g of sugar. Values are rounded.

Concentrated tart cherry juice is a different product: it contains the pigments of a large amount of fruit, but also its sugar, and without most of the fiber.

### Benefits

* **Sleep: a small effect from tart juice.** In a double-blind controlled trial, 20 healthy adults drank tart cherry juice concentrate or a placebo for 7 days. On the juice, urinary melatonin rose, and total sleep time and sleep efficiency increased. [Howatson et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22038497/). In a pilot trial of 15 older adults with insomnia, the juice reduced time awake during the night compared with placebo, but did not shorten the time to fall asleep or lengthen total sleep. [Pigeon et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20438325/). These are small, short studies; tart cherry is not a substitute for treating insomnia.

* **Recovery after hard exercise.** A meta-analysis of 14 studies found that tart cherry supplementation slightly reduced muscle soreness after strenuous exercise and improved recovery of strength and power, for example jump height. [Hill et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33440334/). This mostly involved concentrated juice or powder taken for several days before and after unusual exertion, not a handful of cherries.

* **Gout.** In a study of 633 people with gout followed for a year, eating cherries in the two days before a period was associated with a 35% lower risk of an attack. Combining cherries with allopurinol was associated with a 75% lower risk. [Zhang et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23023818/). This is an observational study; cherries do not replace drug treatment for gout.

* **Anthocyanins.** The dark color comes from anthocyanins, the same pigments studied in blueberries. In the test tube they inhibit inflammatory enzymes, but that does not mean cherries act like a painkiller in the body.

* **A tasty seasonal fruit with few calories.** A cup of cherries is a dessert of about 95 calories, with fiber and potassium.

### Downsides

* **Sugar in sweet cherries and juice.** A cup of sweet cherries contains about 19 g of sugar, and concentrated juice contains far more per serving. People with diabetes should count the carbohydrate and prefer fruit to juice.

* **Short season and price.** In Israel fresh cherries are available mainly around May to July. Out of season, frozen unsweetened cherries are a good alternative.

* **Digestive issues.** Cherries contain sorbitol and fructose. A large portion can cause gas or diarrhea, especially in people with irritable bowel syndrome.

* **Pits.** The pits contain amygdalin, which releases cyanide if a pit is cracked and chewed. Swallowing a whole pit by accident is not dangerous, but do not crush pits. For small children the pit is also a choking hazard.

* **Medications.** There is no evidence of a meaningful interaction between cherries and common medications, but anyone on gout medication should keep taking it rather than replacing it with juice.

### Practical use

* **Fresh in season:** wash only right before eating, and keep them unwashed in the fridge.
* **Frozen:** keeps the pigments well; great in smoothies, yogurt and sauces.
* **Tart cherry juice:** studies usually used diluted concentrate or juice, twice a day, for several days. Choose juice with no added sugar and treat it as a sugary drink.
* **Dried:** concentrated in sugar and sometimes sweetened; a small handful is enough.
* **In cooking:** tart cherries suit sauces for meat, cakes and homemade jam with little sugar and a squeeze of [lemon](/en/food/lemon/).
* **Pitting:** a cherry pitter, or a chopstick pushed through the stem end, speeds up preparation for baking and keeps children safe from the pits.

### Summary

The cherry is a tasty seasonal fruit with few calories. The interesting research was done mainly on tart cherries and concentrated juice, and points to a small improvement in sleep and in recovery after exertion, and to an observational link with fewer gout attacks. The evidence is encouraging but limited: cherries are not a sleeping pill and not a gout drug.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in cherries?**
Answer: About 63 calories per 100 g of sweet cherries and about 50 for tart cherries. A cup of sweet cherries (about 150 g) provides about 95 calories.

**Question: Does tart cherry juice help you fall asleep?**
Answer: In small trials it slightly lengthened sleep and improved sleep efficiency. The effect is modest and the studies were short. It is not a substitute for treating insomnia.

**Question: Do cherries help with gout?**
Answer: An observational study found that eating cherries was linked to fewer attacks, especially together with allopurinol. Do not stop drug treatment; you can include cherries in your diet.

**Question: Are frozen cherries as good as fresh?**
Answer: Yes. Freezing keeps the pigments and fiber well, and frozen cherries are available all year.

**Question: Can people with diabetes eat cherries?**
Answer: Yes, in a measured portion of whole fruit, as part of carbohydrate counting. Juice and dried cherries raise blood sugar faster.

**Question: What happens if you swallow a cherry pit?**
Answer: A whole pit swallowed by accident usually passes through the digestive tract without harm. The risk lies in crushed or chewed pits in quantity.

**Question: Does cherry juice help runners?**
Answer: A meta-analysis found a small reduction in muscle soreness and better recovery of strength after hard exercise. That may be useful around a race or an unusually hard session, less so as a daily habit.
