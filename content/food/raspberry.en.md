## Raspberry

The raspberry (*Rubus idaeus*) is a berry from the rose family. Botanically it is not a single fruit but **a cluster of tiny fruits** (drupelets) attached to one another around a central cavity — which explains both its hollow shape when picked and its poor durability. In Hebrew it is called "פטל" (petel).

Its most striking nutritional figure is an exceptional ratio: 100 grams of raspberries provide about 52 calories, **6.5 grams of dietary fiber** and only **4.4 grams of sugar**. This is one of the best fiber-to-sugar ratios in the fruit world — almost 1.5 grams of fiber for every gram of sugar.

By comparison, in a [banana](/en/food/banana/) the ratio is completely reversed (12 grams of sugar versus 2.6 grams of fiber), and in [blueberries](/en/food/blueberries/) it is about 1:4 in favor of sugar.

### Health benefits

* **Exceptional fiber density for a fruit:** 6.5 grams per 100 grams — more than double most fruits. A cup of raspberries provides about 8 grams of fiber, about a third of the recommended daily intake. Most of it is insoluble and concentrated in the tiny seeds, contributing to bulk and bowel movement.

* **Especially low glycemic load:** thanks to its low sugar and high fiber content, the raspberry is one of the most suitable fruits for carb-restricted diets and for people managing blood sugar levels. Its glycemic index is low (about 25-32).

* **Ellagitannins and urolithins:** raspberries are among the richest sources of ellagitannins, alongside [pomegranate](/en/food/pomegranate/) and [walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/). Gut bacteria convert them into **urolithins** — metabolites with anti-inflammatory activity that are being studied in the context of mitochondrial health. An interesting point: not everyone produces urolithins to the same extent — it depends on the makeup of the individual microbiome, which explains variation in response between study participants.

* **Anthocyanins:** the red-purple pigments, with antioxidant activity. In black raspberries their concentration is significantly higher than in red ones.

* **[Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/):** 26 mg per 100 grams — about a third of the daily value in a cup.

* **[Manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/):** 100 grams provide about 30% of the daily value — an especially high amount for a fruit.

* **Low calorie density:** 52 calories per 100 grams with lots of volume and chewing — an effective combination for fullness as part of weight management.

### Raspberry ketones — a myth worth knowing about

"Raspberry ketone" is the aromatic compound responsible for the fruit's characteristic scent. For years it was marketed as a weight-loss supplement, after lab studies in fat cells and rodents showed an effect on fat breakdown.

There are two problems with the claim, and both are decisive:

1. **There is not a single controlled trial in humans** that has shown weight loss from raspberry ketones alone.
2. The doses that had an effect in rodents are orders of magnitude higher than any amount possible from the fruit. To consume the amount used in those experiments you would need **hundreds of kilograms** of raspberries a day. Commercial supplements usually contain synthetic ketone, not an extract from the fruit.

Eating raspberries is beneficial — but not because of the ketones, and not as a means of losing weight.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Especially short shelf life:** this is the main practical downside. The delicate hollow structure and high moisture make raspberries extremely prone to mold (mainly *Botrytis*), and they often spoil within 1-2 days. **Do not wash before storing** — moisture speeds up mold. Wash only right before eating.

* **Price:** one of the most expensive fruits, mainly because of delicate hand-picking and transport difficulties.

* **Seeds:** the tiny seeds get stuck between the teeth and bother some people. People with inflammatory bowel disease sometimes report discomfort from seeded fruits during flare-ups.

* **FODMAPs in large servings:** in small amounts raspberries are well tolerated on a low-FODMAP diet, but large servings may cause symptoms in sensitive people.

* **Allergic reactions:** rare, but they exist — usually in the context of oral allergy syndrome in people sensitive to pollen.

* **Processed products:** jams, syrups and "raspberry-flavored" baked goods usually contain a lot of sugar and little fruit, and sometimes only flavoring. The advantage of the fiber-to-sugar ratio — which is the whole story here — is completely destroyed by processing.

### Practical use

**Frozen is an excellent choice:** as with [blueberries](/en/food/blueberries/), freezing immediately after picking preserves the nutrients well — sometimes better than "fresh" fruit that has spent days in transit. Given the short shelf life and the price, frozen raspberries are usually the more practical and economical choice.

**Storage:** in the fridge, in an open or ventilated container, **unwashed**, preferably on paper towel. Wash only before eating, gently and in cold water.

**Checking when buying:** look at the bottom of the package — moisture stains or mush indicate fruit that has started to spoil.

**Pairings:** in yogurt, in [oatmeal](/en/food/oatmeal/), in a smoothie, or simply on their own. Thanks to their tartness and low sugar they also work well in savory dishes and salads.

**Black raspberries:** richer in anthocyanins than red ones, with a similar nutritional profile on the other measures.

### Summary

The raspberry is probably the fruit with the best fiber-to-sugar ratio — 6.5 grams of fiber against only 4.4 grams of sugar — and that is its real, clear advantage, especially for people managing blood sugar. It also provides ellagitannins, vitamin C and manganese in good amounts. On the other hand, "raspberry ketones" as a slimming supplement are a myth with no basis in humans. The only significant practical downside is the short shelf life — which is why the frozen version is usually the smart choice.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Are raspberries suitable for people with diabetes?**
Answer: Very. They contain only 4.4 grams of sugar per 100 grams alongside 6.5 grams of fiber, and their glycemic index is low (25-32). They are one of the most suitable fruits for managing blood sugar levels.

**Question: Do raspberry ketones help you lose weight?**
Answer: No. No controlled trial in humans has shown it, and the doses that had an effect in rodents are equivalent to hundreds of kilograms of raspberries a day. The supplements usually contain synthetic ketone.

**Question: Why do my raspberries go moldy so quickly?**
Answer: Their delicate hollow structure and high moisture content make them especially vulnerable. The important rule: **don't wash before storing** — moisture speeds up mold. Store them in the fridge in a ventilated container and wash only before eating.

**Question: Frozen or fresh raspberries?**
Answer: Frozen is usually the practical choice. They are frozen within hours of picking and preserve the nutrients well, are significantly cheaper and available all year — while fresh raspberries are expensive and spoil within a day or two.

**Question: How much fiber is in raspberries?**
Answer: 6.5 grams per 100 grams — more than double most fruits. A cup of raspberries provides about 8 grams, about a third of the recommended daily intake.

**Question: Raspberries or blueberries?**
Answer: Raspberries have a much better fiber-to-sugar ratio (6.5 versus 2.4 grams of fiber, 4.4 versus 10 grams of sugar), so they are better for managing blood sugar. [Blueberries](/en/food/blueberries/) have a unique research base in the area of cognition. Both are excellent.
