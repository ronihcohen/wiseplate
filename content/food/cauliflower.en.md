## Cauliflower (*Brassica oleracea* var. *botrytis*)

Cauliflower (in Hebrew "כרובית") is a vegetable from the cruciferous family — the same family as [broccoli](/en/food/broccoli/), [cabbage](/en/food/cabbage/), [Brussels sprouts](/en/food/brussels-sprouts/) and [kale](/en/food/kale/). At 25 calories per 100 grams it provides more than half the daily intake of [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/), making it one of the most efficient vegetables in terms of vitamins per calorie.

In the past decade it has become the star of the low-carb world — "cauliflower rice," "cauliflower mash," "cauliflower dough." Some of these uses are entirely justified, and some, in their commercial versions, sell something completely different from what's written on the package. On this page we'll separate the two.

### Why it's white — and what that means nutritionally

Cauliflower is not naturally lacking in chlorophyll. The head of the cauliflower (the "curd") is an arrested inflorescence that grows shaded by the large leaves covering it — in some varieties the leaves wrap around it on their own, and in others the farmer ties them over it. Without light there is no chlorophyll production, hence the white color.

The nutritional implication: **cauliflower is very low in carotenoids** compared with its green relatives. It has almost no [vitamin A](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-a/), lutein or zeaxanthin, and its [vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) is also six times lower than broccoli's. On vitamin C, by contrast, it almost matches it. Colored varieties — purple cauliflower (anthocyanins), orange (beta-carotene) and green — close part of the gap.

Like other cruciferous vegetables, cauliflower contains **glucosinolates** and the enzyme **myrosinase**, which turns them into active isothiocyanates. As in broccoli, the enzyme is heat-sensitive and is deactivated at around 60-70 degrees: **cutting and waiting 30-40 minutes before cooking, and steaming briefly instead of boiling, preserves much of the reaction.** Cauliflower's glucoraphanin content is lower than broccoli's, so it is no substitute for it in this respect — but it is not zero either.

### As a carb substitute — how much is it really worth

| Serving | Calories | Carbohydrates |
|---|---|---|
| 100 grams cooked white [rice](/en/food/rice/) | 130 | 28 g |
| 100 grams cauliflower rice (cauliflower only) | 25 | 5 g |
| 100 grams mashed [potatoes](/en/food/baked-potato/) | ~90 | 15 g |
| 100 grams cauliflower mash (no butter) | ~30 | 5 g |

The savings are real and significant. But two caveats:

1. **It's not really "the same thing."** Rice provides available energy; cauliflower doesn't. For people who train, are pregnant or are in a sharp calorie deficit — a blanket substitution may leave an energy gap.
2. **Commercial products are not cauliflower.** Frozen "cauliflower pizza dough" usually also contains [corn](/en/food/corn/) starch or [rice](/en/food/rice/) flour, cheese, oil and salt — and sometimes cauliflower is a minority ingredient. Some products reach 250 calories per 100 grams, i.e. ten times the vegetable they're named after. **Read the ingredient list in order, not the product name.**

### Benefits

* **[Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) in 25 calories:** 54% of daily intake per 100 grams — more than an [orange](/en/food/orange/), at a fifth of the calories.
* **[Folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/):** 14% of daily intake, especially relevant in pregnancy.
* **[Choline](/en/food/supplements/choline/):** 44 mg per 100 grams — one of the few vegetables that contribute to a nutrient most of the population consumes less of than recommended.
* **Very low energy density:** 92% water and 2 grams of fiber — volume and fiber without calories, very useful for weight control.
* **Glucosinolates and isothiocyanates:** Sulfur compounds from the cruciferous family, studied in the context of detoxification and inflammation pathways. The evidence in humans is still early.
* **Exceptional culinary flexibility:** It takes on the flavor of whatever it's cooked with, so it works roasted, mashed and raw.

### Downsides and considerations

* **High FODMAP — the real reason for bloating.** Cauliflower is rich in **mannitol**, a sugar alcohol that is only partially absorbed. That, and not just "the cruciferous family," is what causes gas and bloating in sensitive people. Unlike broccoli, whose florets are considered low-FODMAP in small amounts, cauliflower is restricted on a low-FODMAP diet in almost any amount.
* **Loss of vitamin C when boiled.** The vitamin is water-soluble and heat-sensitive; prolonged boiling can remove over half of it, which goes down the sink. Steaming or roasting preserves most of it.
* **The smell.** During prolonged cooking, sulfur compounds break down and release mercaptans — the source of the characteristic smell. Shorter cooking, in an uncovered pot, reduces it significantly.
* **Goitrogens and the thyroid.** Glucosinolates may interfere with [iodine](/en/food/supplements/iodine/) uptake. As with other cruciferous vegetables, the effect is practical only with a combination of existing iodine deficiency and very large intake of the raw vegetable. Cooking reduces it, and for anyone with adequate iodine intake it is not a consideration.
* **Processed "cauliflower" products.** As detailed above — nuggets, doughs and frozen mixes may be [ultra-processed food](/en/food/ultra-processed-foods/) in every sense.
* **Low in carotenoids.** Anyone looking for [vitamin A](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-a/) or lutein needs [pumpkin](/en/food/pumpkin/), [carrot](/en/food/carrot/) or leafy greens — not cauliflower.

### Practical use

* **Roast in the oven at 220°C (428°F)** with [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/), salt and garlic, until browned at the edges. Caramelization turns it into a completely different vegetable, and anyone who doesn't like cooked cauliflower should try this before giving up.
* **Cut and wait 30-40 minutes** before cooking — lets myrosinase work while still cold.
* **Don't boil.** Steam for 4-5 minutes or roast. If you do boil — keep the water for soup.
* **White, crisp florets and fresh green leaves** are a sign of freshness. Brown spots are oxidation — still edible, but the head is past its prime.
* **Don't throw away the leaves and stalk.** Both are edible, and the leaves are richer than the florets in several nutrients. The stalk is excellent chopped into soup or roasted.
* **Homemade cauliflower rice:** Grate it in a food processor and sauté lightly in a relatively dry pan to draw out moisture. The homemade version is worth 25 calories; the commercial frozen one may contain added oil and salt.
* **For FODMAP-sensitive people:** Start with a very small amount and prefer it well cooked. Boiling draws some of the mannitol into the water.

### Summary

Cauliflower provides 54% of the daily intake of vitamin C, plus folate and choline, in just 25 calories — an excellent ratio that earns it a place on any menu. As a carb substitute it really does save: 25 calories versus 130 in rice. But two things are worth remembering — **the bloating it causes comes from mannitol**, so people with irritable bowel need real caution, and **a "cauliflower product" is not cauliflower**: frozen doughs and nuggets are measured by their ingredient list, not their name.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What are the nutrition facts of cauliflower?**
Answer: 100 grams of fresh cauliflower provide 25 calories, 1.9 grams of protein, 2 grams of fiber, 48.2 mg of vitamin C (54% of daily intake), 57 mcg of folate (14%) and 44 mg of choline. It is 92% water, so its energy density is one of the lowest among vegetables.

**Question: Does cauliflower rice really save calories?**
Answer: Yes, and significantly — 25 calories and 5 grams of carbohydrates per 100 grams, versus 130 calories and 28 grams of carbohydrates in cooked white rice. The caveat is that rice provides available energy and cauliflower doesn't, so a blanket substitution is not suitable for people who train or need energy. In addition, frozen cauliflower rice products sometimes contain added oil and salt.

**Question: Why does cauliflower give me gas and bloating?**
Answer: Mainly because of mannitol, a sugar alcohol that is only partially absorbed in the small intestine and is fermented in the colon. Alongside it there are also sulfur compounds from the cruciferous family. This places cauliflower in the high-FODMAP category in almost any amount, unlike broccoli, whose florets are considered low-FODMAP in a small serving.

**Question: Why is cauliflower white?**
Answer: Because it grows shaded. The head of the cauliflower is an arrested inflorescence covered by the large leaves — in some varieties on their own, and in others the farmer ties them. Without light, no chlorophyll forms. The nutritional implication is that cauliflower is low in carotenoids compared with broccoli, but almost matches it in vitamin C.

**Question: Is "cauliflower pizza dough" a healthy choice?**
Answer: It depends entirely on the product. Many commercial versions contain corn starch or rice flour, cheese, oil and salt, and cauliflower is a minority ingredient — some reach 250 calories per 100 grams, ten times the vegetable. It is worth reading the ingredient list in order, and not relying on the product name.

**Question: How do you cook cauliflower without losing vitamins?**
Answer: Don't boil it. Vitamin C is water-soluble and heat-sensitive, and prolonged boiling can remove half of it. Steaming for 4-5 minutes or roasting in the oven at 220 degrees preserves most of it. Cutting and waiting 30-40 minutes before cooking also lets the enzyme myrosinase work while still cold.

**Question: Is cauliflower bad for the thyroid?**
Answer: Almost always not. Its glucosinolates may interfere with iodine uptake, but the effect is significant only with a combination of existing iodine deficiency and very large intake of the raw vegetable. Cooking reduces it, and for anyone whose iodine intake is adequate it is not a practical consideration.

**Question: Can you eat cauliflower leaves and stalk?**
Answer: Yes, both are edible and nutritious. The leaves are used like kale and are even richer than the florets in several nutrients, and the stalk is excellent chopped into soup or roasted in the oven. Throwing them away wastes about a third of the head.
