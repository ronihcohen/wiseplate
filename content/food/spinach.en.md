## Spinach (*Spinacia oleracea*)

Spinach (in Hebrew, "תרד") is one of the most nutrient-dense vegetables there is: 100 grams provide **four times the daily requirement of [vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/)**, half of the [vitamin A](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-a/) and almost half of the [folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/) — in just 23 calories.

And yet, the two things spinach is most famous for are actually its weakest points: **its iron is barely absorbed, and its calcium is almost unavailable.** Both are the fault of the same compound — **oxalates**.

That doesn't make spinach a bad vegetable. It just means it's worth eating it for what it is truly good at.

**Spinach's real strength** lies in the first three highlighted rows and the lutein row — not in the iron row.

### Iron — the story everyone knows, and it isn't true

Spinach contains 2.7 mg of [iron](/en/food/iron/) per 100 grams, which sounds pretty good. The problem is **availability**:

* **This is non-heme iron**, which is absorbed much less well than iron from animal sources.
* **The oxalates in spinach bind it** and lower absorption even further — estimates speak of single-digit absorption rates.

**As for the popular story** that a decimal point misplaced in the 19th century created the spinach myth — that story itself is disputed, and later historical research found no basis for it. What is true and relevant: **spinach is not a practical source of iron.** Anyone who needs iron will find it in [red meat](/en/food/red-meat/), in [lentils](/en/food/lentil/) with [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/), or in a supplement — not in a spinach salad.

**What does help:** [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) in the same meal significantly increases the absorption of non-heme iron. [Lemon](/en/food/lemon/) juice on spinach is not just about flavor.

### Oxalates — what they are, who should care, and what helps

**Oxalic acid** is a plant compound that binds divalent minerals — mainly [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/), [iron](/en/food/iron/) and [magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) — to form insoluble salts. Spinach is one of the most oxalate-rich foods: about 750 mg per 100 grams.

Two consequences:

* **The calcium in spinach is barely absorbed.** The absorption rate from spinach is around 5%, compared with about 32% from [milk](/en/food/milk/). The 99 mg of calcium listed in tables is in practice about 5 mg.
* **Kidney stones.** In people who have had calcium oxalate stones, large amounts of spinach increase urinary oxalate excretion. **The solution is not avoidance but pairing:** eat spinach **together with a source of calcium** — [yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/), cheese, [tahini](/en/food/tahini/) — so that the oxalate binds to calcium **in the gut** and is excreted in the stool, instead of being absorbed and reaching the kidney. And drink plenty.

**What really reduces oxalates: boiling.** Studies show a 30-87% drop in oxalate in boiled spinach, as it dissolves into the water. **Steaming reduces it much less, and frying hardly at all.** If oxalates concern you — boil and discard the water.

### Benefits

* **[Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) in an exceptional amount:** 403% of the Daily Value per 100 grams. Essential for clotting, and for directing [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) to bone rather than to the walls of blood vessels.
* **Lutein and zeaxanthin:** 12,200 mcg per 100 grams — one of the highest sources in food. These carotenoids accumulate in the macula and filter blue light; in observational studies their intake is linked to a lower risk of age-related macular degeneration.
* **[Folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/):** 49% of the Daily Value — very significant during pregnancy and the childbearing years.
* **[Vitamin A](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-a/)** as beta-carotene: 52%.
* **Natural nitrates.** Spinach is rich in nitrate, which the body converts to nitrite and nitric oxide (NO). This pathway has been studied in the context of blood vessel dilation, blood pressure and mechanical efficiency in endurance exercise — the area where [beets](/en/food/beets/) are better known, but spinach is just as rich in nitrate.
* **[Magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/):** 19% of the Daily Value.
* **Very low energy density:** 23 calories per 100 grams.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Oxalates** — as detailed above: calcium that isn't absorbed, iron that isn't absorbed, and a consideration with kidney stones.
* **[Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) and anticoagulants.** 403% per 100 grams is about the highest amount in any food. **People taking Coumadin (warfarin) don't need to avoid spinach — they need steady, consistent intake**, so that the drug dose is calibrated to it. A sudden bowl of spinach after a month without is the problem, not the spinach.
* **Dramatic shrinking when cooked.** 500 grams of fresh spinach shrink to about 100 grams cooked. This changes the serving calculation in both directions — it's easy to eat a huge dose of vitamin K without noticing.
* **Nitrate and infants.** Infants under three months old are not usually given high-nitrate vegetables, because of the risk of methemoglobinemia. This is not a consideration for older children and adults.
* **Canned spinach** contains added [sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) and loses vitamin C.
* **Short storage life.** Fresh spinach leaves wilt within days and lose some of their folate and vitamin C.
* **Nitrate and cadmium accumulation.** Under certain growing conditions spinach can accumulate nitrate and cadmium at high levels; this is a reason to vary leafy greens and not rely on spinach alone.

### Practical use

* **Eat it both raw and cooked.** Raw — to preserve [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) and folate. Cooked — fewer oxalates and a more concentrated amount of [vitamin A](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-a/) and lutein.
* **Always with fat.** [Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/), [vitamin A](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-a/), lutein and zeaxanthin are all fat-soluble. [Olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/), [avocado](/en/food/avocado/) or [walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/) in the salad significantly increase absorption.
* **With [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/)** — [lemon](/en/food/lemon/), [red bell pepper](/en/food/red-pepper/), [tomato](/en/food/tomato/) — to improve iron absorption.
* **With a source of [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/)** — [yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/), cheese or [tahini](/en/food/tahini/) — so that the oxalate binds in the gut and not in the kidney. This is especially critical for people prone to kidney stones.
* **Boil and discard** if oxalates are a consideration — 30-87% of them pass into the water.
* **Don't cook it in iron or aluminum pans** for a long time — spinach oxidizes and darkens, and the metallic taste intensifies.
* **Vary your greens:** [kale](/en/food/kale/), Swiss chard, arugula and lettuce — different profiles, and some have much lower oxalates. See [the spinach, kale and chard comparison](/en/compare/spinach-kale-chard/).
* **Frozen spinach is an excellent choice.** It is blanched and frozen close to harvest, keeps for months, and is significantly cheaper than fresh.

### Summary

Spinach is an excellent vegetable — but not for what it is famous for. **Its iron and calcium are barely absorbed** because of oxalates; if you're after them, you need another source. **What it does offer:** vitamin K in an amount that has no substitute (403%), lutein and zeaxanthin at some of the highest levels in food, folate, vitamin A and natural nitrate. Three practical rules: **always with fat** (the vitamins are fat-soluble), **with a source of calcium** (the oxalate binds in the gut and not in the kidney), and **boil and discard** when oxalates are a concern. And for people on Coumadin — don't avoid it, keep it consistent.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is the nutritional value of spinach?**
Answer: 100 grams of raw spinach provide 23 calories, 2.9 grams of protein, 483 mcg of vitamin K (403% of the Daily Value), 469 mcg of vitamin A (52%), 194 mcg of folate (49%), 79 mg of magnesium and 12,200 mcg of lutein and zeaxanthin — among the highest in food.

**Question: Is spinach a good source of iron?**
Answer: Not in practice. It contains 2.7 mg per 100 grams, but this is non-heme iron that is poorly absorbed, and the oxalates in spinach bind it and lower absorption to single-digit rates. The popular story about a "misplaced decimal point" is itself disputed. Anyone who needs iron will find it in red meat, in lentils with vitamin C or in a supplement.

**Question: What are oxalates and why do they matter in spinach?**
Answer: Oxalic acid is a plant compound that binds calcium, iron and magnesium into insoluble salts. Spinach contains about 750 mg per 100 grams — among the highest in food. The result: the calcium in spinach is absorbed at a rate of only about 5% (compared with 32% from milk), and the iron is barely absorbed. For people prone to calcium oxalate kidney stones it is also a medical consideration.

**Question: How do you reduce oxalates in spinach?**
Answer: Boiling. Studies show a 30-87% drop in oxalate in boiled spinach, as it passes into the cooking water — so it's important to discard it. Steaming reduces it much less and frying hardly at all. A complementary method is to eat spinach together with a source of calcium — yogurt, cheese or tahini — so the oxalate binds in the gut and is excreted in the stool instead of reaching the kidney.

**Question: Can you eat spinach while taking Coumadin (warfarin)?**
Answer: Yes, and there is no need to avoid it. Spinach contains 403% of the Daily Value of vitamin K per 100 grams, which is Coumadin's antagonist — but the principle is steady, consistent intake that the drug dose is calibrated to. The problem is sharp swings: a month without spinach and then a large bowl. It's worth telling your doctor about any significant change in your habits.

**Question: Which is better — raw or cooked spinach?**
Answer: Both, alternately. Raw preserves vitamin C and folate, which are heat-sensitive. Cooked contains fewer oxalates (if you boil and discard the water), and provides a more concentrated amount of vitamin A and lutein, which heat actually releases from the cell wall. In both cases it's important to eat it with fat — all the main carotenoids and vitamins in spinach are fat-soluble.

**Question: Is frozen spinach as good as fresh?**
Answer: Yes, and sometimes even better. It is blanched and frozen close to harvest, so vitamin loss is smaller than in fresh leaves that have sat in the fridge for days. It is also significantly cheaper, keeps for months, and is already shrunk — which saves you the gap between 500 grams fresh and 100 grams cooked.

**Question: How much spinach is too much?**
Answer: For a healthy person there is no practical ceiling, and daily spinach is a good choice. Two groups need to take care: people prone to calcium oxalate kidney stones — it's worth limiting it and eating it with calcium and plenty of fluids; and people taking anticoagulants — not to limit it, but to keep the amount consistent. In any case it's worth varying your greens and not relying on spinach alone.
