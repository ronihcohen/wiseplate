## Mushrooms

Mushrooms are neither plant nor animal — they belong to a separate biological kingdom, and that is exactly what makes them nutritionally interesting. In Hebrew they are called "פטריות". They supply compounds that vegetables lack (beta-glucans, ergothioneine) and one nutrient that is almost absent from every other plant source: **[vitamin D](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-d/)**.

They are also extremely low in calories — 22 calories per 100 grams — while their texture and umami flavor deliver a meat-like sense of fullness.

### The vitamin D trick — and a fact you can use at home

Mushrooms contain **ergosterol**, a molecule that is the fungal counterpart of the cholesterol in our skin. Just as in human skin, exposure to UV radiation converts it into vitamin D — in this case vitamin D2.

* **Mushrooms grown in the dark** (most commercial mushrooms) contain almost zero vitamin D.
* **Mushrooms exposed to UV** — in a controlled commercial process or simply in the sun — reach 10-25 mcg (400-1,000 IU) per 100 grams.
* **It works at home:** lay sliced mushrooms, **gill side up**, in direct sunlight for 15-60 minutes. Studies have shown a dramatic rise in vitamin D content. The mushrooms keep the vitamin even after drying and storage.
* **Caveat:** vitamin D2 is effective at raising blood levels of the vitamin, but less so than D3, which comes from animal sources and the sun. Still, this is the only meaningful plant source — especially relevant for vegans.

### Benefits

* **Very low in calories with a satisfying texture:** 22 calories per 100 grams. Mixing chopped mushrooms into ground meat (a "blended burger") cuts calories and saturated fat without hurting texture or flavor — a method that has been studied and adopted in institutional kitchens.
* **Beta-glucans:** soluble fibers unique to the fungal cell wall. They have been studied for regulating the immune response and for lowering [cholesterol](/en/food/olive-oil/), similar — though not identical — to the beta-glucan in [oats](/en/food/oatmeal/).
* **Ergothioneine:** an antioxidant amino acid for which mushrooms are by far the richest dietary source. The human body has a dedicated transporter for it, which hints at a physiological role. It is currently being studied in the context of aging and brain health — a promising direction that is not yet settled.
* **B vitamins and minerals:** riboflavin, niacin and pantothenic acid at 23%-31% of the daily value, alongside [copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/) and [selenium](/en/food/supplements/selenium/) — significant amounts for such a low-calorie food.
* **Umami without sodium:** the natural glutamate in mushrooms boosts flavor and lets you use less salt in cooked dishes. Dried mushrooms (shiitake, porcini) are especially concentrated.
* **A partial meat substitute** with a completely different nutritional profile — no saturated fat and no [cholesterol](/en/food/eggs/).

### Downsides and risks

* **Wild mushrooms — the only risk that is real.** Mushroom poisoning is life-threatening, and misidentification is common. *Amanita phalloides* (the "death cap") resembles edible species and contains amatoxins that cause liver failure, sometimes with a delay of 6-24 hours before symptoms appear — a delay that masks how serious the situation is. **Never eat wild mushrooms without expert identification.** Identification apps are not reliable enough for this purpose.
* **Agaritine in raw white mushrooms:** a compound with mutagenic activity in laboratory studies. It is sensitive to heat and storage — cooking reduces it significantly. The risk at normal intake is considered negligible, but it is a good reason to prefer cooked mushrooms over raw.
* **Purines and gout:** mushrooms contain a moderate amount of purines. People with active gout may prefer to limit them.
* **Heavy metal uptake:** mushrooms efficiently absorb from the substrate they grow on. Wild mushrooms from polluted areas or roadsides can accumulate cadmium, lead and radiocesium. Commercial mushrooms are grown on a controlled substrate.
* **Digestive difficulty in large amounts:** the chitin in the cell wall is not digestible by humans and can cause discomfort in large quantities. Cooking breaks some of it down.
* **Allergy** exists but is rare, as is sensitivity to mold in some people.

### Practical use

* **Cook them, don't eat them raw** — this breaks down chitin, reduces agaritine and releases nutrients.
* **Don't soak them in water.** Mushrooms absorb water like a sponge and then won't brown. Wipe them with a damp cloth or brush them.
* **A hot, uncrowded pan.** Mushrooms release water; if the pan is crowded they steam instead of browning, and the flavor goes with it.
* **Expose them to the sun before cooking** — 15-60 minutes gill side up, for vitamin D.
* **Dried mushrooms** are concentrated in flavor, and their soaking water makes an excellent stock — don't throw it out.
* **Shiitake** provide lentinan, a beta-glucan studied in its own right; **portobellos** provide a meaty texture for grilling.

### Summary

Mushrooms provide riboflavin, copper, selenium and beta-glucans for just 22 calories per 100 grams, and they are the only meaningful plant source of [vitamin D](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-d/) — but only if they have been exposed to UV, which you can do at home in half an hour in the sun. They are almost entirely safe when commercially grown and cooked; the only risk worth taking seriously is foraging wild mushrooms without expert identification.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What are the benefits and downsides of mushrooms?**
Answer: The benefits — only 22 calories per 100 grams, riboflavin and copper at 31%-36% of the daily value, beta-glucans, ergothioneine, and the ability to provide vitamin D after UV exposure. The downsides — purines that matter for people with gout, agaritine in raw mushrooms, and digestive difficulty in large amounts. The only real risk is foraging wild mushrooms.

**Question: Do mushrooms contain vitamin D?**
Answer: Only if they have been exposed to UV radiation. Mushrooms grown in the dark contain almost zero, whereas UV-exposed mushrooms reach 10-25 mcg (400-1,000 IU) per 100 grams. They contain ergosterol, which light converts into vitamin D2 — just as in human skin.

**Question: How do you increase the vitamin D content of mushrooms at home?**
Answer: Lay the sliced mushrooms in direct sunlight, gill side up, for 15-60 minutes. The increase in vitamin content is dramatic, and it is retained even after drying and storage.

**Question: Is it OK to eat raw mushrooms?**
Answer: Better not. Raw white mushrooms contain agaritine, a compound with mutagenic activity in the lab, and chitin, which is hard to digest. Cooking reduces both significantly. The risk from occasional consumption is considered negligible, but there is no practical reason to prefer them raw.

**Question: How many calories are in mushrooms?**
Answer: 22 calories per 100 grams — among the lowest of any food. Alongside that come 3.1 grams of protein and a gram of fiber, giving them an exceptional satiety-to-calorie ratio.

**Question: Is it dangerous to forage mushrooms in the wild?**
Answer: Yes, and it is the only real risk with mushrooms. Poisonous species such as Amanita phalloides resemble edible species and contain amatoxins that cause liver failure, sometimes with a delay of 6-24 hours before symptoms appear. Never eat wild mushrooms without expert identification, and identification apps are not reliable enough.

**Question: Why won't my mushrooms brown in the pan?**
Answer: Probably for one of two reasons — they were soaked and absorbed water, or the pan is too crowded. Mushrooms release water as they cook; in a crowded pan they steam instead of browning. Wipe them with a damp cloth instead of rinsing, and fry them in small batches in a hot pan.
