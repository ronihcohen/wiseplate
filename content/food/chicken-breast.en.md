## Chicken Breast

**In the trade it is called *skinless boneless chicken breast*** — chicken breast without skin and without bone. In Hebrew it is "חזה עוף".

Chicken breast is probably **the protein source with the best protein-to-calorie ratio** available at a reasonable price in the supermarket. That is why it has become the default for anyone counting macronutrients — people who train, people in a calorie deficit, and weight-loss meal plans.

The nutritional discussion about it almost always stops at this point. But the two questions that really determine what happens on the plate are not about protein: **what was injected into it before you bought it**, and **how you cooked it**.

*Note: the cooked figures are higher because water loss concentrates the nutrients. 100 grams cooked is about 135 grams raw.*

### Protein density — why it really wins

31 grams of protein in 165 calories. That is a ratio of about **0.19 grams of protein per calorie**, and few whole foods come close to it:

| Food (100 g cooked) | Calories | Protein | Protein per 100 calories |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chicken breast** | **165** | **31 g** | **18.8 g** |
| [Tuna](/en/food/tuna/) in water | 116 | 25.5 g | 22.0 g |
| [Shrimp](/en/food/shrimp/) | 99 | 24 g | 24.2 g |
| [Lean beef](/en/food/red-meat/) | 215 | 26 g | 12.1 g |
| [Salmon](/en/food/salmon/) | 208 | 22 g | 10.6 g |
| [Eggs](/en/food/eggs/) | 155 | 13 g | 8.4 g |
| Firm [tofu](/en/food/tofu/) | 144 | 16 g | 11.1 g |

The protein itself is a **complete protein** with a PDCAAS score close to 1.0, rich in leucine — the amino acid that activates the mTOR pathway and muscle protein synthesis. 100 grams provide about 2.5 grams of leucine, above the threshold of about 2-3 grams considered sufficient to trigger the anabolic response.

### Injected chicken — the most important point

This is the number one practical question in Israel, and it is almost absent from the nutritional discussion.

A considerable share of the chicken breasts sold — especially frozen, packaged and imported ones — undergo **injection with a water and salt solution** (and sometimes also phosphates or dissolved plant protein). The process is called *injection* or *plumping*, and its purpose is twofold: the product weighs more, and the meat stays moister when cooked.

**What it does to the numbers:**

| | Fresh chicken breast | Injected chicken breast |
|---|---|---|
| [Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) per 100 g | about 45-74 mg | **about 300-500 mg** |
| Protein per 100 g | 22.5 g | about 19-20 g (diluted) |
| What you pay for | Meat | Meat + up to 15% water |

The difference in sodium is **five to ten times**. A 200-gram serving of injected breast may provide 600-1,000 mg of sodium **before** you have added a single grain of salt — that is, up to about half the recommended daily limit.

**How to spot it:** read the ingredient list. Real chicken breast contains one ingredient — "chicken breast". If water, salt, sodium phosphate or "brine solution" appear there, the product is injected. Sometimes an explicit percentage also appears ("contains up to 12% solution").

This also explains the familiar phenomenon of a breast that shrinks significantly and releases lots of liquid into the pan — you are cooking the water you paid for.

### Breast vs. thigh — the comparison worth making

The assumption that "breast is always better" deserves a closer look:

| Per 100 g cooked | Breast (skinless) | Thigh (skinless) |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 165 | 209 |
| Protein | 31 g | 26 g |
| Fat | 3.6 g | 10.9 g |
| Of which saturated | 1.0 g | 3.0 g |
| [Iron](/en/food/iron/) | 0.5 mg | 1.3 mg |
| [Zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/) | 1.0 mg | 2.4 mg |
| [Selenium](/en/food/supplements/selenium/) | 27 mcg | 26 mcg |

**The conclusion is more balanced than people tend to think.** The breast clearly wins on protein-to-calorie ratio — which is why it is the right choice in a calorie deficit or with a high protein target. But **the thigh contains 2.5 times the iron and 2.4 times the zinc**, and the extra fat is not a problem in the context of a normal diet. The thigh is also far more forgiving in cooking and almost impossible to dry out.

Anyone who eats chicken every day will benefit from combining the two.

### Benefits

* **Exceptional protein density** — as detailed above, along with a relatively low price and full availability.

* **Niacin (B3) — 86% of the daily value in 100 grams.** Required to make NAD and NADP, key coenzymes in all of the cell's energy-producing processes.

* **[Selenium](/en/food/supplements/selenium/) — 49%** per 100 grams, supporting thyroid function and the antioxidant defense system.

* **[Vitamin B6](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b6/) — 47%.** Involved in amino acid metabolism and neurotransmitter production.

* **Very low in saturated fat:** 1 gram per 100 grams — among the lowest of all animal protein sources.

* **[Choline](/en/food/supplements/choline/):** a nutrient many people fall short on, needed to make acetylcholine and build cell membranes.

* **Culinary flexibility and satiety:** protein is the most satiating macronutrient per calorie, and has the highest thermic effect (TEF) — about 20-30% of its calories are burned in digesting it.

### Downsides and considerations

* **HCA and PAH from high-heat cooking — more than people tend to think.** This point surprises people: **well-done grilled chicken sometimes produces higher HCA levels than beef**. Especially the compound PhIP, classified as a possible carcinogen, which forms from a reaction between creatine, amino acids and sugars at temperatures above 150°C (302°F).

  **This is a factor under your control:** an acidic marinade with herbs, [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) and [garlic](/en/food/garlic/) significantly reduces their formation; cooking at moderate heat, avoiding charring, flipping often and removing burnt parts do the rest. Black char has nothing to do with "rich" flavor — it is simply burning.

* **Campylobacter and Salmonella.** Raw chicken is one of the leading sources of food poisoning. Two crucial rules:
  * **Don't wash raw chicken.** Washing does not remove bacteria, but it splashes droplets up to about a meter around the sink and contaminates surfaces and utensils. This is an official recommendation of health authorities in many countries.
  * **Cook to 74°C (165°F)** in the thickest part. A meat thermometer is the only tool that gives a definite answer.

* **Drying out — the main culinary problem.** Chicken breast is a lean muscle, low in fat and low in collagen. The muscle fibers contract and squeeze out water sharply around 65-70°C (149-158°F), and the difference between juicy and dry is sometimes just 3-4 degrees. This is where most of the "dry chicken" experience comes from.

* **"Striped" white meat and "woody breast".** A phenomenon that has spread in recent years because of rapid fattening and broilers with especially large breasts. It shows up as white stripes along the grain or as a hard, rubbery texture. Nutritionally, the meat contains **less protein and more fat and collagen** than usual, and texture quality drops. It is not a health hazard, but it changes the product.

* **Low in [iron](/en/food/iron/) and [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/):** unlike [red meat](/en/food/red-meat/), chicken breast is not a significant source of heme iron. Anyone who relies on it as an almost exclusive meat source — especially women of childbearing age — should make sure they have other iron sources.

* **Processed chicken products** — chicken sausages, frozen schnitzels, nuggets and chicken pastrami — are an entirely different category: high sodium, fried coating, and sometimes mechanically separated meat. The data on this page does not apply to them.

* **Animal welfare and environmental considerations:** industrial broiler farming raises questions of crowding and growth rate. The carbon footprint of chicken is significantly lower than that of beef, but not zero.

### Practical use — how not to dry it out

**Dry brine — the single step that changes the most.** Sprinkle salt (about 1% of the meat's weight) over the breast and leave it uncovered in the fridge for 40 minutes to overnight. The salt partially breaks down muscle proteins and lets them bind water — the result is significantly juicier. If you bought an **injected** breast, skip this step: it is already salted.

**A thermometer, not a clock.** Take it off at 72°C (162°F) and let it rest 5 minutes — carryover heat brings it to 74°C (165°F). This is the practical difference between juicy and dry.

**Even out the thickness.** Butterfly it or pound it lightly, so the whole piece reaches temperature together instead of the thin end drying out while the thick part cooks.

**Rest before slicing.** 5 minutes lets the juices redistribute through the fibers.

**An acidic marinade** — [lemon](/en/food/lemon/), [yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/), vinegar, [garlic](/en/food/garlic/), rosemary and turmeric. It improves flavor and reduces HCA at the same time. Don't marinate in acid for more than 2-3 hours — the texture turns mealy.

**Gentle cooking is better:** baking at moderate heat, moist cooking, sous vide, or a quick sear finished in the oven — all of these beat long grilling over coals.

**Read the label when buying.** Only one ingredient: "chicken breast". Water, salt or phosphates on the list = an injected product.

**Mix it up with thigh** — 2.5 times the iron and 2.4 times the zinc, and almost impossible to dry out.

### Summary

Chicken breast provides **31 grams of complete protein in 165 calories** — one of the best protein-to-calorie ratios in the supermarket — along with niacin (86% of the daily value), selenium and B6, and very little saturated fat. As an everyday protein source it is an excellent and fully justified choice.

Three practical points change the outcome more than any discussion of macronutrients: **check the label to make sure the product isn't injected** (injection multiplies sodium 5-10 times and dilutes the protein), **cook with a thermometer to 74°C, not by the clock** (the gap between juicy and dry is a few degrees), and **avoid charring** — well-done grilled chicken sometimes produces more HCA than beef, and an acidic marinade reduces this significantly. Finally, it is worth mixing in thigh: less impressive on macros, but 2.5 times the iron and 2.4 times the zinc.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much protein is in chicken breast?**
Answer: 31 grams per 100 grams cooked, in just 165 calories. Raw — 22.5 grams per 100 grams, and the difference comes from water loss during cooking, which concentrates the nutrients. This is one of the best protein-to-calorie ratios among readily available protein sources.

**Question: What are the nutrition facts of chicken breast per 100 grams?**
Answer: Cooked — 165 calories, 31 grams of protein, 3.6 grams of fat (of which 1 gram saturated), zero carbohydrates. Plus 13.7 mg of niacin (86% of the daily value), 27 mcg of selenium, 0.6 mg of vitamin B6 and 228 mg of phosphorus.

**Question: What is injected chicken and how do you spot it?**
Answer: A breast injected with a water and salt solution (sometimes also phosphates) to increase weight and retain moisture. Sodium jumps from about 45-74 mg per 100 grams to about 300-500 mg — five to ten times as much — and the protein is diluted. You spot it by reading the ingredient list: a real breast contains only one ingredient. Water, salt or sodium phosphate on the list indicate injection.

**Question: Which is better — chicken breast or chicken thigh?**
Answer: It depends on the goal. The breast wins on protein-to-calorie ratio (31 grams in 165 calories vs. 26 grams in 209) and suits a calorie deficit. The thigh contains 2.5 times the iron and 2.4 times the zinc, is far more forgiving in cooking and almost impossible to dry out. Combining the two is better than sticking to one.

**Question: How do you cook chicken breast so it isn't dry?**
Answer: Three things. Dry brining — salt at about 1% of the meat's weight, in the fridge for 40 minutes to overnight. A thermometer — take it off at 72 degrees Celsius and let it rest 5 minutes so it reaches 74. And evening out the thickness — pound it or butterfly it so the whole piece cooks evenly. The gap between juicy and dry is only 3-4 degrees.

**Question: Should you wash chicken before cooking?**
Answer: No, and it is explicitly recommended not to. Washing does not remove bacteria such as Campylobacter and Salmonella, but it splashes contaminated droplets up to about a meter around the sink and contaminates surfaces and utensils. Only cooking to 74 degrees Celsius guarantees safety.

**Question: Is chicken breast healthier than red meat?**
Answer: In some respects, yes — less saturated fat, fewer calories per unit of protein, and it is not included in the IARC classifications for red meat. But red meat provides heme iron, zinc, B12 and creatine in much larger amounts. Chicken breast is not a significant source of iron, and anyone who relies on it almost exclusively should make sure they have other iron sources.

**Question: What are the white stripes in chicken breast?**
Answer: A phenomenon called "white striping", together with "woody breast" — the result of rapid fattening and broiler breeds with especially large breasts. The meat contains less protein and more fat and collagen than usual, and the texture is hard or rubbery. It is not a health hazard, but it is an inferior product in terms of quality and texture.

**Question: Is grilled chicken dangerous?**
Answer: It depends how. Prolonged high-heat grilling creates HCA and PAH — and well-done grilled chicken sometimes produces higher HCA levels than beef, especially PhIP. The risk can be reduced significantly: an acidic marinade with herbs, cooking at moderate heat, flipping often, avoiding charring and removing burnt parts.

**Question: How much chicken breast can you eat a day?**
Answer: There is no defined threshold, and it is considered a reasonable everyday food. A typical serving is 150-200 grams. The practical consideration is not the amount but variety — relying exclusively on chicken breast misses out on iron, zinc and omega-3, so it is worth also including thigh, [fish](/en/food/salmon/), [eggs](/en/food/eggs/) and legumes.
