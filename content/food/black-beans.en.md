## Black Beans (*Phaseolus vulgaris*)

Black beans are one of the most nutrient-dense legumes there are: **8.9 grams of protein and 8.7 grams of fiber per 100 grams cooked**, along with **37% of the daily value of [folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/)** — at the price of a dried legume.

And what sets them apart from their relatives is precisely their color: **the black seed coat is rich in anthocyanins**, the same pigments found in [blueberries](/en/food/blueberries/) and [red cabbage](/en/food/cabbage/). In tests of antioxidant capacity, black beans are at the top of the bean list — **and all of that capacity is in the skin.**

**8.7 grams of fiber per 100 grams cooked** — one cup provides almost the entire recommended daily intake of fiber, which most of the population does not reach.

### The gas — and what really works

Gas from legumes is caused by **oligosaccharides** (raffinose, stachyose and verbascose) — carbohydrates the human body has no enzyme to break down, so they reach the large intestine intact and are fermented there.

**Three methods that work, in order of effectiveness:**

1. **Soaking and changing the water.** Soak for 8-12 hours, **then pour off the water and replace it before cooking.** The oligosaccharides are water-soluble and pass into the water — discarding the soaking water removes a substantial share of them. **This is the step with the biggest impact**, and changing the water matters more than the soaking itself.
2. **Thorough cooking.** Beans that remain a little hard are not only less tasty — they also cause more symptoms.
3. **Gradual adaptation.** **The microbiome adapts.** People who eat legumes regularly suffer less from gas — not because the oligosaccharides have disappeared, but because the bacterial population changes. **Start with 3-4 tablespoons and increase over weeks.**

**What also helps:** the enzyme alpha-galactosidase as a supplement (sold over the counter), and seasoning with cumin or the herb epazote — traditional methods with partial logic behind them.

### Resistant starch — an advantage of cooking ahead

As with [rice](/en/food/rice/), **cooking and cooling beans creates resistant starch**: some of the starch undergoes retrogradation into a crystalline structure that digestive enzymes struggle to break down. It behaves like dietary fiber — **fewer available calories, a milder glycemic response, and food for the bacteria of the large intestine**, which produce butyrate from it.

**The practical meaning:** beans cooked yesterday and kept in the refrigerator are metabolically better than fresh beans from the pot — and reheating does not cancel most of the effect.

### Benefits

* **Protein and fiber together:** 8.9 and 8.7 grams per 100 grams cooked — a combination with almost no parallel outside legumes.
* **[Folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/):** 37% of the daily value — one of the best plant sources.
* **Anthocyanins in the skin** — as detailed above.
* **A very low glycemic index (around 30).** The combination of fiber, protein and resistant starch produces one of the mildest glycemic responses of any carbohydrate food.
* **Lipid profile.** Regular legume consumption has been linked in meta-analyses to lower LDL and improved cardiometabolic markers.
* **[Magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/), [iron](/en/food/iron/) and [potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/)** in significant amounts.
* **Price and sustainability.** One of the cheapest sources of protein, with a low environmental footprint — legumes even fix nitrogen and enrich the soil.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Gas and bloating** — as detailed above.
* **FODMAPs.** Black beans are rich in GOS. A serving of about 45 grams cooked is considered low-FODMAP, and beyond that they may trigger symptoms in sensitive people. **Canned, rinsed beans are better tolerated** — some of the GOS has passed into the liquid.
* **Phytate.** Binds [iron](/en/food/iron/), [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/) and [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) and reduces their absorption. Soaking, sprouting and long cooking reduce it, and [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) in the same meal offsets part of the effect.
* **Lectins.** Raw beans contain phytohaemagglutinin, a lectin that causes acute poisoning. **The risk is much higher with red kidney beans than with black beans**, but the rule applies to both: **they must be boiled vigorously for at least 10 minutes — slow cooking at low temperature alone is not enough and may even make it worse.**
* **Incomplete protein.** Rich in lysine, low in methionine and cysteine. Combining them with [rice](/en/food/rice/) or another grain during the day completes the profile — and that is exactly the classic combination in South and Central American cuisines.
* **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) in canned beans.** Canned products usually contain 300-450 mg per 100 grams. **Rinsing in a colander removes about 40%.**
* **A legume, not a vegetable.** 132 calories and 23.7 grams of carbohydrates per 100 grams cooked — they count as a carbohydrate-protein serving.

### Practical use

* **Soak for 8-12 hours and pour off the water.** The most important step for reducing gas.
* **Boil vigorously for 10 minutes** before lowering the heat — a matter of safety, not just texture.
* **Salt at the end, not the beginning.** Early salt toughens the skin and lengthens cooking. **Acid — [tomatoes](/en/food/tomato/), [lemon](/en/food/lemon/), vinegar — only after the beans are soft**, for exactly the same reason.
* **Don't peel or mash off the skin** if possible — that is where the anthocyanins and insoluble fiber are.
* **Cook ahead and cool** — resistant starch and a better glycemic response.
* **Combine with [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/)** — [bell pepper](/en/food/red-pepper/), [tomato](/en/food/tomato/) or [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) — to improve absorption of non-heme iron.
* **With [rice](/en/food/rice/)** — the traditional combination that completes the amino acids, and not by chance.
* **Canned: rinse well** — removes about 40% of the sodium and improves the flavor.
* **Freeze cooked beans** in portions — legumes freeze excellently, and it saves cooking time next time.

### Summary

Black beans are one of the best deals on the menu: **8.9 grams of protein, 8.7 grams of fiber and 37% of the daily value of folate per 100 grams cooked**, with an especially low glycemic index and at the price of a dried legume. The black skin is not decoration — **that is where the anthocyanins are** that put them at the top of the bean list for antioxidant capacity. And as for gas, the main barrier for most people: **soak and pour off the water, cook thoroughly, and increase the amount gradually** — the microbiome adapts within weeks.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is the nutritional value of black beans?**
Answer: 100 grams cooked provide 132 calories, 8.9 grams of protein, 8.7 grams of fiber (31% of the daily value), 149 mcg of folate (37%), 70 mg of magnesium and 2.1 mg of iron. A cooked cup provides almost the entire recommended daily intake of fiber.

**Question: How do you reduce gas from black beans?**
Answer: Three methods, in order of effectiveness. Soak for 8-12 hours and pour off the soaking water before cooking — the oligosaccharides responsible for gas are water-soluble and pass into it, and this is the most significant step. Cook thoroughly. And increase the amount gradually, starting from 3-4 tablespoons, because the microbiome adapts within weeks.

**Question: Why are black beans black, and does it matter?**
Answer: The color comes from anthocyanins in the seed coat — the same pigments found in blueberries and red cabbage. They put black beans at the top of the bean list for antioxidant capacity, and all of that capacity is in the skin. So it is worth not peeling or mashing them when possible.

**Question: Do you have to boil beans?**
Answer: Yes, and it is a matter of safety. Raw beans contain phytohaemagglutinin, a lectin that causes acute poisoning. The risk is much higher with red kidney beans than with black beans, but the rule applies to both: a vigorous boil for at least ten minutes before lowering the heat. Slow cooking at low temperature alone is not enough and may even make it worse.

**Question: When do you add salt to beans?**
Answer: At the end of cooking. Salt at the start of cooking toughens the skin and significantly lengthens the time. The same rule applies to acid — tomatoes, lemon or vinegar should be added only after the beans are already soft, otherwise they will stay hard even after hours.

**Question: Is it worth cooking beans ahead and cooling them?**
Answer: Yes. Cooking and cooling create resistant starch — some of the starch undergoes retrogradation into a structure that digestive enzymes struggle to break down, and it behaves like fiber: fewer available calories, a milder glycemic response and food for gut bacteria. Reheating does not cancel most of the effect.

**Question: Do you need to rinse canned beans?**
Answer: Yes. A good rinse in a colander removes about 40% of the excess sodium and improves the flavor. A bonus: it also removes some of the GOS that has passed into the liquid, so canned, rinsed beans are better tolerated by people with irritable bowel syndrome.

**Question: Are black beans a complete protein?**
Answer: Not on their own — they are rich in lysine but low in methionine and cysteine. Combining them with rice or another grain completes the amino acid profile, and it doesn't need to happen in the same meal, just over the course of the day. The classic combination of black beans and rice in South and Central American cuisines is exactly that.
