## Brown Lentils

**Brown lentils are a cheap and very nutritious legume: a cooked cup (about 200 g) provides roughly 230 calories, 18 g of protein, 15 g of fiber and 6.6 mg of iron, with almost no fat.** In controlled trials, a daily serving of pulses such as lentils slightly lowered LDL cholesterol and improved markers of blood sugar control. In Hebrew they are called "עדשים חומות".

The lentil (Lens culinaris) was one of the first crops domesticated in the Middle East, and it is a staple of the region's cooking in mujadara, soups and stews. Brown lentils (and their green relatives) hold their shape when cooked, unlike red lentils, which collapse into a purée. Nutritionally, the differences between the types are small.

### Nutrition facts

Values are for **cooked** lentils (boiled in water, no salt), approximate, based on USDA data. 100 g of dry lentils gives about 250 g cooked.

| Nutrient | Per 100 g cooked | Per cup cooked (about 200 g) |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | about 116 | about 230 |
| Protein | about 9 g | about 18 g |
| Carbohydrates | about 20 g | about 40 g |
| Dietary fiber | about 7.9 g | about 15.6 g |
| Fat | about 0.4 g | under 1 g |
| Iron | about 3.3 mg | about 6.6 mg |
| Folate | about 181 mcg | about 358 mcg |
| Potassium | about 369 mg | about 730 mg |
| Magnesium | about 36 mg | about 71 mg |
| Zinc | about 1.3 mg | about 2.5 mg |

A cup of lentils covers nearly all of an adult's daily [folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/) needs (400 mcg) [(NIH: folate)](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Folate-HealthProfessional/), and nearly all of a man's iron needs (8 mg), although plant iron is absorbed less well than iron from meat.

### Benefits

**Cheap, substantial protein.** 18 g of protein per cup is about what you get from 70 g of chicken breast, at a fraction of the price. Lentil protein is low in methionine and grain protein is low in lysine, so the traditional pairing of lentils with rice or bulgur (mujadara) completes the amino acid profile. They do not have to be eaten in the same meal; having both over the course of the day is enough.

**LDL cholesterol.** A meta-analysis of 26 controlled trials (about 1,000 participants) found that a diet with roughly one daily serving of pulses (median 130 g a day, including lentils, chickpeas, beans and peas) lowered LDL by about 0.17 mmol/L (about 6.6 mg/dL) compared with a diet without pulses [(Ha et al., CMAJ 2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24710915/). That is a small but consistent drop, driven mainly by soluble fiber.

**Blood sugar control.** Lentils have a low glycemic index: their fiber, protein and resistant starch slow down sugar absorption. A meta-analysis of 41 trials found that pulses, alone or as part of a high-fiber or low-glycemic-index diet, improved fasting glucose and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) [(Sievenpiper et al., Diabetologia 2009)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19526214/). That makes lentils a good replacement for some of the bread or rice for people with diabetes or [insulin resistance](/en/food/supplements/insulin/).

**What about heart disease?** Here some caution is due. A 2023 systematic review found that in trials, legumes improved cholesterol and fasting glucose, but in large cohort studies (about two million participants, most of whom ate few legumes) there was no clear link with the risk of heart disease or diabetes [(Thorisdottir et al., 2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37288088/). The reasonable conclusion: lentils improve risk factors and fit well into a healthy diet, but there is no proof that they prevent heart attacks on their own.

**Fiber and fullness.** 15 g of fiber per cup is close to half the daily recommendation. The fiber feeds gut bacteria, keeps bowel movements regular and increases fullness, which helps with weight control.

**Iron and folate.** Lentils are one of the best plant sources of [iron](/en/food/iron/), which makes them especially important for vegetarians and women of reproductive age. Folate matters most for women before and early in pregnancy.

### Downsides

**Gas and bloating.** Lentils contain oligosaccharides (raffinose and stachyose) that the small intestine cannot break down, so they are fermented in the colon. That causes gas, mainly in people who are not used to legumes. The effect fades when you increase the amount gradually and when you soak them and drain the cooking water. People with irritable bowel syndrome on a low-FODMAP diet need to limit portions.

**Limited iron absorption.** Lentil iron is "non-heme" iron, which is absorbed less well than iron from meat, and the phytates in lentils reduce absorption further [(NIH: iron)](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/). A food rich in [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) in the same meal improves absorption, while tea or coffee with the meal reduces it.

**Lectins in undercooked lentils.** Lentils must be cooked until soft. Lectins and other compounds in raw lentils can upset digestion; full cooking neutralizes them. Lentil sprouts are best eaten lightly steamed.

**Potassium in kidney disease.** A cup of lentils contains about 730 mg of [potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/). For most people that is a plus, but people with kidney disease who have been told to limit potassium should agree on portions with their dietitian.

**Purines and gout.** Legumes contain moderate amounts of purines. Studies have not found that legumes raise gout risk the way meat and seafood do, but people with frequent attacks can check their own response.

### Practical use

- **Soaking:** brown lentils do not need soaking, but 2–8 hours shortens cooking and reduces gas. Discard the soaking water.
- **Cooking:** simmer 1 cup of lentils in 3 cups of water for 20–30 minutes, until soft but still whole. Add salt at the end.
- **Mujadara:** brown lentils with rice or bulgur and fried onions, a complete meal with complementary protein.
- **Salad:** cold cooked lentils with vegetables, [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) and olive oil. The lemon also helps iron absorption.
- **Soups and stews:** lentils thicken soups and can replace part of the meat in stews.
- **Storage:** cooked lentils keep 3–4 days in the fridge and several months in the freezer. Canned lentils are a convenient alternative; rinse them to remove some of the salt.

### Summary

Brown lentils are one of the best-value foods in the kitchen: high in protein, fiber, iron and folate, almost fat-free and inexpensive. Controlled trials show that a daily serving of pulses slightly lowers LDL and improves blood sugar control. The main downsides are gas at the start and partial absorption of their iron, and both can be managed with the right preparation.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much protein is in lentils?**
Answer: A cup of cooked lentils (about 200 g) has about 18 g of protein, and 100 g cooked has about 9 g. Dry lentils are about 2.5 times more concentrated, because they have not yet absorbed water.

**Question: Do brown lentils need to be soaked?**
Answer: No. Soaking shortens cooking time and reduces gas, but brown lentils cook well without it in 20–30 minutes.

**Question: How can I reduce gas from lentils?**
Answer: Increase the amount gradually over a few weeks, soak them and discard the soaking water, and cook them until fully soft. Most people adapt within a few weeks.

**Question: Are lentils a good source of iron?**
Answer: Yes, a cooked cup contains about 6.6 mg of iron. Because it is plant iron, eat lentils with a vitamin C-rich food and keep tea and coffee away from the meal to improve absorption.

**Question: Are lentils suitable for people with diabetes?**
Answer: Yes. They have a low glycemic index, and meta-analyses found that pulses improve fasting glucose and HbA1c. They are still a carbohydrate, so count them within the meal's carbohydrate total.

**Question: What is the difference between brown and red lentils?**
Answer: Red lentils are hulled and split, so they cook faster, fall apart into a purée and contain a little less fiber. Brown lentils keep their shape and suit salads and mujadara.
