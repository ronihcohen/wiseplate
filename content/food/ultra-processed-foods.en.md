## Ultra-Processed Foods

**Ultra-processed food (UPF) is an industrial product made mostly from extracted ingredients — sugars, oils, modified starches and isolated proteins — plus flavourings, emulsifiers and colours that are not used in home cooking.** In one controlled trial, a diet built from such products led people to eat about 500 extra calories a day, and observational studies link it to higher risks of obesity, type 2 diabetes and heart disease. In Hebrew it is called "מזון אולטרה-מעובד".

The term comes from the NOVA classification, developed in Brazil by Carlos Monteiro's research group. NOVA does not rank food by calories or by a single nutrient, but by the extent and purpose of industrial processing. [Monteiro 2019, Public Health Nutr](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30744710/)

### The four NOVA groups

| Group | What it includes | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Unprocessed or minimally processed | Food in its natural form, after cleaning, drying, freezing or simple cooking | Vegetables, fruit, [rice](/en/food/rice/), lentils, eggs, milk, fresh meat |
| 2. Processed culinary ingredients | Substances extracted from food and used for cooking | [Olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/), butter, sugar, salt |
| 3. Processed foods | Group 1 foods with something from group 2 added | Basic bread, cheese, canned vegetables, canned fish |
| 4. Ultra-processed | Industrial formulations with ingredients and additives not found in a home kitchen | [Sugary soft drinks](/en/food/sugary-soft-drinks/), [sweetened breakfast cereals](/en/food/sweetened-cereals/), hot dogs, packaged snacks, [instant soup](/en/food/instant-soup/) |

The practical tell: if the ingredient list includes things like glucose-fructose syrup, isolated whey protein, hydrogenated oil, maltodextrin, emulsifiers, stabilisers, "flavourings" or sweeteners, the product is probably in group 4.

### What the research shows

**Kevin Hall's controlled trial (2019):** 20 adults were admitted to the US National Institutes of Health Clinical Center and given two weeks of an ultra-processed diet and two weeks of an unprocessed diet, in random order. Meals were matched for calories offered, energy density, sugar, sodium and fibre, and participants ate as much as they wanted. On the ultra-processed diet they ate about 508 more calories a day on average, mostly from carbohydrate and fat, and gained about 0.9 kg; on the unprocessed diet they lost a similar amount. [Hall 2019, Cell Metab](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31105044/)

**An umbrella review in the BMJ (2024):** an analysis of 45 pooled analyses covering almost 10 million participants found that higher exposure to ultra-processed food was associated with higher risks of cardiovascular death, type 2 diabetes, obesity, and anxiety and depression. Most of the evidence was rated low or very low quality, because it comes from observational studies. [Lane 2024, BMJ](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38418082/)

### Why it may drive overeating

The mechanism is not settled, and researchers are testing several explanations: soft food that is eaten quickly, high energy density, combinations of fat with sugar or fat with salt that are hard to stop eating, and little fibre or water. In Hall's trial, participants ate the ultra-processed meals faster, but the study did not prove that this was the cause.

### Benefits (and what to weigh)

Not every ultra-processed product is the same, and there is a practical side too:

* **Convenience:** ready-made food saves time, which is a real consideration for busy families.
* **Safety and shelf life:** some processing, such as pasteurisation and preservation, reduces spoilage and food poisoning.
* **Fortification:** some products are fortified with vitamins and minerals, for example soy drinks with added [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/).
* **Big differences between products:** industrial wholemeal bread, flavoured yogurt or a soy drink are not equivalent to a hot dog or a soft drink, even though they may land in the same group.

### Downsides

* **Energy density:** many products deliver a lot of calories in a small volume with little satiety.
* **Sugar, sodium and saturated fat:** a major source of [sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) and added sugar in the Western diet.
* **Crowding out whole food:** every ultra-processed meal usually replaces vegetables, legumes and whole grains.
* **Heavy marketing:** much of the advertising targets children — see [food marketing to children](/en/food/marketing-to-children/).

### Counterarguments and caveats

The definition itself is disputed. Researchers such as Michael Gibney argue that NOVA defines too broad a group, that products are hard to classify consistently with it, and that the link to disease may come from nutrient content (sugar, salt, fat, calories) rather than processing itself. [Gibney 2019, Curr Dev Nutr](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30820487/) Hall's trial was also small (20 people) and short (4 weeks), so it cannot show what happens over years.

### Industry and regulation in Israel

* **Red and green labels:** since January 2020, Israel's Ministry of Health has required a red warning symbol on products high in sodium, sugar or saturated fat, alongside a voluntary green symbol for products that fit the national dietary recommendations. In a national survey, most respondents said they intended to buy fewer red-labelled products. [Bromberg 2022, Int J Food Sci Nutr](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34311654/)
* **The thresholds:** for solid food, the sodium threshold is about 400 mg per 100 g. A product reformulated to sit just below the threshold avoids the symbol — which does lower its sodium, but does not make it healthy. Read the nutrition table, not only the symbol.
* **Sugary drinks tax:** the tax took effect in January 2022 and was repealed a year later. A Ministry of Health study found that sales of large, fully taxed bottles fell by about 24% during the tax year, then crept back after repeal while staying below pre-tax levels. [Fliss Isakov 2026, Public Health](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41518863/)

### Practical use

* **Start with drinks:** swapping soft drinks for water removes the most sugar for the least effort.
* **An unprocessed base:** build meals from groups 1 and 3 — legumes, grains, vegetables, eggs, cheese — and add a ready-made product as a side, not as the base.
* **A short ingredient list:** the shorter and more familiar the list, the closer the product is to real food.
* **No need for perfection:** partial reduction still changes the picture. You do not have to give up every packaged product.

### Summary

Ultra-processed food is defined by how it is made, not by a single nutrient. A controlled trial showed that it leads to overeating, and large observational studies link it to disease, but most of the evidence is low quality and the mechanism is still being studied. The practical advice is simple: build most of your diet from unprocessed or lightly processed food, and treat ultra-processed products as an occasional extra.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is the difference between processed and ultra-processed food?**
Answer: Processed food is natural food with a simple addition such as salt, sugar or oil — cheese or basic bread, for example. Ultra-processed food is an industrial formulation with extracted ingredients and additives not used at home, such as emulsifiers, sweeteners and flavourings.

**Question: Does ultra-processed food cause weight gain?**
Answer: In one controlled trial, an ultra-processed diet led to about 500 extra calories a day and a gain of about 0.9 kg in two weeks. Observational studies support a link with obesity, but they cannot prove cause and effect.

**Question: How do I spot ultra-processed food in the supermarket?**
Answer: Read the ingredient list. Glucose-fructose syrup, isolated protein, hydrogenated oil, maltodextrin, emulsifiers, sweeteners or "flavourings" are signs that a product is ultra-processed.

**Question: Is all ultra-processed food equally harmful?**
Answer: No. In a large European cohort, the link to disease was found mainly for animal-based products (such as processed meat) and sweetened drinks, and not for industrial breads and breakfast cereals. [Cordova 2023, Lancet Reg Health Eur](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38115963/) The broad definition is one of the main criticisms of NOVA.

**Question: Is Israel's red label enough to choose well?**
Answer: It helps, but it only checks sodium, sugar and saturated fat. A product without a red label can still be ultra-processed and low in fibre, so read the ingredient list too.

**Question: What is the first step to cut back?**
Answer: Drop sugary drinks and swap one packaged snack a day for fruit, nuts or plain yogurt. Small, consistent changes beat trying to clear out the kitchen in one go.
