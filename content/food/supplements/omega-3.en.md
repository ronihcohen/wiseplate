## Omega-3

**Omega-3s are a family of essential fatty acids with three main forms: ALA from plants, and EPA and DHA from fish and algae.** In Hebrew they are called "אומגה 3". For most adults about 250 milligrams of EPA plus DHA a day is enough — the amount two servings of oily fish a week provide. [EFSA — dietary reference values for fats](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1461). A supplement helps mainly people who don't eat fish, and at high doses, under a doctor's guidance, to lower triglycerides.

The body can't make omega-3s, so they must come from food. ALA can be converted into EPA and DHA, but only partly — especially into DHA — so flax and chia seeds are not a full substitute for fish. [Baker et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27496755/).

### How much you need a day

| What | Daily amount | Source |
|---|---|---|
| ALA for men | 1.6 g | NIH |
| ALA for women | 1.1 g | NIH |
| EPA + DHA for adults | 250 mg | EFSA |
| EPA + DHA in pregnancy and breastfeeding | 250 mg plus 100–200 mg DHA | EFSA |
| Upper limit from supplements | up to 5 g EPA + DHA | FDA (via NIH) |

No official recommended dietary allowance (RDA) has been set for EPA and DHA. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) considers up to 5 grams a day from supplements safe. [NIH fact sheet on omega-3](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/).

### Food sources

| Food (3 oz / 85 g, cooked) | EPA + DHA |
|---|---|
| Farmed Atlantic [salmon](/en/food/salmon/) | about 1.8 g |
| Canned [sardines](/en/food/sardines/) | about 1.2 g |
| Atlantic [mackerel](/en/food/mackerel/) | about 1.0 g |
| Trout | about 0.8 g |
| Canned light [tuna](/en/food/tuna/) in water | about 0.2 g |

| Plant food | ALA |
|---|---|
| [Flax seeds](/en/food/flax-seeds/), 1 tablespoon | about 2.3 g |
| [Chia seeds](/en/food/chia-seeds/), 1 tablespoon | about 1.8–2.5 g |
| [Walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/), 1 oz (28 g) | about 2.5 g |
| [Hemp seeds](/en/food/hemp-seeds/), 3 tablespoons | about 2.5 g |

The gap between the two tables is the point: one serving of salmon provides nearly a week's EPA and DHA, while plants provide ALA, only a little of which becomes the active forms.

### Benefits

- **Lowering triglycerides:** at about 4 grams of EPA and DHA a day, omega-3s lower triglycerides by roughly 20–30% in people with high levels — their best-established benefit. [American Heart Association, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31422671/).
- **People at high cardiac risk:** in the REDUCE-IT trial, 4 grams a day of pure EPA (icosapent ethyl, a prescription drug) cut cardiovascular events by about 25% in people with high triglycerides who were already on statins. [REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/). The result applies to that specific drug and population, not to an ordinary fish oil capsule.
- **Pregnancy and development:** DHA is a structural part of the brain and retina, and EFSA recommends extra DHA in pregnancy for women who don't eat fish.
- **A vegan alternative:** algal oil provides EPA and DHA directly, without relying on conversion from ALA.

### Downsides and side effects

- **For healthy adults the benefit is limited.** In the VITAL trial, about 26,000 adults took 1 gram of omega-3 a day or a placebo for about five years, and the supplement did not significantly reduce cardiovascular events or cancer. [VITAL trial — omega-3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415637/). The STRENGTH trial, with 4 grams of EPA and DHA a day, also found no cardiac benefit. [STRENGTH](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33190147/).
- **Atrial fibrillation:** a meta-analysis of controlled trials found that omega-3 supplements slightly raise the risk of atrial fibrillation, and that the increase is larger at doses above 1 gram a day. [Gencer et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34612056/).
- **Bleeding:** at usual doses there is no evidence of a meaningful bleeding risk, but people taking anticoagulants (such as warfarin) or facing surgery should talk to their doctor before supplementing.
- **Minor effects:** fishy taste, burping, heartburn and diarrhea, mainly at high doses. Taking it with a meal reduces them.
- **Oxidation:** fish oil oxidizes on contact with air, heat and light. Oil with a sharp rotten-fish smell is probably oxidized. The health effect of oxidized oil is unclear, but there is no reason to consume it.
- **Contaminants:** mercury binds mainly to the protein in fish flesh rather than the oil, so tested fish oil supplements usually contain very little. Third-party testing adds confidence.

### Practical use

- **Fish first:** two servings of oily fish a week — salmon, sardines, mackerel — provide the recommended amount, along with protein, vitamin D and selenium.
- **If you don't eat fish:** a supplement of 250–500 mg EPA and DHA a day is a reasonable way to fill the gap. Vegans can choose algal oil.
- **Read the label correctly:** "1,000 mg fish oil" is not 1,000 mg of omega-3. What matters is EPA plus DHA, which in a standard capsule is sometimes only 300 mg.
- **High doses only under guidance:** 2–4 grams a day for high triglycerides is medical treatment, not a general supplement — because of the atrial fibrillation risk and drug interactions.

### Summary

Omega-3s are essential, and most people can get them from two servings of oily fish a week. People who don't eat fish can supplement with a few hundred milligrams of EPA and DHA, or with algal oil. The large trials did not find that supplements prevent heart disease or cancer in healthy adults; the proven benefit is lowering triglycerides at high doses, where the atrial fibrillation risk has to be weighed too. Flax and chia seeds provide ALA but are not a full substitute for fish.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much omega-3 do you need a day?**
Answer: For most adults about 250 mg of EPA and DHA a day, which two servings of oily fish a week provide. NIH also recommends 1.6 g of ALA for men and 1.1 g for women.

**Question: What is the difference between EPA, DHA and ALA?**
Answer: EPA and DHA are the active forms that come from fish and algae. ALA comes from plants, and the human body converts only a small part of it into EPA and even less into DHA.

**Question: Can flax or chia seeds replace fish?**
Answer: Not fully. They are rich in ALA, but conversion to EPA and especially DHA is limited. Vegans who want EPA and DHA can choose algal oil.

**Question: Do omega-3 supplements prevent heart disease?**
Answer: In healthy adults, probably not meaningfully, according to large trials such as VITAL. The proven benefit is lowering triglycerides at high doses, and a specific EPA drug for people at high risk.

**Question: Is there mercury in fish oil supplements?**
Answer: Usually very little, because mercury binds to the protein in fish flesh rather than the oil. A product tested by an independent lab adds confidence.

**Question: When should you take omega-3?**
Answer: With a meal, preferably one that contains fat. Absorption is better, and burping and fishy taste are reduced.

**Question: Does omega-3 thin the blood?**
Answer: It has a mild effect on clotting, and at usual doses there is no evidence of a meaningful bleeding risk. Anyone on anticoagulants or facing surgery should check with their doctor.
