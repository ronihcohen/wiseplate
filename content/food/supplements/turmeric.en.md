> This page is about **curcumin as a supplement** — doses, concentrated forms and clinical effectiveness. For **turmeric as a spice** — how much curcumin is in a teaspoon, culinary use and safety — see [Turmeric](/en/food/turmeric/).

## Curcumin Supplement

**Curcumin is the main yellow compound in turmeric, and as a supplement it has been tested mostly for joint pain: in small trials, about 1,000 mg a day modestly reduced osteoarthritis pain, similar to common painkillers. Its absorption is very low, and the evidence for its other uses is weak.** [Daily et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27533649/) In Hebrew it is "כורכומין", and the supplement is "תוסף כורכומין".

[Turmeric](/en/food/turmeric/) is a yellow root used as a spice in Indian and other Asian cooking. It contains only a few percent of curcuminoids, so clinical trials have almost all used concentrated extracts rather than the kitchen spice. Curcumin is one of the most studied plant compounds — more than 120 clinical trials have been run on it — but very few of them were large and well controlled. [Nelson et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28074653/)

### Dosage and forms

Curcumin has no recommended intake, because it is not an essential nutrient. The figures below are based on what has been tested in trials and on safety assessments:

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Dose tested in joint-pain trials | usually 500–1,500 mg of curcuminoids a day, split into 2–3 doses |
| EFSA acceptable daily intake (ADI) as a food additive | 3 mg per kg of body weight a day (about 210 mg for a 70 kg adult) |
| Curcumin in a teaspoon of ground turmeric (about 3 g) | roughly 60–150 mg (2%–5% by weight) |
| Typical trial length | 4–16 weeks |

The European Food Safety Authority's ADI was set for curcumin as a food coloring, and it is far below the doses in supplements. That does not mean supplement doses are necessarily dangerous, but it is a reminder that long-term safety at high doses has not been well studied. [EFSA — re-evaluation of curcumin as a food additive](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1679)

**Common supplement forms:**

- **95% curcumin with piperine** (black pepper extract) — the most common and cheapest form.
- **"Bioavailable" formulas** — phytosome (bound to phospholipids), micelles, nanoparticles. They raise blood levels of curcumin, but the comparisons between them are mostly funded by the manufacturers.
- **Turmeric root powder** — contains little curcumin per capsule, and differs from what most trials tested.

### Benefits

- **Osteoarthritis joint pain:** a meta-analysis of eight controlled trials found that turmeric extract or curcumin (usually about 1,000 mg a day) reduced pain and improved a knee-function score compared with placebo, and found no significant difference compared with painkillers such as ibuprofen. The authors themselves stressed that the trials were few and small, and some used the manufacturers' own products. [Daily et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27533649/)
- **Inflammation and oxidation:** in the lab, curcumin affects many inflammatory pathways. However, a thorough review by medicinal chemists showed that curcumin is an unstable molecule that is poorly absorbed and interferes with many lab assays — so test-tube results don't predict benefit in the body. [Nelson et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28074653/)
- **A tool, not a cure:** for someone with knee osteoarthritis who wants to try something alongside exercise, weight management and standard treatment, curcumin is a reasonable short trial. For "general inflammation", immunity or disease prevention, there is no good human evidence.

### Downsides and side effects

#### Very low absorption
Curcumin is broken down quickly in the gut and liver. In a classic study, 2 grams of curcumin alone barely reached the blood, and adding 20 mg of piperine raised its bioavailability about 20-fold (2,000%) — but even then levels were low and short-lived. [Shoba et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9619120/)

#### Liver injury
The US Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network (DILIN) described ten cases of liver injury from turmeric supplements, most since 2017; five patients were hospitalized and one died. Three of the products also contained piperine, and seven patients carried the gene variant HLA-B\*35:01. The researchers believe the frequency is rising, perhaps because of wider use and the combination with black pepper. [Halegoua-DeMarzio et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36252717/) Warning signs — fatigue, nausea, dark urine, or yellowing of the skin and eyes — mean stopping at once and seeing a doctor.

#### Bleeding and medications
Curcumin may strengthen the effect of blood thinners (warfarin, antiplatelet drugs and the newer anticoagulants), and piperine changes how the liver breaks down drugs and may raise the levels of other medications. Anyone on regular medication — especially blood thinners, diabetes drugs or chemotherapy — should talk to their doctor before taking a supplement. It's worth stopping it before planned surgery.

#### Digestion and gallstones
At high doses, nausea, heartburn and diarrhea are reported. Curcumin increases gallbladder contraction, so people with gallstones or bile duct obstruction should avoid it.

#### Pregnancy and breastfeeding
The amount of spice in food is safe, but there is not enough information on the safety of concentrated supplements in pregnancy and breastfeeding — so it's better to avoid them.

### Practical use

- **Worth considering for:** adults with knee osteoarthritis pain who want to try a supplement alongside standard care, after talking to their doctor.
- **Dose:** usually 500 mg twice a day with a meal, and no more than the label says.
- **Review after 8–12 weeks:** if there is no noticeable improvement, there is no reason to continue.
- **Don't use it:** with liver disease, gallstones, blood thinners (without a doctor's approval), in pregnancy, or before surgery.
- **Choose a third-party-tested product** (USP, NSF and similar); imported turmeric has in the past been found to contain lead (see the turmeric spice page).
- **The spice in the kitchen** is a different matter: a teaspoon of turmeric in a stew with oil and black pepper is part of a good diet, but it is not a therapeutic dose.

### Summary

Curcumin is not "the most powerful natural anti-inflammatory in the world", as it is often marketed. The best evidence is for modest relief of knee osteoarthritis pain, in small trials, at about 1,000 mg a day. Its absorption is low, the "enhanced" formulas cost more, and reports of liver injury — mainly with piperine-containing products — call for caution. If you want to try it: after checking with your doctor, for a limited period, and stop if there is no benefit or if signs of liver injury appear.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Does curcumin help joint pain?**
Answer: Possibly, to a modest degree. A meta-analysis of eight small trials found that about 1,000 mg a day reduced knee osteoarthritis pain compared with placebo, but the trials are few and the conclusion is not strong.

**Question: How much curcumin should I take a day?**
Answer: Trials usually tested 500–1,500 mg of curcuminoids a day, split into 2–3 doses with meals. There is no official recommended dose, and it's best not to exceed what the label says.

**Question: Why is black pepper added to curcumin supplements?**
Answer: Piperine, the pungent compound in black pepper, slows the breakdown of curcumin in the gut and liver. In a 1998 study it raised bioavailability about 20-fold. On the other hand, it also affects how drugs are broken down, and in some of the liver-injury cases the product contained piperine.

**Question: Can curcumin supplements damage the liver?**
Answer: Yes, in rare cases. The US DILIN network described ten cases, including one death, and the frequency appears to be rising. Fatigue, nausea, dark urine or yellowing mean stopping and seeing a doctor.

**Question: Can I take curcumin with blood thinners?**
Answer: Only with your doctor's approval. Curcumin may strengthen the blood-thinning effect, and piperine may change the level of the drug in the blood.

**Question: What is the difference between turmeric and curcumin?**
Answer: Turmeric is the whole root and spice; curcumin is its main yellow compound and makes up only a few percent of its weight. Supplements contain concentrated curcumin, in amounts you can't reach through cooking.

**Question: Can I take curcumin during pregnancy?**
Answer: The amount of spice in food is safe, but there is not enough information on concentrated supplements in pregnancy and breastfeeding, so it's best to avoid them during this time.
