A common rule of thumb says that "water-soluble vitamins aren't dangerous, because the excess is excreted in the urine." **Vitamin B6 is the main exception to that rule, and that is the most important point on this page.**

**Taking high-dose B6 over a long period causes sensory neuropathy** — damage to the peripheral nerves, with numbness, burning and loss of sensation in the hands and feet, and sometimes balance problems. The effect is well documented, and it is why regulators in various countries have capped the dose in over-the-counter supplements.

**And the practical problem:** many people don't know how much B6 they are taking. It turns up in **B-complex products, energy supplements, energy drinks, "anti-PMS" supplements and hair-and-nail supplements** — sometimes at 50-100 mg per unit, 40-75 times the daily requirement.

**One serving of chicken or fish a day covers about half the daily requirement.** Primary B6 deficiency is rare on a Western diet.

## Benefits (Pros)

### 1. More than 100 enzymatic reactions
The active form, **pyridoxal 5'-phosphate (P5P)**, is a cofactor for more than 100 enzymes — more than any other vitamin. Most of them deal with **amino acid metabolism**: breaking down, building and converting.

### 2. Producing neurotransmitters
B6 is needed to synthesize **serotonin, dopamine, GABA and noradrenaline**. This is the reason for the link between B6 deficiency and depression and irritability, and for its use in neuropsychiatric contexts.

### 3. Producing hemoglobin
It is a cofactor in the first step of heme synthesis. **B6 deficiency causes microcytic anemia that does not respond to [iron](/en/food/iron/)** — a finding reminiscent of [copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/) deficiency.

### 4. Morning sickness
B6 is **the recommended first-line treatment for nausea and vomiting of pregnancy** according to professional guidelines — usually 10-25 mg three times a day, sometimes combined with doxylamine. This is one of its best-established uses, and it is done under medical supervision.

### 5. Homocysteine
Together with [folic acid](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/) and [vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/), B6 breaks down homocysteine. **An important caveat:** lowering homocysteine with supplements has not been shown to reduce cardiac events.

## Downsides and side effects (Cons)

### 1. Neuropathy — the central caveat
As described above. **The effect has been clearly described at doses of hundreds of mg per day, but cases have also been reported at much lower doses taken for months and years.** The symptoms: numbness, burning, loss of touch and vibration sense, and unsteady gait. **They are usually reversible once you stop taking it, but recovery is slow and sometimes partial.**

### 2. Doses that add up without you noticing
**This is the common practical scenario.** Someone takes a B-complex (50 mg), an energy drink (10-20 mg) and a "hair and nails" supplement (25 mg) — and reaches more than 80 mg a day without meaning to. **It's worth adding up the B6 from all sources.**

### 3. The form you take matters
There is evidence that the form **pyridoxine hydrochloride** — the most common in supplements — is more strongly linked to neurotoxicity than the active form **P5P**. The proposed explanation is that excess pyridoxine competes for the binding sites of the active form. The question isn't settled by research, but it's worth knowing.

### 4. Drug interactions
B6 **reduces the effectiveness of levodopa** (in Parkinson's treatment) when it is taken without carbidopa. Conversely, isoniazid (for tuberculosis), penicillamine and other drugs **deplete B6** and require supplementation under medical supervision.

### 5. Deficiency — when it does happen
Not from diet, but from **alcohol addiction, kidney disease, malabsorption syndromes and certain drugs**. The symptoms: cracks at the corners of the mouth, an inflamed tongue, a greasy rash, confusion and, in severe cases, seizures.

## Summary

Vitamin B6 is essential for more than 100 enzymatic reactions, for producing neurotransmitters and for hemoglobin synthesis — **and it is easy to get from food**: a serving of chicken, fish, [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/) or [sunflower seeds](/en/food/sunflower-seeds/). **The most important thing to know about it is that it is the exception to the "water-soluble means harmless" rule: long-term high doses cause sensory neuropathy.** So the practical recommendation is: **add up the B6 from every supplement you take** — B-complex, energy, PMS, hair and nails — and make sure the total doesn't approach 100 mg a day. B6 deficiency is rare; excess is not.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much vitamin B6 do you need per day?**
Answer: 1.3 mg for adults up to age 50, 1.5-1.7 mg over 50, and 1.9-2.0 mg during pregnancy and breastfeeding. The Tolerable Upper Intake Level is 100 mg per day. One serving of chicken or fish provides about half the daily requirement, so primary dietary deficiency is rare.

**Question: Can vitamin B6 be harmful?**
Answer: Yes, and it is the important exception to the rule that "water-soluble vitamins are harmless." Taking high doses over a long period causes sensory neuropathy — numbness, burning and loss of sensation in the hands and feet, and sometimes unsteady gait. The effect has been clearly described at hundreds of mg per day, but cases have also been reported at low doses taken for months and years.

**Question: How do I know how much B6 I'm taking?**
Answer: You need to add it up from all sources, and that is exactly the point many people miss. B6 appears in B-complex products (sometimes 50 mg), energy supplements and energy drinks (10-20 mg), "anti-PMS" supplements and hair-and-nail supplements (25 mg). Combining three such products goes past 80 mg a day without you meaning to.

**Question: Does B6 help with nausea in pregnancy?**
Answer: Yes, and this is one of its best-established uses. Professional guidelines recommend it as a first-line treatment for nausea and vomiting of pregnancy, usually 10-25 mg three times a day, sometimes combined with doxylamine. It's important that treatment be done under medical supervision and not on your own initiative, because of the question of cumulative dose.

**Question: What's the difference between pyridoxine and P5P?**
Answer: P5P (pyridoxal 5'-phosphate) is the active form in the body, and pyridoxine hydrochloride is the common form in supplements, which the body converts. There is evidence that pyridoxine in particular is more strongly linked to neurotoxicity — the proposed explanation is that an excess of it competes for the binding sites of the active form. The question isn't settled by research, but it's worth knowing when choosing a supplement.

**Question: Which foods are rich in B6?**
Answer: Sunflower seeds (1.35 mg per 100 grams), salmon (0.82), chicken breast (0.55), banana (0.37), baked potato (0.31) and red bell pepper (0.29). B6 is widely spread through food, especially protein sources, so it is very easy to reach the recommended intake on a varied diet.

**Question: Does B6 interact with medications?**
Answer: Yes, in both directions. It reduces the effectiveness of levodopa in Parkinson's treatment when it is taken without carbidopa. Conversely, isoniazid for tuberculosis and penicillamine deplete B6 and may require supplementation — but this is done under medical supervision and at a controlled dose.

**Question: Who is at risk of B6 deficiency?**
Answer: Not people eating a normal diet. Deficiency appears mainly with alcohol addiction, kidney disease, malabsorption syndromes and certain medications. Symptoms include cracks at the corners of the mouth, an inflamed tongue, a greasy rash, confusion and, in severe cases, microcytic anemia that does not respond to iron, and even seizures.
