## Vitamin E

**Adults need 15 mg of vitamin E a day, which a handful of almonds or sunflower seeds plus vegetable oil supplies. High-dose supplements (400 IU or more) did not prevent heart disease or cancer in large trials, and some were linked to harm — so most people have no reason to take them.** [NIH fact sheet on vitamin E](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-HealthProfessional/) In Hebrew it is "ויטמין E".

Vitamin E is **one of the key case studies in the world of supplements**: a promising theory that did not hold up in large trials.

The logic was compelling. Vitamin E is the body's main fat-soluble antioxidant; it sits inside cell membranes and stops the chain reaction of lipid oxidation. Oxidation of LDL is an early step in atherosclerosis. **From this came the assumption that high-dose vitamin E would prevent heart disease and cancer** — and doctors' recommendations and supplement sales soared accordingly.

**The trials showed otherwise.** A meta-analysis linked doses of 400 IU per day and above to **a small increase in all-cause mortality** [Miller et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15537682/), and extended follow-up of the SELECT trial found **a 17% increase in prostate cancer incidence** in men taking vitamin E. [Klein et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21990298/) A supplement considered "unable to hurt" turned out to be useless, and at high doses even harmful.

### Benefits

#### 1. Protecting cell membranes
Vitamin E sits inside the phospholipid layer of the cell membrane and **stops the chain oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids**. This is its central biological role, and it is well established.

#### 2. Working in a cycle with [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/)
When vitamin E neutralizes a radical, it becomes a relatively stable radical itself. **[Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) "recycles" it** and returns it to its active form — a classic example of antioxidants working as a system rather than individually, which is why food beats a single molecule in a capsule.

#### 3. Immune function
Vitamin E deficiency impairs T-cell function. **In older adults, a moderate dose improved immune markers in some trials**, but it is unclear whether that translates into fewer infections.

#### 4. Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease
This is its **best-established clinical use**: the PIVENS trial found that 800 IU of vitamin E a day for about two years improved liver biopsy findings in non-diabetic NASH patients compared with placebo. [Sanyal et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20427778/) **The treatment is given under medical supervision**, weighing the benefit against the risks below.

### Downsides and side effects

#### 1. Higher mortality at high doses
A widely cited meta-analysis of controlled trials found a link between supplementing **400 IU per day or more** and a small rise in all-cause mortality. [Miller et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15537682/) In the HOPE trial, people with heart disease or diabetes who took 400 IU a day for about seven years had no fewer cardiac events or cancers, and a slightly higher risk of heart failure. [HOPE-TOO, 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15769967/) The finding sparked methodological debate, but it changed the recommendations: **no professional body today recommends high-dose vitamin E for the general population.**

#### 2. The SELECT trial — prostate cancer
In a trial of more than 35,000 men, the vitamin E arm (400 IU per day) showed **a 17% increase in prostate cancer incidence** compared with placebo. [Klein et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21990298/) [Selenium](/en/food/supplements/selenium/), tested alongside it, showed no benefit.

#### 3. Effect on blood clotting
Vitamin E inhibits platelet aggregation and interferes with the activity of [vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/). **High doses raise the risk of bleeding, especially alongside Coumadin, aspirin or the newer anticoagulants.** A meta-analysis of nine trials found about a 22% rise in hemorrhagic stroke (alongside a small drop in ischemic stroke). [Schürks et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21051774/) Anyone on blood thinners should check with their doctor before any vitamin E supplement.

#### 4. Natural vs. synthetic — a real difference
**d-alpha-tocopherol (RRR)** is the natural form, and **dl-alpha-tocopherol (all-rac)** is the synthetic one. The synthetic form is a mixture of eight isomers, only some of which are biologically active — so **the natural form is roughly twice as active** for the same amount. It's worth checking the label.

#### 5. The single form vs. the family
"Vitamin E" is **a family name for eight compounds** — four tocopherols and four tocotrienols. **Supplements almost always contain alpha-tocopherol alone**, and high doses of it **lower gamma-tocopherol levels** in the body — a form with roles of its own. In food, all the forms come together. This is a leading hypothesis for the gap between the results for food and the results for supplements.

#### 6. Deficiency — when it does happen
Not from diet, but from **fat malabsorption syndromes**: cystic fibrosis, Crohn's disease, cholestasis, bariatric surgery and rare genetic diseases. The symptoms are neurological — ataxia, peripheral neuropathy and muscle weakness.

### Practical use

- **From food:** a handful (30 grams) of almonds provides about half the daily amount; sunflower seeds, hazelnuts, canola oil and olive oil fill in the rest.
- **A supplement is not recommended for the general population**, and certainly not at 400 IU or more. An ordinary multivitamin contains a small amount, which is fine.
- **Who might:** people with NASH, as their doctor decides, and people with conditions that impair fat absorption.
- **Blood thinners:** warfarin, antiplatelet drugs and the newer anticoagulants — don't add vitamin E without advice.

### Summary

Vitamin E is essential for protecting cell membranes, and it is very easy to get from food — **a handful of almonds, [sunflower seeds](/en/food/sunflower-seeds/) or [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) does the job.** But it is also the sharpest lesson in the world of supplements: **the theory was excellent, and the trials showed harm.** 400 IU per day and above have been linked to higher mortality and more prostate cancer, and high doses raise the risk of bleeding. **The practical recommendation: food yes, high-dose supplements no** — except in specific clinical contexts, such as fatty liver, and only under medical supervision.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much vitamin E do you need per day?**
Answer: 15 mg of alpha-tocopherol for adults (19 mg while breastfeeding), with a Tolerable Upper Intake Level of 1,000 mg from supplements. A 30-gram handful of almonds provides about half the amount, and a spoonful of olive oil or sunflower seeds easily makes up the rest. Dietary deficiency barely exists.

**Question: Are vitamin E supplements dangerous?**
Answer: At high doses — possibly. Meta-analyses linked supplementing 400 IU per day or more to a small but consistent rise in all-cause mortality, and the SELECT trial found a 17% increase in prostate cancer incidence. In addition, high doses inhibit platelet aggregation and raise the risk of bleeding. No professional body today recommends them for the general population.

**Question: What's the difference between natural and synthetic vitamin E?**
Answer: d-alpha-tocopherol (RRR) is the natural form, and dl-alpha-tocopherol (all-rac) is the synthetic one — a mixture of eight isomers, only some of which are biologically active. The result is that the natural form is roughly twice as active for the same amount. If you do take a supplement, it's worth checking which form is listed on the label.

**Question: Why is food rich in vitamin E beneficial but the supplement is not?**
Answer: There are two leading hypotheses. The first: "vitamin E" is a family name for eight compounds, while supplements almost always contain alpha-tocopherol alone — which at high doses even lowers gamma-tocopherol levels in the body. The second: antioxidants work as a system, and vitamin C "recycles" vitamin E — so a single molecule in a capsule doesn't reproduce what food does.

**Question: When is a vitamin E supplement recommended?**
Answer: In specific clinical contexts and under medical supervision. The most prominent is non-alcoholic fatty liver disease — the PIVENS trial found that 800 IU per day improved liver histology in non-diabetic NASH patients. Supplementation is also needed in fat malabsorption syndromes, such as cystic fibrosis or cholestasis. These are medical decisions, not something to start on your own.

**Question: Does vitamin E interact with anticoagulants?**
Answer: Yes. It inhibits platelet aggregation and interferes with the activity of vitamin K, so high doses alongside Coumadin, aspirin or the newer anticoagulants raise the risk of bleeding. Meta-analyses have even pointed to an increase in hemorrhagic stroke. Anyone taking anticoagulants should consult a professional before any vitamin E supplement.

**Question: Which foods are rich in vitamin E?**
Answer: Wheat germ oil (149 mg per 100 grams) leads by a wide margin, followed by sunflower seeds (35), almonds (26), hazelnuts (15) and olive oil (14). Avocado and red bell pepper also have reasonable amounts. Because the vitamin is fat-soluble, it is concentrated in nuts, seeds and oils.

**Question: Who is at risk of vitamin E deficiency?**
Answer: Not people eating a normal diet. Deficiency appears almost exclusively in fat malabsorption syndromes — cystic fibrosis, Crohn's disease, cholestasis, after bariatric surgery, and in rare genetic diseases. The symptoms are neurological: ataxia, peripheral neuropathy and muscle weakness.
