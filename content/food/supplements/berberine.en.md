## Berberine: Between Research Interest and Promises of a Cut

Berberine (in Hebrew, "ברברין") is a plant compound from the alkaloid family, marketed as a [dietary supplement](/en/food/supplements/) for blood sugar and weight loss. For people who train, the question is not only whether it changes a lab marker, but whether it has been proven to help with their athletic goal. There is no justification for presenting it as a proven supplement for building muscle, improving endurance or "cutting" in healthy athletes. NCCIH, the U.S. center for complementary health, stresses that research findings on weight loss are insufficient for firm conclusions. [NCCIH's review of berberine](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/berberine-and-weight-loss-what-you-need-to-know).

### Why is the nickname "nature's Ozempic" misleading?

A marketing nickname is not a medical comparison. To establish that a supplement is equivalent to a drug, appropriate studies on effectiveness, safety and long-term outcomes are needed. The fact that two substances are discussed in the context of weight or blood sugar does not make them equivalent alternatives.

Similarly, a description of a biochemical pathway is not a prediction of a training outcome. Even if a substance affects an enzyme or a metabolic marker, you still need to check what happens in humans: who took part in the trial, what the treatment was compared against, what changed and by how much. That is how you distinguish a possible explanation of action from a proven benefit.

### What does the research say about weight and belly fat?

Early studies pointed to a possible improvement in some weight measures, but many were small, differed from one another or were at risk of bias. Sometimes participants with metabolic diseases were studied, rather than healthy people who train. [NCCIH's assessment of the evidence](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/berberine-and-weight-loss-what-you-need-to-know).

A randomized trial published in 2026 in JAMA Network Open included 337 people without diabetes, with obesity and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Taking one gram of berberine a day for six months did not significantly reduce visceral fat area or liver fat content compared with placebo. These were the primary outcomes tested. Changes were found in some secondary measures, including LDL, but there was no sweeping proof of weight loss. The secondary analyses were not corrected for multiple comparisons, so they should be interpreted as findings that require confirmation. [The 2026 randomized trial](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2844037).

This does not mean berberine has no activity at all, but that the claim it "melts belly fat" is not supported by this trial. And even a positive result in participants with obesity and liver disease would not prove a benefit in a healthy runner or a woman doing strength training. The conclusion has to match the population studied. For a separate discussion of the evidence on performance supplements, see the articles on [creatine](/en/food/supplements/creatine/) and [caffeine](/en/food/supplements/caffeine/).

### Blood sugar and cholesterol: what can be concluded?

There are preliminary findings pointing to a possible benefit in some blood sugar and lipid measures, mainly in populations with metabolic disorders. They are not a basis for replacing diabetes treatment. A change in LDL or glucose is not in itself proof of fewer heart attacks, prevention of diabetes complications or longer life. [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/berberine-and-weight-loss-what-you-need-to-know).

An athlete who gets an abnormal blood test result should go for a workup bringing previous results, a list of medications and information about their training and diet routine. Buying a supplement right away may skip ahead of the important question: what does the finding mean, and is treatment needed at all?

### Doses in research are not instructions for use

The 2026 trial used one gram a day. Do not conclude that this is an effective dose for cutting: in that same trial, the main benefit tested was not found. Likewise, increasing the dose after being disappointed by a result is not an evidence-based way to improve effectiveness. [The trial's protocol and results](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2844037).

In a combination product it is important to distinguish between the amount of the blend and the amount of berberine. A result from a product containing several substances doesn't tell you which ingredient worked, and a label stating "enhanced absorption" does not prove a clinical advantage. When comparing products you can ask for the research on the exact formulation, instead of settling for general research on one of the ingredients.

### Side effects and interactions

Berberine may cause abdominal pain, constipation, diarrhea and nausea. It should not be used during pregnancy or breastfeeding, and should not be given to infants. It may affect medications, in particular cyclosporine. [NCCIH's safety information](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/berberine-and-weight-loss-what-you-need-to-know).

In a human study, repeated berberine intake reduced the activity of drug-metabolizing enzymes, including CYP2D6, CYP2C9 and CYP3A4. The finding explains why combinations should be checked with a pharmacist; it does not mean that every drug metabolized through these pathways will be affected to the same degree. [The study on drug metabolism](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4898966/).

The ANSES opinion notes risks including lowered blood sugar and blood pressure, and warns against combining it with medications without a professional assessment. In particular, do not add berberine to diabetes treatment or change medication doses on your own. [The ANSES opinion](https://anses.fr/en/system/files/NUT2018SA0095EN.pdf).

### How to make a practical decision?

| Goal | What to find out before deciding |
|---|---|
| Improving a training result | Is there a trial that tested the type of effort and the relevant population of athletes? |
| Changing body composition | Was a change in fat measured, and not just in weight or a lab marker? |
| Treating high blood sugar | What is the diagnosis, which medications are being taken and what is the follow-up plan? |
| Buying a combination product | Are the formulation and dose the same as in the cited study? |

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Is berberine a substitute for metformin or Ozempic?**
Answer: No. It should not be treated as an equivalent alternative, and you should not stop a prescribed treatment because you are using a supplement.

**Question: Does it help reduce belly fat?**
Answer: There is no such promise. In the 2026 trial, no significant reduction in visceral fat was found compared with placebo in the population studied.

**Question: Does a plant source guarantee safety?**
Answer: No. Berberine has biological activity, side effects and the potential for interactions with medications.
