## Vitamin A

**Adults need 900 micrograms of vitamin A a day (men) or 700 (women), and the safe upper limit is 3,000 micrograms — but that limit applies only to retinol, the preformed kind from animal foods and supplements, not to beta-carotene from vegetables.** An ordinary diet covers most people's needs; a supplement is needed only in unusual cases. [NIH fact sheet on vitamin A](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminA-HealthProfessional/) In Hebrew it is "ויטמין A".

"Vitamin A" is one name for two different families, and the difference between them is **the difference between something that can build up to toxic levels and something that cannot**:

* **Retinoids (retinol)** — the preformed version, from animal sources: liver, [eggs](/en/food/eggs/), butter, fish oil and supplements. **The body does not regulate how much of it is absorbed, so any excess builds up in the liver and causes toxicity.**
* **Carotenoids (beta-carotene)** — a plant provitamin: [pumpkin](/en/food/pumpkin/), carrots, sweet potatoes, leafy greens. **The body converts only what it needs, so there is no toxicity** — at most an orange tint to the skin that fades.

**This is the one difference that really matters to remember from this page**, and it explains both why liver is best avoided in pregnancy and why beta-carotene supplements actually harmed smokers.

### Dosage and sources

| Age | Recommended per day (mcg RAE) | Upper limit per day (mcg retinol) |
|---|---|---|
| Infants 0–12 months | 400–500 | 600 |
| 1–3 | 300 | 600 |
| 4–8 | 400 | 900 |
| 9–13 | 600 | 1,700 |
| 14–18 | boys 900, girls 700 | 2,800 |
| 19 and over | men 900, women 700 | 3,000 |
| Pregnancy (19+) | 770 | 3,000 |
| Breastfeeding (19+) | 1,300 | 3,000 |

RAE (Retinol Activity Equivalents) is a unit that accounts for differences in absorption: 12 micrograms of beta-carotene from food count as 1 microgram of retinol. The upper limit applies to preformed retinol only, not to beta-carotene. [NIH ODS — vitamin A](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminA-HealthProfessional/)

Food values in the next table are approximate, per 100 grams:

| Source | Vitamin A per 100 grams |
|---|---|
| **Beef liver** | **~9,000 mcg RAE (1,000%)** |
| Baked sweet potato | 961 mcg RAE |
| [Carrot](/en/food/carrot/) | 835 mcg RAE |
| [Pumpkin](/en/food/pumpkin/) | 426 mcg RAE |
| [Spinach](/en/food/spinach/) | 469 mcg RAE |
| [Red bell pepper](/en/food/red-pepper/) | 157 mcg RAE |
| One [egg](/en/food/eggs/) | 75 mcg RAE |

**Note the first row:** a single serving of liver provides **ten times the daily requirement** and comes close to the Tolerable Upper Intake Level. It is the only food where an ordinary serving can be dangerous.

### Benefits

#### 1. Vision — especially in the dark
Retinal, a derivative of vitamin A, binds to the protein opsin to form **rhodopsin** — the light-sensitive pigment in the retina's rods. **Night blindness is the earliest sign of vitamin A deficiency**, and severe deficiency is **the world's leading cause of preventable blindness in children.**

#### 2. Healthy epithelial tissue
Vitamin A regulates the differentiation of epithelial cells — the skin and the mucous membranes of the respiratory, digestive and urinary tracts. **These are the body's first protective barriers**, hence the close link between vitamin A and immune function.

#### 3. Immune function and child mortality
Where vitamin A deficiency is common, supplementing children is an established public-health measure, and it reduces deaths, mainly from measles and diarrhea, in low-resource regions. In Israel and other Western countries such deficiency is rare.

#### 4. Growth and reproduction
It is needed for normal fetal development, sperm production and bone growth.

### Downsides and side effects

#### 1. Pregnancy — the sharpest warning
**High-dose retinol is a teratogen** — it can cause birth defects, so in pregnancy retinol intake should stay under the upper limit. [NIH ODS — vitamin A](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminA-HealthProfessional/)

* **Liver is off-limits in pregnancy.** A single serving of liver can contain ten times the daily requirement, and health authorities advise pregnant women to avoid it and liver spreads.
* **Retinoid drugs** — isotretinoin (Roaccutane) for acne and acitretin — **are powerfully teratogenic**, and require strict contraception before, during and after treatment.
* **Beta-carotene from food, by contrast, is safe in pregnancy.** There is no limit on carrots, sweet potatoes or pumpkin. Prenatal vitamins usually contain beta-carotene or a moderate amount of retinol — check the label and don't add a separate vitamin A supplement without advice.

#### 2. The CARET and ATBC trials — smokers and beta-carotene
This is one of the defining findings in the supplement field. Two large trials tested whether **beta-carotene** supplements prevent lung cancer in smokers and people exposed to asbestos.

**The result was the opposite:** in the ATBC trial, about 29,000 Finnish male smokers given 20 mg of beta-carotene a day developed more lung cancer than the control group [ATBC, 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8127329/). In CARET, a combination of beta-carotene and retinol in smokers and asbestos workers raised lung cancer incidence and mortality, and the trial was stopped early [CARET, 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8602180/). Following these trials, the US Preventive Services Task Force recommends against beta-carotene supplements for preventing cancer or heart disease [USPSTF, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35727271/).

**The lesson that has stayed with us:** beta-carotene **in food** is safe and beneficial; **beta-carotene at high supplemental doses is not**, and for smokers it is genuinely dangerous. It is a classic example of the difference between a nutrient in its natural context and an isolated molecule at a megadose.

#### 3. Chronic hypervitaminosis A
Long-term retinol intake above the UL causes **headaches, nausea, dry skin, hair loss, bone pain, liver damage and raised intracranial pressure**. **Reduced bone density and a higher risk of fractures** have also been described with chronically high intake.

#### 4. Interaction with [vitamin D](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-d/)
Retinol and [vitamin D](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-d/) compete for shared nuclear receptors. **High-dose vitamin A may cancel out some of vitamin D's action on bone.**

#### 5. Beta-carotene conversion — variable efficiency
The rate at which beta-carotene is converted to retinol is not fixed: **it depends on the fat in the meal, the body's vitamin A status and genetics** — polymorphisms in the **BCO1** gene reduce conversion efficiency substantially in some people. **Two conclusions follow:** eat carotenoids **with fat**, and remember that vegans who rely on plants alone may have a gap.

### Practical use

- **Most people don't need a supplement.** A diet with orange and green vegetables, eggs and dairy covers the need.
- **Check your multivitamin:** if it contains retinol (retinyl palmitate or acetate), add it to what you get from food and make sure the total stays under 3,000 mcg a day.
- **Liver:** for adults who are not pregnant — an occasional serving, not a daily one.
- **Medications:** anyone taking retinoids for acne or psoriasis (isotretinoin, acitretin) should not take vitamin A supplements on top, because the effects add up. [NIH ODS — vitamin A](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminA-HealthProfessional/)
- **Smokers and former smokers:** avoid high-dose beta-carotene supplements.

### Summary

Vitamin A is one of the few cases where **the source determines whether the nutrient is safe or dangerous**. **Beta-carotene from food is completely safe**, because the body converts only as much as it needs; the only price of excess is orange palms. **Retinol from liver or supplements does accumulate and is toxic.** Two practical rules: **in pregnancy, avoid liver and retinol supplements** and rely on plant carotenoids; and **don't take high-dose beta-carotene supplements**, certainly not if you smoke — the CARET and ATBC trials showed a rise in lung cancer there. And finally: **eat carotenoids with fat**, otherwise most of them are not absorbed.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is the difference between retinol and beta-carotene?**
Answer: Retinol is preformed vitamin A from animal sources — liver, eggs, butter, fish oil and supplements — and the body does not regulate its absorption, so excess builds up in the liver and causes toxicity. Beta-carotene is a plant provitamin that the body converts only as needed, so it is not toxic. The Tolerable Upper Intake Level of 3,000 mcg applies to retinol only.

**Question: Why shouldn't you eat liver during pregnancy?**
Answer: Because high-dose retinol is a proven teratogen — it causes birth defects. A single serving of liver can contain about 9,000 mcg RAE, ten times the daily requirement and close to the Tolerable Upper Intake Level. Health authorities advise pregnant women to avoid liver and liver spreads. Carrots, sweet potatoes and pumpkin, on the other hand, are completely safe.

**Question: Are beta-carotene supplements safe?**
Answer: Not at high doses, and especially not for smokers. The CARET and ATBC trials tested whether beta-carotene supplements prevent lung cancer in smokers and people exposed to asbestos, and found the opposite — a rise in lung cancer incidence and mortality. The CARET trial was stopped early. Beta-carotene from food is entirely safe; the isolated molecule at a megadose is not.

**Question: How much vitamin A do you need per day?**
Answer: 900 mcg RAE for men and 700 for women, 770 in pregnancy and 1,300 while breastfeeding. The Tolerable Upper Intake Level is 3,000 mcg — but it applies only to retinol, not to plant beta-carotene, which has no limit.

**Question: Why should you eat carrots and pumpkin with fat?**
Answer: Because beta-carotene is fat-soluble and trapped inside the plant cell wall. Without fat in the meal, absorption is very low. Cooking breaks down the cell wall, and fat — olive oil, avocado, tahini or nuts — lets the carotenoid be absorbed in the gut. This changes the amount actually absorbed dramatically.

**Question: Are orange palms a sign of a problem?**
Answer: No. This is carotenoderma — a buildup of beta-carotene in the outer layer of the skin after high, sustained intake of carrots, pumpkin or sweet potatoes. It is completely harmless, fades on its own within weeks once you cut back, and is unrelated to jaundice — in jaundice the whites of the eyes turn yellow too.

**Question: What happens with too much vitamin A?**
Answer: Chronic hypervitaminosis A from retinol causes headaches, nausea, dry skin, hair loss, bone pain, liver damage and raised intracranial pressure. Reduced bone density and a higher risk of fractures have also been described. It is important to note that these effects are caused by retinol — not by beta-carotene.

**Question: Are vegans at risk of vitamin A deficiency?**
Answer: Not necessarily, but there is a point worth knowing. They depend entirely on converting beta-carotene to retinol, and conversion efficiency varies — it depends on the fat in the meal, the body's vitamin status and genetics: polymorphisms in the BCO1 gene reduce it noticeably in some people. The practical solution is generous intake of orange and green vegetables, always with fat.
