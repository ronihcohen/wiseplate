## Iron Supplement

> This page is about **iron supplements** — forms, doses, side effects and when to supplement at all. For iron as a dietary mineral, deficiency symptoms and food sources, see [Iron](/en/food/iron/).

An iron supplement is one of the most effective supplements there is — and also one of the most commonly taken the wrong way. Unlike most vitamins, the human body **has no mechanism for excreting excess iron**; it regulates only the rate of absorption. That means accumulated iron stays in the body, so supplementing "just to be safe" is not a neutral act.

### When you actually need to supplement

Supplementation is justified only on the basis of a blood test, not on the basis of feeling tired. The key marker is **ferritin** — the iron storage protein that reflects the size of the stores — and not hemoglobin alone. You can have depleted stores and full-blown symptoms while hemoglobin is still in the normal range, a condition called iron deficiency without anemia.

Common guidelines put the deficiency threshold at ferritin below about 30 ng/mL, and even higher in the presence of inflammation, since ferritin is also an acute-phase protein and may be misleadingly elevated. Interpretation is medical, and should come from your treating doctor, not from an article.

Populations in which supplementation is common and justified: women with heavy menstrual bleeding, pregnant women, infants and toddlers after six months of age, people after bariatric surgery, patients with chronic gastrointestinal bleeding, regular blood donors and endurance runners. At the same time, there are populations in which an iron supplement is dangerous — first among them carriers of **hemochromatosis**, a genetic disease in which the body absorbs excess iron.

### Benefits

- **Correcting iron-deficiency anemia:** this is the most effective and cheapest treatment for a condition that affects about a third of the world's population. A rise in hemoglobin is usually seen within two to four weeks.
- **Less fatigue even before the anemia is corrected:** studies in women with low ferritin and normal hemoglobin showed reduced fatigue after supplementation — meaning the benefit doesn't depend on full anemia.
- **Better aerobic performance:** in athletes with depleted stores, replenishing iron improves endurance capacity. In athletes with normal stores — no benefit.
- **Cognitive development in childhood:** iron deficiency in early childhood is linked to impaired development, and some of the damage may be irreversible. This is the reason for monitoring in toddlers.

### Forms of iron supplements — the practical difference

This is the most important consideration when choosing a supplement, and it mainly affects side effects rather than effectiveness.

| Form | Elemental iron | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Ferrous sulfate** | about 20% | The cheapest and most studied, and so the default in prescriptions. Its rate of gastrointestinal side effects is the highest of the forms |
| **Ferrous gluconate** | about 12% | Gentler than sulfate, but needs a larger dose for the same amount of iron |
| **Ferrous fumarate** | about 33% | High iron content per capsule, moderate tolerability |
| **Iron bisglycinate** | about 20% | Chelated iron bound to two glycine molecules. Absorbed via a somewhat different pathway, and its digestive tolerability is significantly better. More expensive |
| **Liposomal iron** | varies | Wrapped in a fat layer. Very good tolerability, but the evidence base for effectiveness is thinner |

**The number that matters is "elemental iron," not the weight of the compound.** A 325 mg ferrous sulfate preparation provides about 65 mg of elemental iron. Comparing products by capsule weight is meaningless.

**The practical recommendation:** anyone who suffers side effects from sulfate — and many do — should switch to bisglycinate before giving up on treatment altogether. Stopping treatment because of discomfort is the most common reason anemia correction fails.

### Dose and timing — the point that changes the most

- **Every other day beats every day.** This is the most significant change in guidelines in recent years. A dose of iron raises the hormone **hepcidin**, which blocks iron absorption for about 24 hours. So a second dose the next day is poorly absorbed. Studies found that a single dose every other day increases **cumulative absorption** while reducing side effects.
- **One dose a day, not split:** by the same hepcidin logic, splitting the dose into three a day harms absorption instead of helping.
- **On an empty stomach, if possible:** absorption is better. Anyone who gets nauseous can take it with a little food — reduced absorption is better than stopping treatment.
- **With [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/):** a glass of citrus juice or 250 mg of vitamin C improves absorption.
- **Keep two hours away** from [coffee](/en/food/coffee/), [tea](/en/food/green-tea/), [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) supplements, [milk](/en/food/milk/) and antacid medications.
- **Treatment length:** correcting hemoglobin takes weeks, but refilling stores takes longer. It is usual to continue for three to six months after hemoglobin normalizes, and to monitor ferritin.

### Downsides and side effects

- **Gastrointestinal side effects:** constipation, nausea, abdominal pain, metallic taste and diarrhea. These are very common — in some studies a third to a half of those taking sulfate — and are the leading reason for stopping treatment. Effective steps: switching to bisglycinate, switching to every-other-day dosing, and increasing fiber and fluids.
- **Black stool:** common, harmless and not worrying. It's important to know about it in advance so you don't confuse it with gastrointestinal bleeding — although black stool that is **sticky and foul-smelling** requires medical evaluation.
- **Tooth staining:** relevant mainly to liquid preparations used in children. Dilute, drink through a straw and brush afterward.
- **Accumulation and chronic toxicity:** because there is no excretion mechanism, prolonged supplementation without monitoring may lead to buildup. In carriers of hemochromatosis, the buildup damages the liver, pancreas and heart.
- **Oxidative stress:** free iron catalyzes the formation of free radicals via the Fenton reaction. This is the theoretical basis for caution about excess.
- **Acute poisoning in children:** iron supplements are among the leading causes of fatal poisoning in young children. Swallowing a large amount is a medical emergency. Store in child-resistant packaging, out of reach.
- **Drug interactions:** iron reduces the absorption of tetracycline and fluoroquinolone antibiotics, levothyroxine (a drug for an underactive thyroid) and levodopa. Separate them by two to four hours.

### When an oral supplement isn't enough

Some patients don't respond to oral supplementation — because of intolerance, poor absorption (celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, after bariatric surgery) or ongoing bleeding that outpaces replacement. In these cases there is **intravenous iron**, given in a medical setting, which can replenish stores in one or two sessions. This is a fully medical decision.

### Summary

An iron supplement is an effective and sometimes lifesaving treatment, but it is not a supplement you take "just to be safe." The three practical points: base the decision on a **ferritin** test, not on feeling tired; choose **bisglycinate** if sulfate causes side effects, instead of stopping treatment; and take it **every other day** rather than every day, which improves cumulative absorption and is easier on the digestive system.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is the difference between iron bisglycinate and iron sulfate?**
Answer: Both forms are effective at correcting iron deficiency, and the main difference is tolerability. Ferrous sulfate is cheap and well studied but causes constipation, nausea and abdominal pain at a high rate. Bisglycinate is chelated iron bound to glycine; its digestive tolerability is much better, and it is more expensive. For anyone who stops taking sulfate because of side effects, bisglycinate is the obvious alternative.

**Question: Should you take an iron supplement every day or every other day?**
Answer: Every other day. A dose of iron raises the hormone hepcidin, which blocks absorption for about 24 hours, so a second daily dose is poorly absorbed. Studies have shown that every-other-day dosing increases cumulative absorption and reduces side effects. The regimen should be set with your treating doctor.

**Question: How long does an iron supplement take to work?**
Answer: Improvement in hemoglobin usually appears within two to four weeks, and fatigue sometimes improves earlier. Refilling the stores themselves is much slower, so it is usual to continue for three to six months after hemoglobin returns to normal, with ferritin tests.

**Question: My iron supplement makes me constipated — what should I do?**
Answer: First, switch to bisglycinate instead of sulfate. Second, switch to every-other-day dosing. Third, increase fiber and fluid intake. Stopping treatment is the worst solution, and it is the most common reason anemia goes uncorrected.

**Question: Why is my stool black since I started iron?**
Answer: It's a common, harmless side effect — unabsorbed iron reaches the large intestine and changes the color of the stool. However, black stool that is sticky and especially foul-smelling may indicate bleeding in the upper digestive tract and requires a medical check.

**Question: Can you take iron and calcium together?**
Answer: Better not. Calcium is the only inhibitor that also blocks animal-source iron, and it reduces absorption of the supplement. Separate them by at least two hours — for example, iron in the morning on an empty stomach and calcium in the evening.
