## Whey protein: a tool for rounding out your diet

Whey protein is a protein derived from [milk](/en/food/milk/) that provides all the essential amino acids and is rich in leucine. The practical advantage of the powder is the ability to top up protein in a convenient, measured serving. You don't have to use it to build muscle, and you shouldn't conclude that fast absorption necessarily leads to more muscle over months. [The ISSN position stand on protein and exercise](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/).

### What does research show about building muscle?

A meta-analysis of 49 studies and 1,863 participants found that protein supplementation can moderately improve gains in fat-free mass and strength following resistance training. The effect added to the effect of training; it did not replace it. In the statistical model, no clear additional gain in fat-free mass was found beyond a total intake of about 1.6 grams of protein per kg per day. [The meta-analysis by Morton and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/).

The number 1.6 is not an absorption limit, not a medical ceiling, and not a guarantee that every trainee will get the same result. It is a group estimate drawn from different studies. Also, the review looked at protein supplementation from various sources, so it does not prove that whey is always better than food or than any [plant-based powder](/en/food/protein-powder/).

### How much protein do you need per day?

The ISSN position stand suggests a range of about 1.4–2.0 grams per kg of body weight per day for most healthy exercisers, from all sources combined. A personal target may vary with the type of training, age, energy intake and medical condition. [ISSN protein recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/).

| Body weight | Daily protein in this range |
|---|---|
| 60 kg | 84–120 grams |
| 70 kg | 98–140 grams |
| 80 kg | 112–160 grams |

These are worked examples, not a prescription. For instance, if you choose a target of 112 grams and regular food provides about 90 grams, the gap is about 22 grams. You can fill it with food, powder or a combination of both. If you already reach your target, an extra serving isn't needed just because you trained that day.

### The post-workout serving and the "anabolic window"

Servings of about 20–40 grams of high-quality protein are common in recommendations and studies, but the right amount isn't the same for everyone. Spreading protein over the day can be useful; total intake matters more than sticking to a window of a few minutes after training. [Protein timing and servings — ISSN](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/).

To plan simply, look at the meal before your workout and the one after it. If a shake is convenient at those times, it's a reasonable option. If a meal is available and more convenient, there's no need to add a separate ritual just to consume powder. The plan should fit your workday, appetite and preferences, not just the time your workout ends.

### Concentrate, isolate and hydrolysate

| Type | What characterizes it | What to check in the product |
|---|---|---|
| Concentrate | Less filtered than isolate; usually contains more lactose and fat | Protein per serving and personal tolerance |
| Isolate | Higher protein percentage and usually less lactose | Don't assume it is lactose-free unless labeled |
| Hydrolysate (partially broken down) | Broken down into smaller protein fragments | The price, and whether there's a real need for this specific product |

Processing methods change the composition, but don't guarantee an advantage in building muscle for every user. [A review of protein sources and their processing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/).

An important distinction is between **lactose intolerance** and **milk allergy**. The first relates to digesting milk sugar; the second to an immune reaction to milk proteins. Isolate may suit some people with lactose intolerance, but it still contains milk protein and is therefore not a solution for an allergy. [NIDDK's explanation of lactose intolerance](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/lactose-intolerance/definition-facts).

### Does powder speed up recovery?

Protein is needed to adapt to training, but there is no guarantee that a shake will prevent muscle soreness or shorten recovery in every situation. Studies on performance, muscle damage and soreness don't give uniform results. An improvement in the muscle protein synthesis response after a meal is not the same as a proven improvement in readiness for the next workout. [A review of protein, performance and recovery](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2018.00083/full).

So it's worth pinning down the problem itself. Hunger after training, difficulty fitting in a meal or low protein intake are different considerations from recurring muscle soreness. Defining it precisely will keep you from buying powder in the hope that it will solve every recovery problem.

### Quality, price and safety

Calculate the price per 25 grams of **protein**, not the price per scoop of powder: two scoops of the same weight may contain different amounts of protein. Check the ingredient list, the allergen labeling and the serving size. Bloating after a shake may justify checking the amount and the composition, and not necessarily switching to the most expensive product.

With kidney disease, protein intake should be adjusted with your care team; a general sports target doesn't automatically suit this situation. [NIDDK — nutrition in chronic kidney disease](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/chronic-kidney-disease-ckd/eating-nutrition).

For competitive athletes, batch testing for banned substances reduces the risk but doesn't eliminate it. Make sure the specific batch appears in the testing body's database. [AIS guidelines](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/athlete-resources).

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Do you need protein powder to build muscle?**
Answer: No. You can meet your protein intake through food; powder is a convenient option when there is a gap or a practical difficulty.

**Question: Does a 30-gram scoop of powder contain 30 grams of protein?**
Answer: Not necessarily. You need to read the amount of protein per serving in the nutrition table.

**Question: Is isolate suitable for people allergic to milk?**
Answer: No. Whey isolate still contains milk protein, even when the amount of lactose is low.
