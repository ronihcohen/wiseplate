Selenium is one of the few minerals where **the margin between a helpful dose and a toxic dose is truly narrow**. The recommended daily intake is 55 mcg, and the tolerable upper intake level is 400 mcg — a ratio of less than eightfold. For comparison, with [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) the ratio is 20-fold or more.

And this is not a theoretical exercise: **a single [Brazil nut](/en/food/brazil-nuts/) can contain 68-91 mcg of selenium, and sometimes much more.** One or two nuts a day cover the entire requirement; a daily handful, as a habit, can reach the toxic range.

### Dosage and sources

| Measure | Value |

| Pregnancy / breastfeeding | 60 / 70 mcg |
| Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) | 400 mcg per day |

## Benefits (Pros)

### 1. The thyroid — the highest concentration in the body
The thyroid gland contains **the highest concentration of selenium per gram of tissue** in the whole body. The reason: the **deiodinase** enzymes, which convert the hormone T4 into its active form T3, are selenoproteins. Without selenium, the conversion is impaired — and this holds even when [iodine](/en/food/supplements/iodine/) is adequate.

### 2. Glutathione peroxidase
Selenium is the core of this family of enzymes, which neutralizes hydrogen peroxide and lipid peroxides. It is one of the body's main antioxidant defense mechanisms, and it **works in partnership with [vitamin E](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-e/)** — each partially compensates for a deficiency of the other.

### 3. Immune and antiviral function
Selenium deficiency has been linked to a weakened immune response. An interesting documented phenomenon: **in a selenium-deficient host, certain viruses mutate into more virulent forms** — a finding described in coxsackievirus studies.

### 4. Hashimoto's thyroiditis
Several controlled trials found that **selenium supplementation (usually 200 mcg a day) lowers TPO antibody levels** in people with Hashimoto's. The finding is relatively consistent, but **its clinical meaning — whether it changes the course of the disease or the need for medication — is still unclear.**

## Downsides and Side Effects (Cons)

### 1. Selenosis — a narrow safety margin
Prolonged excess intake causes **selenosis**: hair loss, brittle nails, rash, a characteristic [garlic](/en/food/garlic/) breath odor (from dimethyl selenide in exhaled air), digestive disturbances and, in severe cases, neuropathy. **Documented poisonings come mainly from faulty supplements and excessive consumption of Brazil nuts.**

### 2. The SELECT trial — the result that reversed the direction
The SELECT trial, which included more than 35,000 men, tested whether selenium and [vitamin E](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-e/) prevent prostate cancer. **It was stopped early: no benefit at all was found from selenium, and the vitamin E arm showed a 17% increase in prostate cancer incidence.** This is one of the most cited cases of a supplement considered "can't hurt" that turned out otherwise.

### 3. Risk of type 2 diabetes
Several studies in populations **already getting enough selenium** found a link between supplementation and an increased risk of type 2 diabetes. The finding is not conclusive, but it reinforces the rule: **supplementation is justified only in genuine deficiency.**

### 4. Soil dependence — why there is no single number
The selenium content of food depends almost entirely on **the amount in the soil where the crop was grown**, and it varies by orders of magnitude between regions. In selenium-poor regions of China, **Keshan disease** has been described — a cardiomyopathy caused by deficiency. Conversely, endemic selenosis has been described in selenium-rich regions. **This is also the reason for the huge gap in the content of Brazil nuts** — it all depends on the grove.

### 5. Interactions
High doses may affect the activity of other minerals, and an effect on [insulin](/en/food/supplements/insulin/) sensitivity has been described.

## Summary

Selenium is essential for the thyroid, the antioxidant defense system and immune function — **and it is very easy to get from food**: a daily serving of [tuna](/en/food/tuna/), [sardines](/en/food/sardines/) or [eggs](/en/food/eggs/) covers the requirement. The most important practical rule is **not to think of it as "just another antioxidant"**: the SELECT trial showed that supplementation in a population that isn't deficient doesn't help, and may even harm. And as for [Brazil nuts](/en/food/brazil-nuts/) — **one to two nuts a day, not a handful.**

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much selenium do you need per day?**
Answer: The recommended daily intake for adults is 55 mcg (60 in pregnancy, 70 when breastfeeding), and the tolerable upper intake level is 400 mcg. This is one of the narrowest ranges between a helpful and a toxic dose in the world of minerals — less than eightfold between the two.

**Question: How many Brazil nuts can you eat per day?**
Answer: One to two. A single Brazil nut contains 68-91 mcg of selenium, i.e. more than the entire daily intake, and sometimes much more — the content depends on the soil where the tree grew and varies by orders of magnitude. A daily handful as a regular habit can reach the toxic range and cause selenosis.

**Question: What is selenosis?**
Answer: Selenium poisoning from prolonged excess intake. Symptoms include hair loss, brittle nails, rash, digestive disturbances and a characteristic garlic breath odor — caused by dimethyl selenide in exhaled air. In severe cases neuropathy develops. The documented sources are mainly wrongly dosed supplements and excessive consumption of Brazil nuts.

**Question: Does selenium help the thyroid?**
Answer: It is essential to it. The thyroid contains the body's highest concentration of selenium, and the deiodinase enzymes that convert T4 into active T3 are selenoproteins. In Hashimoto's, controlled trials found that supplementing 200 mcg a day lowers TPO antibodies — but the clinical meaning of this drop is still unclear, and there is no evidence that it changes the course of the disease.

**Question: Should you take a selenium supplement to prevent cancer?**
Answer: No. The SELECT trial, which included over 35,000 men, tested exactly this question and was stopped early: no benefit from selenium was found, and the vitamin E arm showed a 17% increase in prostate cancer incidence. Other studies have even linked supplementation in a population that isn't deficient to an increased risk of type 2 diabetes.

**Question: Which foods are rich in selenium?**
Answer: Brazil nuts by a huge margin (68-91 mcg per nut), followed by fish and seafood — tuna 90 mcg per 100 g, oysters 77, sardines 53. Chicken breast (25 mcg per 100 g) and eggs (15 mcg each) also contribute. A serving of fish or two eggs a day easily covers intake.

**Question: Why does the amount of selenium in food vary so much?**
Answer: Because it depends almost entirely on the amount of selenium in the soil where the crop was grown, which varies by orders of magnitude between geographic regions. In selenium-poor regions of China, Keshan disease — a cardiomyopathy caused by deficiency — has been described; in rich regions, endemic selenosis. This is also why no exact number can be given for a Brazil nut.
