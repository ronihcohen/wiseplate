Folic acid is one of the great success stories of public health: **supplementation before pregnancy reduces the incidence of neural tube defects — spina bifida and anencephaly — by 50-70%.**

**And within that success there is a critical point many people miss: timing.** The neural tube closes on **days 21-28 of pregnancy** — usually **before the woman knows she is pregnant**. A supplement started at the first doctor's visit is **too late**.

Hence the blanket recommendation: **every woman of childbearing age who does not rule out pregnancy needs 400 mcg per day** — not only those who are planning.

**The name itself tells the story:** "folate" comes from *folium* — Latin for leaf.

## Benefits (Pros)

### 1. Preventing neural tube defects
This is its best-established role, and it has almost no parallel in nutrition in terms of the strength of the evidence. **Supplementation in the period before conception and in the first trimester reduces the incidence of these defects by 50-70%.**

### 2. DNA synthesis and cell division
Folate carries single-carbon units and is needed to make DNA bases. **The tissues hit first by deficiency are those that turn over quickly** — the bone marrow and the lining of the digestive tract. Hence the megaloblastic anemia of deficiency.

### 3. Breaking down homocysteine
Together with [B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/) and [B6](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b6/). **An important caveat:** large trials showed that lowering homocysteine with supplements **does not reduce cardiac events** — high homocysteine is probably a marker, not a cause.

### 4. National fortification programs
Countries that fortified flour with folic acid — the US, Canada and about 80 other countries — **saw an immediate, measurable drop in the incidence of neural tube defects.** It is one of the most successful moves in the history of public health.

## Downsides and side effects (Cons)

### 1. Masking [vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/) deficiency
**The most significant caveat.** High-dose folic acid **corrects the anemia caused by B12 deficiency — but not the nerve damage.** The result: the blood test looks normal, and meanwhile the damage to the spinal cord progresses quietly and may become irreversible. **This is the reason for the Tolerable Upper Intake Level of 1,000 mcg, and why you don't take a high dose without checking B12.**

### 2. Three different forms — and it matters
* **Folate** — the natural form in food. Less stable, destroyed by cooking, and about 50% absorbed.
* **Folic acid** — the synthetic form in supplements and fortified foods. Stable and absorbed at about 85% efficiency. **It requires conversion in the body** by the enzyme DHFR.
* **5-MTHF (methylfolate)** — the active form, available as a supplement and needing no conversion.

**This is why the dose is measured in DFE** (dietary folate equivalents) rather than raw micrograms.

### 3. MTHFR — far more noise than substance
A polymorphism in the **MTHFR** gene (especially C677T) reduces the efficiency of conversion to the active form, and it is **very common — up to 10-15% of the population carry two copies.**

**What is true:** carriers with two copies may show slightly higher homocysteine.
**What is not supported:** the widespread online advice that MTHFR carriers "can't process folic acid" and must take 5-MTHF specifically. **Professional organizations do not recommend routine MTHFR testing**, and the recommendation for supplementation in pregnancy is the same for carriers and non-carriers. If you prefer 5-MTHF, there's no harm in it, but there's no need for it either.

### 4. Unmetabolized folic acid in the blood
Very high doses may leave unconverted folic acid in the bloodstream. **The health significance of this finding is unknown**, and it is the subject of ongoing research — not a reason to worry about the recommended doses.

### 5. Drug interactions
Methotrexate, phenytoin and phenobarbital, and sulfasalazine — **all interfere with folate metabolism**. Patients on methotrexate usually receive a controlled folic acid supplement to reduce side effects.

### 6. Deficiency — when it does happen
With alcohol addiction, celiac disease and inflammatory bowel diseases, pregnancy, hemodialysis and the drugs listed above. The symptoms: megaloblastic anemia, fatigue, mouth ulcers and a red, inflamed tongue.

## Summary

Folic acid is one of the few supplements whose recommendation is **universal rather than conditional on deficiency**: every woman of childbearing age who does not rule out pregnancy needs **400 mcg per day, on an ongoing basis**. The reason is timing — **the neural tube closes in the third to fourth week, usually before the pregnancy is known.** Two practical caveats: **don't take a high dose without checking [B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/)**, because folic acid masks the anemia and leaves the nerve damage; and **the noise around MTHFR far exceeds its significance** — the recommendation is the same for carriers and non-carriers.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: When should you start taking folic acid?**
Answer: Before conception, and that is the critical point. The neural tube closes on days 21-28 of pregnancy — usually before the woman knows she is pregnant — so a supplement started at the first doctor's visit is too late. The recommendation is that every woman of childbearing age who does not rule out pregnancy take 400 mcg per day on an ongoing basis.

**Question: What's the difference between folate, folic acid and 5-MTHF?**
Answer: Folate is the natural form in food — less stable, destroyed by cooking, and about 50% absorbed. Folic acid is the synthetic form in supplements and fortified foods, stable and about 85% absorbed, but it requires conversion in the body. 5-MTHF (methylfolate) is the active form that needs no conversion. This is why the dose is measured in DFE units rather than raw micrograms.

**Question: Do MTHFR carriers need a special supplement?**
Answer: According to professional bodies — no. The polymorphism is very common (up to 10-15% of the population carry two copies) and slightly reduces conversion efficiency, but the recommendation for supplementation in pregnancy is the same for carriers and non-carriers, and routine MTHFR testing is not recommended. If you prefer 5-MTHF, there's no harm in it, but there's no need for it either.

**Question: Why is it dangerous to take folic acid without checking B12?**
Answer: Because it corrects the anemia caused by B12 deficiency without correcting the nerve damage. The blood test looks normal, and meanwhile the damage to the spinal cord progresses quietly and may become irreversible. That is why the Tolerable Upper Intake Level was set at 1,000 mcg — it is meant to prevent exactly this scenario.

**Question: Which foods are rich in folate?**
Answer: The name gives it away — "folate" comes from folium, Latin for leaf. The leaders are raw spinach (194 mcg per 100 grams), cooked lentils (181), mung beans (159), asparagus (149), avocado (81), broccoli (63) and cauliflower (57). Legumes and leafy greens are the strong categories, and beef liver provides 290 mcg.

**Question: How much folic acid do you need in pregnancy?**
Answer: 600 mcg DFE per day in pregnancy and 500 while breastfeeding, compared with 400 normally. Women with a previous pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect receive a much higher dose — 4,000-5,000 mcg — but only on a doctor's instructions, not on their own initiative.

**Question: Does folic acid prevent heart disease?**
Answer: No, even though the theory sounded logical. Folate breaks down homocysteine, and high homocysteine is linked to cardiovascular risk — but large trials showed that lowering it with supplements does not reduce cardiac events. The accepted explanation is that homocysteine is a marker, not a cause.

**Question: Who is at risk of folate deficiency?**
Answer: Pregnant women, people with alcohol addiction, people with celiac disease and inflammatory bowel diseases, hemodialysis patients, and those taking certain drugs — methotrexate, phenytoin, phenobarbital and sulfasalazine. Symptoms include megaloblastic anemia, fatigue, mouth ulcers and a red, inflamed tongue.
