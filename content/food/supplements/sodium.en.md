Sodium is the main positive ion **outside** the cells, just as [potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/) is the main one inside them. It determines the volume of extracellular fluid, enables nerve conduction and muscle contraction, and drives transporters that bring glucose and amino acids into cells.

**It is absolutely essential. The problem is the amount.**

Within that, two practical points change the whole picture:

1. **About 70-75% of the sodium in the Western diet doesn't come from the salt shaker.** It is already in bread, cheese, processed meat, sauces, canned goods and restaurant food. **What this means: cutting salt in your cooking is a quarter of the problem.**
2. **Sea salt, Himalayan salt and table salt contain almost the same amount of sodium by weight.** The difference is in crystal size, color and price — not in health.

### Dosage and sources

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| World Health Organization recommendation | **less than 2,000 mg sodium per day** (5 g salt) |
| Optimal target for heart health (AHA) | 1,500 mg per day |
| Actual average intake | 3,000-4,500 mg per day |
| Minimum physiological requirement | ~500 mg per day |

**A conversion worth knowing:** salt is sodium chloride. **1 g of salt = 400 mg of sodium.** A teaspoon of salt (about 6 g) = **2,400 mg of sodium** — more than the entire daily allowance.

## Benefits (Pros)

### 1. Fluid volume and normal blood pressure
Sodium determines how much water stays in the extracellular space. **Without it, blood volume and normal blood pressure cannot be maintained** — which is why a sharp drop in sodium is no less dangerous than an excess.

### 2. Nerve conduction and muscle contraction
The sodium-potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase) creates the resting potential of every excitable cell. **The action potential itself is an inward flow of sodium** — the basis of every nerve signal and every muscle contraction.

### 3. Absorbing nutrients
Sodium-dependent transporters (SGLT) bring glucose and amino acids into intestinal cells. **This is also the basis of oral rehydration solutions (ORS)** for diarrhea — the solution combines salt and sugar precisely to exploit this mechanism.

### 4. Physical performance in prolonged effort
Sweat contains 400-1,500 mg of sodium per liter. **In activity lasting more than two hours, especially in the heat, replacing sodium matters** — and heavy sweaters need it more.

### 5. Iodized salt
In some countries iodized table salt is a major source of [iodine](/en/food/supplements/iodine/) — although **Israel has no mandatory iodization**, and that is an entirely separate consideration.

## Downsides and Side Effects (Cons)

### 1. Blood pressure — the established link
High sodium intake raises blood pressure, and reducing intake lowers it. **The DASH-Sodium trial showed this clearly**, and the effect is stronger in people who already have hypertension, in older adults and in certain populations. Hypertension is a leading risk factor for stroke, cardiac events and kidney disease.

### 2. "Salt sensitivity" — the explanation for the debate
**Not everyone responds to the same degree.** About a third of the population is classed as "salt-sensitive," and in them the change in blood pressure is marked; in others it is small. Sensitivity rises with age, excess weight and kidney disease. **Because there is no readily available test for sensitivity, the public recommendation applies across the board.**

### 3. Additional risks beyond blood pressure
* **Stomach cancer** — high salt intake, and especially pickled and smoked foods, has been consistently linked to increased risk.
* **Kidney stones and [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) loss** — excess sodium increases urinary calcium excretion.
* **Worse fluid retention** in heart failure and cirrhosis.

### 4. Sea salt and Himalayan salt — the myth
**This is one of the most common misconceptions in the food aisle.** Sea salt, Himalayan salt and table salt are all **97-99% sodium chloride**. The "extra" minerals in Himalayan salt are present in tiny amounts with no nutritional significance.

**The only nuance that does exist:** coarse salt with large crystals takes up more volume, so **a teaspoon of coarse salt contains less sodium than a teaspoon of fine salt** — but **by equal weight, the amount is the same.** See [the comparison of table, sea and Himalayan salt](/en/compare/table-himalayan-sea-salt/).

### 5. Hyponatremia — the opposite direction
**Sodium that is too low is dangerous, and sometimes fatal.** Symptoms: confusion, headaches, nausea, seizures and brain swelling.

The main causes:
* **Drinking too much water during prolonged exertion** — "exercise-associated hyponatremia," a known phenomenon in marathons and triathlons. **Runners affected by it drank too much water, not too little.**
* **Diuretic drugs**, especially thiazides.
* **SIADH**, heart failure, cirrhosis and kidney disease.

### 6. Salt substitutes — not for everyone
Salt substitutes based on [potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/) chloride reduced stroke and mortality in a large trial. **But they are dangerous for kidney patients and for people taking ACE inhibitors, angiotensin blockers or potassium-sparing diuretics.**

## Summary

Sodium is essential, and most people consume twice the recommended amount. **The most effective tool for cutting back is not the salt shaker** — 70-75% of sodium is already in [bread](/en/food/sourdough/), cheese, [processed meat](/en/food/salami/), sauces and restaurant food. **Cutting processed food does more than any change in cooking.** Two myths worth letting go of: **sea salt and Himalayan salt are not "healthier"** — they are 97-99% sodium chloride, just like table salt; and **less sodium is not always better** — hyponatremia from exertion and excessive water drinking is a real danger. And finally: **increasing [potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/) works alongside cutting sodium**, and sometimes even better.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much sodium is OK per day?**
Answer: The World Health Organization recommends less than 2,000 mg of sodium a day — that is, 5 g of salt — and the optimal target for heart health is 1,500 mg. Actual average intake is 3,000-4,500 mg. To illustrate: one teaspoon of salt (6 g) contains 2,400 mg of sodium, more than the entire daily allowance.

**Question: Is sea salt or Himalayan salt healthier?**
Answer: No. All kinds of salt — table, sea and Himalayan — are 97-99% sodium chloride, and the "extra" minerals are present in tiny amounts without nutritional significance. The only nuance is that coarse crystals take up more volume, so a teaspoon of coarse salt contains less sodium than a teaspoon of fine salt — but by equal weight, the amount is the same.

**Question: Where does most of the sodium in the diet come from?**
Answer: About 70-75% comes from processed and restaurant food, not the salt shaker. The main sources are processed meat and sausages (1,200-1,800 mg per 100 g), crackers (1,100), yellow cheese (600-1,000), frozen pizza (550-650) and bread (450-550). The practical upshot: cutting processed food does more than any change in home cooking.

**Question: Is everyone equally sensitive to salt?**
Answer: No. About a third of the population is classed as "salt-sensitive," and in them the change in blood pressure following a change in intake is marked; in others it is small. Sensitivity rises with age, excess weight and kidney disease. Because there is no readily available test that identifies sensitivity, the public recommendation remains across the board.

**Question: Is too little sodium dangerous?**
Answer: Yes. Hyponatremia causes confusion, headaches, nausea, seizures and brain swelling, and it can be fatal. The most surprising cause is drinking too much water during prolonged exertion — "exercise-associated hyponatremia" in marathons and triathlons, caused by too much water rather than too little salt. Other causes are diuretic drugs, heart failure and kidney disease.

**Question: How much sodium does an athlete need?**
Answer: Sweat contains 400-1,500 mg of sodium per liter, so in activity lasting over two hours — especially in the heat — replacing sodium matters, not just water. People differ greatly in sweat rate and in sweat sodium concentration. For short or moderate activity there is no special need for replacement.

**Question: Are salt substitutes recommended?**
Answer: At the population level they have shown benefit — a large trial found a reduction in stroke and mortality using a potassium chloride-based substitute. But they are dangerous for anyone with kidney disease or taking ACE inhibitors, angiotensin blockers or potassium-sparing diuretics, in whom potassium may build up to dangerous levels. It's a decision worth discussing with a professional.

**Question: What is the most effective way to cut sodium?**
Answer: Cut processed food, rather than focusing on the salt shaker — which accounts for only about a quarter of sodium. At the same time, increase potassium from vegetables, legumes and fruit: the ratio between the two predicts blood pressure better than either one alone. And in cooking, herbs, acidity and spices take over much of salt's role in flavor.
