## Sodium

**Sodium is an essential mineral that regulates fluid volume and blood pressure, and the problem with it is almost always too much. The World Health Organization recommends that adults eat less than 2,000 mg of sodium a day — about 5 g of salt — while the global average is double that, and most of it comes from processed and restaurant food rather than the salt shaker.** In Hebrew it is "נתרן". [World Health Organization — sodium reduction](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/sodium-reduction).

Sodium is the main positive ion outside the cells, just as [potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/) is the main one inside them. It sets the volume of extracellular fluid, enables nerve conduction and muscle contraction, and drives transporters that bring glucose and amino acids into cells. Table salt is sodium chloride, and about 40% of its weight is sodium: 1 g of salt contains about 400 mg of sodium.

### How much per day

| Body | Recommendation for adults |
|---|---|
| World Health Organization (WHO) | Less than 2,000 mg sodium a day (less than 5 g salt) |
| Children, per WHO | Less than that, scaled to energy needs by age |
| American Heart Association — ideal target | 1,500 mg a day |
| Global average intake (2021) | About 4,300 mg a day (about 11 g salt) |
| Physiological need | A few hundred mg a day |

WHO's formal guideline rests on reviews of controlled trials showing that cutting sodium lowers blood pressure without harming blood lipids or kidney function. [WHO guideline on sodium intake](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241504836). A teaspoon of fine salt weighs 5–6 g, so it holds the whole daily allowance.

### Where sodium comes from

In a study in which 450 US adults recorded everything they ate and even collected samples of the salt they added at home, about 71% of sodium came from food that had sodium added outside the home — processed and restaurant food. Salt added in home cooking and at the table together made up only about 10%. [Harnack et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28483828/).

| Source | Sodium per 100 g |
|---|---|
| [Salami](/en/food/salami/) / [sausages](/en/food/sausages/) | 1,200–1,800 mg |
| White-flour [crackers](/en/food/white-flour-crackers/) | about 1,100 mg |
| Yellow [cheese](/en/food/cheese/) | 600–1,000 mg |
| [Frozen pizza](/en/food/frozen-pizza/) | 550–650 mg |
| Bread | 450–550 mg |
| [Instant soup](/en/food/instant-soup/) (prepared) | 300–400 mg |
| [Celery](/en/food/celery/) — among the saltiest fresh vegetables | about 80 mg |

### Benefits

- **Fluid volume and normal blood pressure:** Without sodium the body cannot maintain blood volume and blood pressure, so a sharp drop in sodium is as dangerous as excess.
- **Nerves and muscles:** The sodium–potassium pump creates the cell's resting voltage, and every nerve signal begins with sodium flowing in.
- **Nutrient absorption:** Sodium-dependent transporters carry glucose into gut cells. That is the basis of oral rehydration solutions (ORS) for diarrhoea, which combine salt and sugar.
- **Long exercise:** Sweat carries sodium, in amounts that vary greatly between people. In activity lasting more than two hours, especially in the heat, replacing sodium matters.
- **Iodised salt:** In many countries it is a major source of [iodine](/en/food/supplements/iodine/), though Israel has no mandatory iodisation.

### Downsides and side effects

- **Blood pressure:** In a meta-analysis of 34 randomised trials, cutting salt by about 4.4 g a day lowered systolic blood pressure by about 4 mmHg on average, and more in people with hypertension. [He et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23558162/). In the DASH-Sodium trial, lowering sodium reduced blood pressure even in people with normal blood pressure, and combined with the DASH diet the drop reached about 11.5 mmHg in people with hypertension. [Sacks et al., 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11136953/).
- **Salt sensitivity:** Not everyone responds equally. Sensitivity is higher in older people, people with hypertension and people with kidney disease, and since there is no simple test to identify it, public advice applies to everyone.
- **Other risks:** Diets high in salted and pickled foods have been linked to stomach cancer, and excess sodium increases urinary [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) loss and worsens fluid retention in heart failure and cirrhosis.
- **Sea and Himalayan salt:** All salts are overwhelmingly sodium chloride. The "extra" minerals in Himalayan salt are negligible in the amounts people eat. A teaspoon of coarse salt holds less sodium than a teaspoon of fine salt only because the crystals take up more space — by equal weight the amount is nearly the same. See [the comparison of table, sea and Himalayan salt](/en/compare/table-himalayan-sea-salt/).
- **Hyponatraemia — the opposite problem:** Low blood sodium causes confusion, headache, nausea, seizures and brain swelling, and can be fatal. At the Boston Marathon, 13% of runners tested finished with low sodium, and the main risk factors were weight gain during the race (that is, drinking too much) and a finishing time over 4 hours. [Almond et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15829535/). Other causes: thiazide diuretics, heart failure, cirrhosis and kidney disease.
- **Salt substitutes:** A substitute in which part of the sodium was replaced by potassium reduced stroke and death in a large trial in China. [Neal et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34459569/). But it is dangerous for people with kidney disease and for those taking ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers or potassium-sparing diuretics.

### Practical use

- **Read labels:** Compare sodium per 100 g between similar products — the differences in bread, cheese and processed meat are large.
- **Less processed and restaurant food:** That is where most sodium is, so this is the most effective step.
- **In cooking:** Herbs, lemon, vinegar and spices take over part of salt's job in flavour. Taste buds adjust to less salt within a few weeks.
- **More potassium:** Vegetables, legumes and fruit improve the sodium-to-potassium balance.
- **Athletes:** In long efforts in the heat, drink to thirst and add sodium, rather than drinking large amounts of plain water "just in case".

### Summary

Sodium is essential, but most people eat about twice the recommended amount. Cutting it lowers blood pressure, and about 70% of sodium comes from processed and restaurant food, so that is where the most effective step lies — not at the salt shaker. Sea and Himalayan salt are no healthier than table salt. And less is not always better: drinking too much water during long exercise can cause dangerous hyponatraemia.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much sodium is OK per day?**
Answer: The World Health Organization recommends less than 2,000 mg of sodium a day — under 5 g of salt, roughly a teaspoon. The American Heart Association sets an ideal target of 1,500 mg. The global average is about 4,300 mg.

**Question: Is sea salt or Himalayan salt healthier?**
Answer: No. They are all overwhelmingly sodium chloride, and the extra minerals are negligible. A teaspoon of coarse salt has less sodium only because the crystals are bigger; by equal weight the amount is nearly the same.

**Question: Where does most dietary sodium come from?**
Answer: Processed and restaurant food — about 70% in a US study that measured it directly. Salt added at home is only about a tenth. Bread, cheese, processed meat, sauces and ready meals are the big sources.

**Question: Is everyone equally sensitive to salt?**
Answer: No. Older people, people with hypertension and people with kidney disease respond more. But in the DASH-Sodium trial, cutting sodium lowered blood pressure even in people with normal blood pressure, so the advice applies to everyone.

**Question: Is too little sodium dangerous?**
Answer: Yes. Hyponatraemia causes confusion, nausea, seizures and brain swelling. The surprising cause is drinking too much during long exercise: at the Boston Marathon 13% of runners tested finished with low sodium, mostly those who gained weight during the race.

**Question: How much sodium does an athlete need?**
Answer: It depends heavily on sweat volume and sweat sodium, which vary between people. In activity lasting more than two hours, especially in the heat, replace sodium and not just water; in short sessions there is no special need.

**Question: Are salt substitutes recommended?**
Answer: For most people, yes — a potassium-based substitute reduced stroke and death in a large trial. But it is dangerous for people with kidney disease and for those taking ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers or potassium-sparing diuretics, so ask first.

**Question: What is the most effective way to cut sodium?**
Answer: Eat less processed and restaurant food, compare labels, and get more potassium from vegetables and legumes. In cooking, herbs, lemon and spices can replace part of the salt.
