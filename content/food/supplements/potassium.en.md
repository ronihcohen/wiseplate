## Potassium

**Potassium is the mineral that balances sodium and directly affects blood pressure. Adults need 2,600 mg (women) to 3,400 mg (men) a day, and most people get less. The gap is closed with vegetables, legumes and fruit — not a supplement — while people with kidney disease or on certain drugs need to watch out for too much.** In Hebrew it is "אשלגן". [NIH fact sheet on potassium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Potassium-HealthProfessional/).

Potassium is the main positive ion inside cells, just as [sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) is the main one outside them. The difference between the two across the cell membrane underlies every heartbeat, muscle contraction and nerve signal. The Western diet is the reverse of the one humans evolved on: lots of sodium from processed food and little potassium from plants.

### How much you need a day

| Age | Adequate intake a day (AI) |
|---|---|
| Infants 0–6 months | 400 mg |
| Infants 7–12 months | 860 mg |
| 1–3 | 2,000 mg |
| 4–8 | 2,300 mg |
| 9–13 | 2,500 mg (boys) / 2,300 mg (girls) |
| 14–18 | 3,000 mg (boys) / 2,300 mg (girls) |
| Men 19+ | 3,400 mg |
| Women 19+ | 2,600 mg |
| Pregnancy | 2,900 mg (2,600 up to age 18) |
| Breastfeeding | 2,800 mg (2,500 up to age 18) |

No upper limit has been set for potassium from food, because healthy kidneys excrete the excess. The World Health Organization recommends at least 3,510 mg a day for adults. [WHO guideline on potassium intake](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241504829). The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) set 3,500 mg.

### Food sources

| Source | Potassium per 100 g |

| Dried [figs](/en/food/figs/) | about 680 mg |
| Raw [spinach](/en/food/spinach/) | about 560 mg |
| [Baked potato](/en/food/baked-potato/) with skin | about 535 mg |
| [Avocado](/en/food/avocado/) | about 485 mg |
| [Banana](/en/food/banana/) | about 360 mg |
| Cooked [black beans](/en/food/black-beans/) | about 355 mg |
| [Pumpkin](/en/food/pumpkin/) | about 340 mg |

Bananas are a good source but not an exceptional one: according to NIH, a medium baked potato provides about 610 mg and a cup of cooked lentils about 730 mg, against about 420 mg in a medium banana. Variety also wins — leafy greens, legumes, root vegetables and dried fruit together supply far more than any single food.

### Benefits

- **Blood pressure:** A meta-analysis of 22 randomised trials found that higher potassium intake lowered systolic blood pressure by about 3.5 mmHg on average — an effect seen in people with high blood pressure but not in those with normal blood pressure. [Aburto et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23558164/). The mechanism: more sodium excreted in urine and relaxation of the blood vessel wall.
- **Stroke:** In the same review, cohort studies linked higher potassium intake to a 24% lower risk of stroke.
- **Potassium salt substitute:** In the SSaSS trial of about 21,000 adults in rural China, with a history of stroke or with high blood pressure over age 60, a salt in which a quarter of the sodium chloride was replaced by potassium chloride reduced stroke by 14% and death by 12%. [Neal et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34459569/).
- **Muscle and heart:** A cell's resting voltage depends on potassium, so both low and high blood levels disturb heart rhythm.
- **Bone and kidney stones:** Potassium from fruit and vegetables reduces urinary loss of [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/), which may support bone balance and reduce kidney stones.

### Downsides and side effects

- **Kidney disease:** The kidney is potassium's main regulator. In chronic kidney disease potassium builds up, and excess (hyperkalaemia) causes weakness, rhythm disturbances and, in extreme cases, cardiac arrest. Kidney patients are often told to limit potassium — that is, the very foods recommended to everyone else.
- **Drugs that raise potassium:** ACE inhibitors (such as enalapril and ramipril), angiotensin receptor blockers (such as losartan), potassium-sparing diuretics (such as spironolactone) and anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Anyone taking them should not add a supplement or salt substitute without asking their doctor.
- **Drugs that lower potassium:** Loop and thiazide diuretics increase potassium loss, so sometimes the doctor recommends topping it up.
- **Supplements:** In the US, over-the-counter supplements usually contain up to 99 mg per capsule — about 3% of the need — partly because concentrated potassium chloride tablets were once linked to small-bowel injury. So a supplement is not an effective way to close the gap.
- **Deficiency (hypokalaemia):** Usually caused not by diet but by vomiting, diarrhoea, diuretics or laxative overuse. Symptoms: muscle weakness, cramps, constipation and heart rhythm problems.

### Practical use

- **Half the plate vegetables:** Legumes, potatoes with skin, leafy greens, tomatoes and avocado are the main way to reach the target.
- **Less processed food:** Cutting sodium improves the sodium-to-potassium balance, which is what affects blood pressure.
- **Salt substitute:** Helpful for most people, especially those with high blood pressure, but dangerous for kidney patients and people on potassium-raising drugs. Ask before switching.
- **Blood tests:** In people taking diuretics or ACE inhibitors, the doctor monitors potassium; there is no need to guess.

### Summary

Potassium is essential for the heart, muscles and nerves, and most people eat less than recommended. Raising intake lowers blood pressure in people with hypertension and has been linked to fewer strokes, and a potassium salt substitute reduced stroke and death in a large trial. The way there is vegetables, legumes and fruit, not a pill. The warning: people with kidney disease and those taking ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers or potassium-sparing diuretics should be careful with supplements and salt substitutes.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much potassium do I need a day?**
Answer: The adequate intake is 3,400 mg for men and 2,600 mg for women. The World Health Organization recommends at least 3,510 mg a day, and most people get less.

**Question: Are bananas a good source of potassium?**
Answer: Good, but not exceptional. A medium banana provides about 420 mg, a medium baked potato about 610 mg and a cup of cooked lentils about 730 mg. Per 100 g, spinach, avocado, dried figs and tomato paste provide more.

**Question: Why do potassium supplements contain so little?**
Answer: Over-the-counter supplements usually contain up to 99 mg per capsule, partly because concentrated potassium tablets were once linked to small-bowel injury and because excess potassium is dangerous for the heart. In practice, food is the way to reach the target.

**Question: Who should be careful with potassium?**
Answer: People with chronic kidney disease, and people who regularly take ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, potassium-sparing diuretics or NSAIDs. For them, supplements and salt substitutes can push potassium to dangerous levels.

**Question: Are potassium-based salt substitutes recommended?**
Answer: For most people, yes. In a large trial in China they reduced stroke and death without a clear rise in hyperkalaemia, but the participants did not have kidney disease. Anyone with kidney disease or on potassium-raising drugs should ask before using one.

**Question: What causes potassium deficiency?**
Answer: Usually losses rather than diet: vomiting, prolonged diarrhoea, diuretics or laxative overuse. A significant deficiency needs medical evaluation, not self-prescribed supplements.

**Question: How can I get more potassium from food?**
Answer: Add legumes, leafy greens, root vegetables or fruit to every meal. Baked potatoes with skin, lentils, beans, spinach, avocado, tomatoes and dried fruit are strong sources.
