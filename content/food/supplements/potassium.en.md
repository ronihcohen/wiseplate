Potassium is the main positive ion **inside** cells, just as [sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) is the main one outside them. The difference between the two on either side of the cell membrane is the **resting potential** — the basis of every heartbeat, every muscle contraction and every nerve signal.

Two practical points worth opening with:

1. **The picture is not "how much potassium" but "what is the ratio to sodium."** Studies show again and again that the potassium-to-sodium ratio predicts blood pressure and cardiovascular risk better than either one alone. **The Western diet is the reverse of the one humans evolved on** — lots of processed sodium and little potassium from plants.

2. **[Bananas](/en/food/banana/) are not the best source of potassium.** They provide 358 mg per 100 g — less than [potatoes](/en/food/baked-potato/), [beans](/en/food/black-beans/), cooked [spinach](/en/food/spinach/), [avocado](/en/food/avocado/) or [tomato](/en/food/tomato/) paste. Their reputation is a marketing success, not a nutritional one.

## Benefits (Pros)

### 1. Blood pressure — the best-established effect
Increasing potassium intake lowers blood pressure, especially in people with hypertension and high sodium intake. The mechanism is twofold: **increased sodium excretion in the urine (natriuresis)** and relaxation of smooth muscle in blood vessel walls. This is one of the dietary interventions with the strongest evidence base in the field.

### 2. Lower stroke risk
Meta-analyses of cohort studies have found a consistent link between higher potassium intake and a **lower risk of stroke** — an effect that appears stronger than the one on cardiac events.

### 3. Muscle and heart function
The cell's resting potential depends on the potassium gradient. **Abnormalities in either direction — hypokalemia and hyperkalemia — disrupt heart rhythm**, and at the extreme can cause life-threatening arrhythmias.

### 4. Bone and kidney stones
High potassium intake, especially from fruits and vegetables, is linked to **reduced urinary excretion of [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/)** — which supports bone balance and lowers the risk of kidney stones.

## Downsides and Side Effects (Cons)

### 1. Potassium supplements — why they are restricted by law
Over-the-counter potassium supplements are **limited in most countries to about 99 mg per capsule** — around three percent of daily intake. The reason is twofold: **concentrated potassium pills have caused ulcers and perforations in the small intestine**, and excess potassium in the blood is dangerous to the heart. **The practical conclusion: get your potassium from food, not from a bottle.**

### 2. Kidney disease — the serious risk
The kidney is the main regulator of potassium. **In chronic kidney disease this ability is impaired, and potassium builds up.** **Hyperkalemia** causes weakness, arrhythmias and, at the extreme, cardiac arrest. Kidney patients are usually told to **limit** potassium — that is, to avoid precisely the foods recommended to everyone else.

### 3. Medications that raise potassium
ACE inhibitors (enalapril, ramipril), angiotensin receptor blockers (losartan), potassium-sparing diuretics (spironolactone) and NSAIDs — **all raise blood potassium levels.** Anyone taking them who adds salt substitutes or supplements is at real risk.

### 4. Salt substitutes — a two-sided story
Salt substitutes based on potassium chloride (KCl) showed, in a large trial in China, a significant reduction in stroke and mortality. **But the same product is genuinely dangerous for anyone with kidney disease or taking the drugs above.** It is a product with clear population benefit and real individual risk.

### 5. Hypokalemia — the opposite direction
Potassium deficiency is usually caused not by diet but by **vomiting, prolonged diarrhea, diuretics or laxative misuse**. Symptoms: muscle weakness, cramps, constipation and arrhythmias.

## Summary

Potassium is one of the minerals almost the whole population consumes less of than recommended — and the solution is not a supplement but **more vegetables, legumes and fruit on the plate**. Two practical points: **the ratio to sodium matters more than the absolute number**, so cutting processed food is worth exactly as much as adding vegetables; and **the [banana](/en/food/banana/) is not the best source** — baked [potato](/en/food/baked-potato/), [beans](/en/food/black-beans/), [avocado](/en/food/avocado/) and [tomato](/en/food/tomato/) paste provide more. **And an unequivocal warning: potassium supplements and salt substitutes are dangerous for anyone with kidney disease or taking ACE inhibitors.**

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much potassium do you need per day?**
Answer: Adequate intake is 3,400 mg for men and 2,600 mg for women, and the World Health Organization recommends 3,510 mg or more. Most of the population consumes less. No less important than the absolute number is the ratio to sodium — it predicts blood pressure and cardiovascular risk better than either one alone.

**Question: Are bananas a good source of potassium?**
Answer: Only moderate. They provide 358 mg per 100 g — less than baked potato with skin (535), raw spinach (558), avocado (485), dried figs (680) and concentrated tomato paste (1,014). The banana's reputation is a marketing success, not a nutritional one; legumes, root vegetables and leafy greens are much better sources.

**Question: Why are potassium supplements limited to 99 mg?**
Answer: For two safety reasons. Concentrated potassium pills have caused ulcers and perforations in the small intestine in the past, and excess potassium in the blood can cause dangerous arrhythmias. This regulatory limit is essentially a statement: potassium should come from food, where it arrives diluted and accompanied by fiber and fluid.

**Question: Who needs to be careful with potassium?**
Answer: People with chronic kidney disease, whose kidneys don't regulate it properly, and anyone taking ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, potassium-sparing diuretics or NSAIDs — all of which raise blood potassium. For them, salt substitutes and supplements are genuinely dangerous, and the guidance is usually to limit potassium, not increase it.

**Question: Are potassium-based salt substitutes recommended?**
Answer: At the population level they have shown real benefit — a large trial in China found a reduction in stroke and mortality. But at the individual level they are dangerous for anyone with kidney disease or taking drugs that raise potassium. It's a product worth asking a professional about, not one to adopt automatically off the shelf.

**Question: What causes potassium deficiency?**
Answer: Usually not diet but losses: vomiting, prolonged diarrhea, diuretic drugs or laxative misuse. Symptoms include muscle weakness, cramps, constipation and, in severe cases, arrhythmias. Significant deficiency calls for medical evaluation, not self-supplementation.

**Question: How do you increase potassium intake from food?**
Answer: Add legumes, root vegetables and leafy greens to your plate. Baked potato with skin, beans, lentils, spinach, avocado, pumpkin, dried fruit and tomato paste are the strong sources. At the same time, cutting processed food improves the potassium-to-sodium ratio — which is exactly what the studies measure.
