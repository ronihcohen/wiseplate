## Choline

**Choline is an essential nutrient the body can only partly make, so it has to come from food: 425 mg a day for women, 550 mg for men and 450 mg in pregnancy. Most people fall short, and the most accessible source is eggs — one egg provides about 147 mg.** In Hebrew it is "כולין". [NIH fact sheet on choline](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Choline-HealthProfessional/).

Choline was only recognised as an essential nutrient in 1998, long after the vitamins. It is neither a vitamin nor a mineral, and because the liver makes a little of it, it was considered "not needed from food" for years. In the US national nutrition survey, only about 11% of people aged two and over — and about 7% of adults — reached the adequate intake. [Wallace & Fulgoni, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26886842/).

### How much you need a day

There is not enough data to set a Recommended Dietary Allowance, so an "Adequate Intake" (AI) was set instead.

| Age | Adequate intake a day (AI) | Tolerable upper limit a day (UL) |
|---|---|---|
| Infants 0–6 months | 125 mg | Not set |
| Infants 7–12 months | 150 mg | Not set |
| 1–3 | 200 mg | 1,000 mg |
| 4–8 | 250 mg | 1,000 mg |
| 9–13 | 375 mg | 2,000 mg |
| Boys 14–18 | 550 mg | 3,000 mg |
| Girls 14–18 | 400 mg | 3,000 mg |
| Men 19+ | 550 mg | 3,500 mg |
| Women 19+ | 425 mg | 3,500 mg |
| Pregnancy | 450 mg | 3,500 mg (3,000 up to age 18) |
| Breastfeeding | 550 mg | 3,500 mg (3,000 up to age 18) |

### Food sources

| Food | Serving | Choline |
|---|---|---|
| Beef liver, pan-fried | 85 g (3 oz) | about 356 mg |
| Whole [egg](/en/food/eggs/) | 1 large | about 147 mg (mostly in the yolk) |
| Roasted [chicken breast](/en/food/chicken-breast/) | 85 g | about 72 mg |
| Cod | 85 g | about 71 mg |
| Kidney beans, cooked | ½ cup | about 45 mg |
| Cooked [quinoa](/en/food/quinoa/) | 1 cup | about 43 mg |
| Cooked [Brussels sprouts](/en/food/brussels-sprouts/) | ½ cup | about 32 mg |
| Cooked [broccoli](/en/food/broccoli/) | ½ cup | about 31 mg |
| Cooked [cauliflower](/en/food/cauliflower/) | ½ cup | about 24 mg |

One egg provides about a third of a woman's daily need, which also explains why vegans are at higher risk: the dense sources are animal foods, and from plants you need large amounts of legumes, brassicas and grains.

### Benefits

- **Liver:** Choline is needed to make VLDL, the particle that exports fat from the liver. In a trial where volunteers ate an almost choline-free diet, 77% of men and 80% of postmenopausal women developed fatty liver or muscle damage, compared with 44% of premenopausal women; things returned to normal when choline was added back. [Fischer et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17490963/). The sex difference comes from oestrogen, which boosts the liver's own production.
- **Pregnancy and brain development:** Choline matters for development of the hippocampus and for methylation in the foetus. In a small trial (26 women), 930 mg a day in the third trimester, versus 480 mg, was linked to faster information processing in the infants. [Caudill et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29217669/). That is an encouraging finding rather than proof, but it supports reaching at least 450 mg in pregnancy — alongside [folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/) and [iron](/en/food/iron/).
- **Acetylcholine and cell membranes:** Choline is the raw material for acetylcholine, the neurotransmitter of muscle control and memory, and for phosphatidylcholine, the most common phospholipid in cell membranes.
- **Methylation:** Through betaine, choline works with folate and [vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/) to break down homocysteine.

### Downsides and side effects

- **TMAO — an open question:** Gut bacteria turn choline into TMA, which the liver converts to TMAO. High TMAO levels were linked to cardiovascular events in a large observational study. [Tang et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23614584/). But in a feeding trial, [fish](/en/food/salmon/) raised blood TMAO 46–62 times more than eggs or beef, and fish is associated with heart health; the response also depended heavily on gut bacteria. [Cho et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27377678/). There is currently no good reason to avoid choline-rich foods because of TMAO.
- **Fishy body odour:** High-dose supplements (above the 3.5 g upper limit) lead to TMA in sweat and breath and a characteristic fishy smell. They can also cause sweating, vomiting, diarrhoea and a drop in blood pressure.
- **Who is at risk of low intake:** Vegans, pregnant and breastfeeding women, postmenopausal women, and carriers of gene variants (for example in PEMT) that reduce the body's own production.

### Practical use

- **Eggs:** One or two eggs a day cover a third to two-thirds of the need, and together with chicken, fish, legumes and brassicas, all of it.
- **Supplements:** Mainly relevant to vegans and to pregnant women who don't eat eggs. Most prenatal vitamins contain little or no choline, so check the label.
- **Forms:** Choline bitartrate and choline chloride are the inexpensive forms; citicoline and alpha-GPC have no proven advantage for simply meeting dietary needs.
- **Dose:** A supplement is meant to close the gap to the adequate intake, not to overshoot it by a wide margin.

### Summary

Choline is essential for the liver, the brain and foetal development, and most people eat less than recommended. Controlled studies showed that a low-choline diet causes fatty liver within weeks, and that higher intake in pregnancy may support infant development. The simple fix is eggs, along with chicken, fish, legumes and brassicas. The TMAO concern is not solid enough to justify avoiding choline-rich foods.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much choline do I need a day?**
Answer: The adequate intake is 550 mg for men, 425 mg for women, 450 mg in pregnancy and 550 mg while breastfeeding. The adult upper limit is 3,500 mg a day.

**Question: Which foods are rich in choline?**
Answer: Beef liver (about 356 mg per 85 g) and eggs (about 147 mg each), followed by chicken, cod, legumes, quinoa and brassicas such as broccoli and Brussels sprouts.

**Question: Why does choline matter in pregnancy?**
Answer: It is needed for brain development and methylation in the foetus. A small trial found that twice the recommended intake in the third trimester was linked to faster information processing in the infants. In practice, a sensible goal is at least 450 mg a day.

**Question: What is TMAO, and should I worry?**
Answer: TMAO is made when gut bacteria break down choline, and high levels have been linked to heart events. But fish raises TMAO far more than eggs and is linked to good heart health, so causation is unproven. It is not a reason to avoid eggs.

**Question: Is choline linked to fatty liver?**
Answer: Yes. In a trial where volunteers ate an almost choline-free diet, most men and postmenopausal women developed fatty liver or muscle damage, which resolved when choline was put back into the diet.

**Question: Who is at risk of choline deficiency?**
Answer: Vegans, pregnant and breastfeeding women, postmenopausal women, and people with gene variants that reduce the liver's own production.

**Question: Should I take a choline supplement?**
Answer: Most people who eat eggs don't need one. A supplement is mainly relevant to vegans and to pregnant women who can't reach the target from food. Doses above 3.5 g a day cause a fishy body odour and a drop in blood pressure.
