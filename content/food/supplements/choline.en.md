Choline is an essential nutrient that was only recognized as such **in 1998** — very late compared with the vitamins. It is neither a vitamin nor a mineral, and because the body makes a small amount of it in the liver, for years it was considered "not needed from food."

**Today the picture is different, and the key finding is surprising:** according to dietary surveys, **only a few percent of the population reach adequate intake of choline.** It is one of the most lacking nutrients in the Western diet — largely because of falling consumption of **[eggs](/en/food/eggs/)**, its main source.

## Benefits (Pros)

### 1. Fetal brain development
This is the best-established and most urgent role. Choline is needed for **development of the hippocampus** — the memory region — and for methylation processes in the fetus. Controlled human trials have shown that **higher choline intake in pregnancy was linked to better cognitive function in the baby**. Choline is **the forgotten nutrient** of pregnancy nutrition, alongside [folic acid](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/) and [iron](/en/food/iron/).

### 2. Acetylcholine — a neurotransmitter
Choline is the raw material for **acetylcholine**, the neurotransmitter of memory, learning and neuromuscular transmission.

### 3. Cell membranes
**Phosphatidylcholine** is the most abundant phospholipid in the body's cell membranes, and a major component of lecithin.

### 4. The liver — preventing fatty liver
Choline is needed to make **VLDL** — the particle that carries fat out of the liver into the blood. **Without it, fat gets stuck in the liver**, and this is a documented mechanism: studies in which volunteers were deprived of choline showed development of **fatty liver within weeks**, which reversed once choline was restored.

### 5. Methyl donor
Alongside [folic acid](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/) and [B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/), choline (via **betaine**) takes part in the methylation cycle that breaks down homocysteine.

## Downsides and Side Effects (Cons)

### 1. TMAO — the open debate
Certain gut bacteria convert choline (and carnitine) into **TMA**, which the liver converts into **TMAO**. High blood levels of TMAO **have been linked in observational studies to cardiovascular risk**.

**What to know before drawing conclusions:**
* **Causation has not been proven.** It is not known whether TMAO causes harm or is a marker of something else.
* **[Fish](/en/food/salmon/) raise TMAO more than [eggs](/en/food/eggs/) or meat** — and fish are consistently linked to **lower** cardiovascular risk. This is a contradiction the theory struggles to explain.
* **The makeup of the microbiome determines** the amount of conversion more than the amount of choline does.

**The currently accepted conclusion:** there is no justification for avoiding choline-rich foods because of TMAO.

### 2. Fishy body odor at high doses
High doses of choline supplements (above 3.5 grams) produce excess TMA that is excreted through sweat and breath — **a characteristic fishy body odor**. This is also the mechanism of the rare genetic disease **trimethylaminuria**.

### 3. Other side effects at high doses
Nausea, diarrhea, excessive sweating and a drop in blood pressure.

### 4. Populations at risk of deficiency
* **Vegans and vegetarians** — the densest sources come from animals.
* **Pregnant women** — the need rises just when the supply from the diet stays the same.
* **Postmenopausal women** — estrogen boosts the liver's own production of choline, so its decline increases dependence on diet.
* **Carriers of polymorphisms in the PEMT gene** — lower efficiency of the body's own production.

### 5. Supplements — when they make sense
For most people, **one to two [eggs](/en/food/eggs/) a day solve the problem without a supplement.** Supplementation is relevant for vegans and for pregnant women who don't eat eggs — preferably with professional advice.

## Summary

Choline is the nutrient most people don't know about and most don't get enough of: **only a few percent of the population reach adequate intake.** It is needed for acetylcholine, for cell membranes, for clearing fat from the liver — and above all **for fetal brain development**, where it is considered the forgotten nutrient of pregnancy nutrition. **The solution is very simple: [eggs](/en/food/eggs/).** One egg provides 147 mg — about a quarter of daily intake — mostly in the yolk. As for the worry about TMAO: it is **not well founded enough** to justify avoiding choline-rich foods.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much choline do you need per day?**
Answer: Adequate intake is 550 mg for men and 425 mg for women, and 450 and 550 mg respectively in pregnancy and breastfeeding. According to dietary surveys, only a few percent of the population reach this amount — it is one of the most lacking nutrients in the Western diet.

**Question: Which foods are rich in choline?**
Answer: Beef liver by a wide margin (420 mg per 100 g), followed by eggs — 147 mg per whole egg, mostly in the yolk. Salmon provides 91 mg per 100 g, chicken breast 85, and among plant sources: black beans (54), cauliflower (44), quinoa (43) and Brussels sprouts (41). One egg covers about a quarter of a woman's daily intake.

**Question: Why is choline important in pregnancy?**
Answer: It is needed for development of the hippocampus — the brain's memory region — and for methylation processes in the fetus. Controlled human trials found that higher choline intake in pregnancy was linked to better cognitive function in the baby. It is considered the forgotten nutrient of pregnancy nutrition, alongside folic acid and iron, and it is worth making sure intake is adequate.

**Question: What is TMAO, and should you worry?**
Answer: Gut bacteria convert choline into TMA, which the liver turns into TMAO, and high levels of it have been linked in observational studies to cardiovascular risk. But causation has not been proven, and the theory struggles to explain a basic fact: fish raise TMAO more than eggs or meat, and fish are consistently linked to lower risk. The currently accepted conclusion is that there is no justification for avoiding choline-rich foods.

**Question: Is choline linked to fatty liver?**
Answer: Yes, and this is a documented mechanism. Choline is needed to make VLDL, the particle that carries fat out of the liver into the blood, and without it fat gets stuck in the liver. In studies where volunteers were deprived of choline, fatty liver developed within weeks and reversed once choline was restored.

**Question: Who is at risk of choline deficiency?**

**Question: Should you take a choline supplement?**

Note that a high dose (above 3.5 grams) causes a characteristic fishy body odor, as well as nausea, diarrhea and a drop in blood pressure.
