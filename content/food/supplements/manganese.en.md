## Manganese

**Manganese is an essential trace mineral adults need 1.8 mg (women) to 2.3 mg (men) of a day, and that amount comes easily from whole grains, nuts, legumes and tea. Dietary deficiency in healthy people is barely documented, and manganese's known risks are about excess — from occupational exposure, liver disease and manganese-rich drinking water.** In Hebrew it is "מנגן". [NIH fact sheet on manganese](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Manganese-HealthProfessional/).

Manganese appears in almost every multivitamin and in "bone" supplements, usually for no good reason. It is essential — it is part of the main antioxidant enzyme inside mitochondria and helps build cartilage and bone — but food supplies it in abundance.

### How much you need a day

There is not enough data to set a Recommended Dietary Allowance, so an "Adequate Intake" (AI) was set instead.

| Age | Adequate intake a day (AI) | Tolerable upper limit a day (UL) |
|---|---|---|
| Infants 0–6 months | 0.003 mg | Not set |
| Infants 7–12 months | 0.6 mg | Not set |
| 1–3 | 1.2 mg | 2 mg |
| 4–8 | 1.5 mg | 3 mg |
| 9–13 | 1.9 mg (boys) / 1.6 mg (girls) | 6 mg |
| 14–18 | 2.2 mg (boys) / 1.6 mg (girls) | 9 mg |
| Men 19+ | 2.3 mg | 11 mg |
| Women 19+ | 1.8 mg | 11 mg |
| Pregnancy | 2.0 mg | 9–11 mg |
| Breastfeeding | 2.6 mg | 9–11 mg |

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) set an adequate intake of 3 mg a day for adults and did not set an upper limit.

### Food sources

| Source | Manganese per 100 g |
|---|---|
| [Teff](/en/food/teff/) (dry) | about 9.2 mg |
| [Hazelnuts](/en/food/hazelnuts/) | about 6.2 mg |
| [Oats](/en/food/oatmeal/) (dry) | about 4.9 mg |
| [Buckwheat](/en/food/buckwheat/) (dry) | about 1.3 mg |
| [Chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/) (cooked) | about 1.0 mg |
| [Pineapple](/en/food/pineapple/) | about 0.9 mg |
| [Green tea](/en/food/green-tea/) | 0.4–1.5 mg per brewed cup |

A bowl of porridge made from 40 g of oats provides about 2 mg, and a handful of hazelnuts (30 g) about 1.7 mg — almost the whole daily need either way.

### Benefits

- **MnSOD — the mitochondrial antioxidant:** Manganese is the central ion in manganese superoxide dismutase (SOD2), which neutralises free radicals inside the mitochondria, right where they form.
- **Cartilage and bone:** Manganese is a cofactor for enzymes that build proteoglycans, the building blocks of cartilage and bone matrix. It works alongside [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/), [vitamin D](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-d/) and [vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/), not instead of them; there is no evidence that manganese supplements alone improve bone density.
- **Metabolism:** It takes part in making glucose in the liver, in amino acid and cholesterol metabolism, and in the enzyme arginase, which completes the urea cycle and clears ammonia.
- **Wound healing:** Manganese is needed for the enzyme prolidase, which is involved in making [collagen](/en/food/collagen/).

### Downsides and side effects

- **Manganism — toxicity from inhalation:** Long-term inhalation of manganese dust or fumes — in welding, mining and steelmaking — can cause a Parkinson-like syndrome with tremor, stiffness and behavioural changes. Inhaled manganese bypasses the gut's regulation. [Kulshreshtha et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33819420/).
- **Liver disease:** Manganese is excreted almost entirely through bile. In cirrhosis and chronic liver disease it accumulates in the brain and shows up on brain scans. Long-term intravenous feeding can also cause build-up. People with liver disease should avoid manganese supplements.
- **Drinking water:** In a Canadian study of 362 children, higher manganese in household tap water was linked to lower IQ, even after adjusting for family factors. [Bouchard et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20855239/). It is a cross-sectional study and does not prove causation, but it is relevant to people who drink well water.
- **Iron:** Manganese and iron share absorption transporters in the gut. People low in [iron](/en/food/iron/) absorb more manganese, and high doses of manganese may reduce iron absorption.
- **Medications:** Manganese may reduce absorption of quinolone and tetracycline antibiotics when taken at the same time.

### Deficiency: what is actually known

True manganese deficiency has been described only in a handful of people, mostly in metabolic-ward studies where volunteers were fed diets almost free of manganese. They developed skin rashes and changes in blood cholesterol that reversed when manganese was restored. Outside such experiments, no common deficiency syndrome is known, and a blood test is not a reliable measure of manganese status anyway.

### Practical use

- **A healthy person does not need a manganese supplement.** Whole grains, nuts, legumes and tea cover the need.
- **If you take several supplements** (a multivitamin, a joint supplement, a "bone" formula), check how much manganese they add up to — each adds a few milligrams.
- **People with liver disease** should be wary of any supplement containing manganese.
- **Welders and metal workers** need ventilation and respiratory protection; this is an occupational risk, not a dietary one.

### Summary

Manganese is essential, plentiful, and almost nobody is short of it: a bowl of oats, a handful of nuts or a cup of tea provides a large share of the daily need. There is therefore no need for a manganese supplement, and anyone taking several supplements should check how much manganese they add. The groups that need active caution are people with liver disease and people exposed to manganese at work.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much manganese do I need a day?**
Answer: The adequate intake is 2.3 mg for men and 1.8 mg for women, and the upper limit is 11 mg a day. In Europe the adequate intake was set at 3 mg.

**Question: Should I take a manganese supplement?**
Answer: Almost never. Food provides plenty of manganese and deficiency is very rare. Most multivitamins already contain it, and combining several supplements adds amounts with no proven benefit.

**Question: What is manganism?**
Answer: A Parkinson-like neurological syndrome — tremor, stiffness and behavioural changes — caused by long-term inhalation of manganese dust or fumes in welding, mining and steelmaking. It is an occupational risk, not a dietary one.

**Question: Who should be careful with manganese?**
Answer: People with liver disease, because manganese is excreted through bile and builds up when the liver is damaged; patients on long-term intravenous feeding; and workers exposed to manganese dust. People drinking well water high in manganese should have their water tested.

**Question: Which foods are rich in manganese?**
Answer: Teff, hazelnuts, oats, buckwheat, chickpeas, pineapple and tea. Nearly every whole grain, nut or legume contributes.

**Question: Does manganese affect iron absorption?**
Answer: Yes. The two share absorption transporters in the gut, so people low in iron absorb more manganese, and high doses of manganese can reduce iron absorption.
