Manganese is a trace mineral that appears in almost every multivitamin, usually **for no reason**. It is indeed essential — it is part of the mitochondria's main antioxidant enzyme, and takes part in building bone and in metabolism — but **dietary manganese deficiency in a healthy person is so rare as to be exceptional**, and it is abundant in everyday foods.

**The real story of manganese runs in the opposite direction:** almost all of its known risks relate to **excess** — from industrial exposure, liver disease and unnecessary supplementation.

## Benefits (Pros)

### 1. MnSOD — the mitochondria's antioxidant
Manganese is the central ion in the enzyme **manganese superoxide dismutase (MnSOD/SOD2)**, which sits inside the mitochondria and neutralizes superoxide radicals formed during cellular respiration. This is the first line of defense against oxidative stress, right where it is generated.

### 2. Building bone and cartilage
Manganese is a cofactor of enzymes that build **glycosaminoglycans and proteoglycans** — the basic components of cartilage and the bone matrix. It works alongside [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/), [vitamin D](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-d/) and [vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/), not instead of them.

### 3. Metabolism
It takes part in enzymes of gluconeogenesis (making glucose), amino acid and cholesterol metabolism, and in **arginase** — the enzyme that closes the urea cycle and allows ammonia to be cleared.

### 4. Wound healing
It is needed for the activity of **prolidase**, an enzyme involved in producing [collagen](/en/food/collagen/) and renewing skin tissue.

## Downsides and Side Effects (Cons)

### 1. Manganism — inhalation toxicity
This is the best-documented risk of manganese, and it is **occupational, not dietary**. Prolonged inhalation of manganese dust or fumes — in welding, mining and steelmaking — causes **manganism**: a Parkinson's-like neurological syndrome with tremor, rigidity and behavioral disturbances. **Inhaled manganese bypasses the gut's regulatory mechanisms and reaches the brain directly via the olfactory nerve.**

### 2. Liver disease — the real clinical risk
Manganese is excreted almost entirely through bile. **In chronic liver disease and cirrhosis, clearance is impaired and manganese accumulates** — it is clearly visible on brain imaging in these patients, and is linked to hepatic encephalopathy. **Anyone with liver disease should avoid manganese supplements.**

### 3. Competing with [iron](/en/food/iron/) for absorption
Manganese and [iron](/en/food/iron/) share the same absorption transporters in the gut (DMT1). This has two consequences: **people with iron deficiency absorb more manganese**, and anyone taking a high-dose manganese supplement may reduce iron absorption.

### 4. Unnecessary supplementation
Manganese appears in most multivitamins and in every "bone" supplement, usually at 1-5 mg. **Added to a normal diet, it's easy to approach the UL of 11 mg a day** with no proven benefit. Dedicated manganese supplements are not justified for a healthy person.

### 5. Parenteral nutrition
Patients receiving long-term intravenous feeding get manganese directly into the blood, bypassing gut regulation — a group in which buildup in the brain has been described and monitoring is required.

## Summary

Manganese is essential, abundantly available, and almost nobody is short of it. **It is found in [oats](/en/food/oatmeal/), nuts, whole grains, [pineapple](/en/food/pineapple/) and [tea](/en/food/green-tea/) in amounts that easily cover daily intake.** So the practical recommendation is simple: **there's no need for a manganese supplement**, and anyone taking a multivitamin should check whether they need the manganese in it at all. Two populations do need active caution: **people with liver disease** and those with occupational exposure to manganese.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much manganese do you need per day?**
Answer: Adequate intake is 2.3 mg for men and 1.8 mg for women, and the tolerable upper intake level is 11 mg a day. A cup of oats, a handful of hazelnuts or a serving of whole grains covers the whole amount, which is why dietary deficiency on a Western diet is so rare as to be exceptional.

**Question: Should you take a manganese supplement?**
Answer: Almost never. Manganese is abundant in everyday foods, and deficiency is extremely rare. It already appears in most multivitamins and "bone" supplements at 1-5 mg, and adding these to a normal diet can bring you close to the tolerable upper intake level with no proven benefit.

**Question: What is manganism?**
Answer: A Parkinson's-like neurological syndrome — tremor, rigidity and behavioral disturbances — caused by prolonged inhalation of manganese dust or fumes in welding, mining and steelmaking. Inhaled manganese bypasses the gut's regulatory mechanisms and reaches the brain directly. It is purely an occupational risk, not a dietary one.

**Question: Who needs to be careful with manganese?**
Answer: Mainly people with chronic liver disease or cirrhosis. Manganese is excreted almost entirely through bile, and when the liver is damaged it accumulates — something visible on brain imaging and linked to hepatic encephalopathy. Patients on long-term intravenous feeding and workers exposed to manganese dust are also at risk.

**Question: Which foods are rich in manganese?**
Answer: The leaders are teff (9.2 mg per 100 g), hazelnuts (6.2 mg) and dry oats (4.9 mg). Buckwheat, pineapple, chickpeas and tea also have good amounts — a cup of brewed green tea provides 0.4-1.3 mg. Almost any whole grain, nut or legume contributes to the balance.

**Question: Does manganese affect iron absorption?**
Answer: Yes, in both directions. The two share the same absorption transporters in the gut, so people with iron deficiency actually absorb more manganese, and anyone taking a high-dose manganese supplement may reduce their iron absorption. This is another reason to avoid dedicated manganese supplements.
