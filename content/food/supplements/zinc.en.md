## Zinc

**Zinc is a trace mineral essential for the immune system, wound healing and the work of hundreds of enzymes. Women need 8 mg a day and men 11 mg, and the safe upper limit is 40 mg. Meat, shellfish, legumes and seeds supply it; supplements help mainly in deficiency, and zinc lozenges may shorten a cold — but long-term high doses cause copper deficiency.** In Hebrew it is "אבץ". [NIH fact sheet on zinc](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-HealthProfessional/).

The body has no dedicated zinc store, so it needs a steady supply. Absorption from plant sources is lower because of the phytate in grains and legumes, so vegetarians and vegans may need up to 50% more than the recommended intake.

### How much you need a day

| Age | Recommended intake a day (RDA) | Tolerable upper limit a day (UL) |
|---|---|---|
| Infants 0–6 months | 2 mg (AI) | 4 mg |
| Infants 7–12 months | 3 mg | 5 mg |
| 1–3 | 3 mg | 7 mg |
| 4–8 | 5 mg | 12 mg |
| 9–13 | 8 mg | 23 mg |
| 14–18 | 11 mg (boys) / 9 mg (girls) | 34 mg |
| Men 19+ | 11 mg | 40 mg |
| Women 19+ | 8 mg | 40 mg |
| Pregnancy | 11 mg (12 up to age 18) | 34–40 mg |
| Breastfeeding | 12 mg (13 up to age 18) | 34–40 mg |

### Food sources

| Source | Serving | Zinc |
|---|---|---|
| [Oysters](/en/food/oysters/) | 100 g | 16–90 mg, depending on species |
| Beef ([red meat](/en/food/red-meat/)) | 85 g | about 4–8 mg |
| [Pumpkin seeds](/en/food/pumpkin-seeds/) | 30 g | about 2.2 mg |
| Yogurt | 1 cup | about 1.7 mg |
| [Cashews](/en/food/cashews/) | 30 g | about 1.6 mg |
| Cooked [lentils](/en/food/lentil/) | ½ cup | about 1.3 mg |
| Cooked [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/) | ½ cup | about 1.3 mg |

Oysters are by far the densest source, and a serving of red meat provides about half the daily need. Soaking, sprouting and fermenting legumes and grains reduces phytate and improves the zinc you absorb from them.

### Benefits

- **Immune system:** Zinc is needed for immune cells to develop and work, and deficiency increases susceptibility to infection. In the developing world, zinc supplements for deficient children reduce diarrhoea and respiratory infections.
- **Colds — lozenges, not prevention:** A 2024 Cochrane review found that zinc has little or no effect on the chance of catching a cold, but may shorten a cold that has already started, at the cost of more mild side effects (bad taste, nausea). [Nault et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38719213/). In a pooled analysis of three trials of zinc acetate lozenges, they shortened colds by about 2.7 days, against an average duration of about a week. [Hemilä et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27378206/).
- **Macular degeneration:** In the AREDS trial, a formula with 80 mg zinc (plus antioxidants and 2 mg [copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/)) slowed progression to advanced macular degeneration in people at high risk. [AREDS, 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11594942/). That is a therapeutic dose for a specific condition, under an eye doctor's guidance — not general advice.
- **Wound healing and skin:** Zinc is needed for cell division and for building [collagen](/en/food/collagen/). In deficiency, wounds heal slowly and a rash appears; in people who aren't deficient, the benefit of supplements for skin is less clear.
- **Fertility and hormones:** Zinc deficiency impairs sperm production and testosterone. In men who aren't deficient, there is no good evidence that supplements raise testosterone.

### Downsides and side effects

- **Copper deficiency:** High-dose zinc (over 40 mg a day for a long time) makes gut cells produce a protein that binds copper and blocks its absorption. The result: anaemia, low white blood cells and, in advanced cases, nerve damage. Such cases have even been described from zinc-rich denture adhesives. [Nations et al., 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18525032/).
- **Nausea and stomach pain:** Common, especially when zinc is taken on an empty stomach or at high doses.
- **Zinc nasal sprays:** Linked to loss of the sense of smell, sometimes permanent, so they should not be used.
- **Antibiotics:** Zinc reduces absorption of quinolones and tetracyclines, so they are usually taken a few hours apart, as the pharmacist advises. It also reduces the effect of penicillamine.
- **Thiazide diuretics** increase zinc loss in urine.
- **Iron:** High-dose iron supplements can reduce zinc absorption when taken together.

### Who is at risk of deficiency

- Vegetarians and vegans, because absorption from plants is lower.
- Pregnant and breastfeeding women.
- People with gut diseases (Crohn's, coeliac) or after bariatric surgery.
- People with chronic heavy alcohol use.
- Exclusively breastfed infants older than six months, because breast milk no longer covers the full need from that age.

### Practical use

- **Food first:** Meat, shellfish, dairy, legumes, nuts and seeds cover most people's needs.
- **For a cold:** If you want to try zinc lozenges, start within 24 hours of symptoms and stop when the cold is over — don't take them for weeks.
- **Long-term supplements:** Stay under 40 mg a day from all sources combined unless a doctor advises otherwise; at higher doses copper is given too.
- **With food:** Reduces nausea, even if absorption is slightly lower.

### Summary

Zinc is essential for immunity, healing and growth, and most people get enough from food. Zinc lozenges may shorten a cold by a few days, but zinc does not prevent colds. A supplement is justified in deficiency, in at-risk groups and in specific conditions such as macular degeneration. Beyond the 40 mg upper limit over time, the real risk is copper deficiency — so more is not better.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much zinc do I need a day?**
Answer: 11 mg for men and 8 mg for women, 11 in pregnancy and 12 while breastfeeding. The adult upper limit is 40 mg a day. Vegetarians and vegans may need up to 50% more.

**Question: Does zinc help with colds?**
Answer: It probably doesn't prevent colds, but zinc lozenges started at the first symptoms may shorten one by a few days. The trade-off is a bad taste and nausea for some people.

**Question: Can a zinc supplement do harm?**
Answer: Yes, at high doses over time. Above 40 mg a day for months it can cause copper deficiency, with anaemia and nerve damage. In the short term it causes nausea, and zinc nasal sprays have been linked to loss of smell.

**Question: Which foods are rich in zinc?**
Answer: Oysters by a wide margin, then beef, pumpkin seeds, dairy, cashews, lentils and chickpeas. Zinc from animal foods is absorbed better than from plants.

**Question: Who is at risk of zinc deficiency?**
Answer: Vegetarians and vegans, pregnant and breastfeeding women, people with gut diseases or after bariatric surgery, people who drink heavily, and exclusively breastfed infants over six months.

**Question: Does zinc raise testosterone?**
Answer: Only if you are deficient. Zinc deficiency impairs testosterone and sperm production, and correcting it helps, but in men who aren't deficient there is no good evidence that supplements raise testosterone.

**Question: Which medications does zinc interact with?**
Answer: Quinolone and tetracycline antibiotics, whose absorption it reduces, and penicillamine. Thiazide diuretics increase zinc loss. Ask your pharmacist how far apart to take them.
