## Vitamin D

**Vitamin D is a fat-soluble vitamin the body makes in the skin when exposed to sunlight, and its best-established role is calcium absorption and bone health.** Adults up to age 70 need 15 micrograms (600 international units, IU) a day, and adults over 70 need 20 micrograms (800 IU). In Hebrew it is written "ויטמין D". [NIH fact sheet on vitamin D](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-HealthProfessional/).

Unlike most vitamins, very few foods contain it naturally, so sun exposure, fortified foods and supplements are the main sources. In the body it is converted in the liver and kidneys into an active form that works as a hormone — hence the nickname "the sunshine hormone".

### How much you need a day

| Age | Recommended intake a day | Tolerable upper limit a day |
|---|---|---|
| Infants up to 1 year | 10 mcg (400 IU) | 25–38 mcg (1,000–1,500 IU) |
| 1–70 | 15 mcg (600 IU) | 63–75 mcg at ages 1–8; 100 mcg (4,000 IU) from age 9 |
| Over 70 | 20 mcg (800 IU) | 100 mcg (4,000 IU) |
| Pregnancy and breastfeeding | 15 mcg (600 IU) | 100 mcg (4,000 IU) |

The conversion is simple: 1 microgram = 40 IU. The European Food Safety Authority also sets the adult upper limit at 100 micrograms a day. [EFSA — upper limit for vitamin D](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2023.8145). In Israel, the Ministry of Health recommends 400 IU a day for infants from birth to age one.

### Normal levels in a blood test

The standard test measures 25(OH)D, the form that circulates in the blood and reflects the body's stores.

| Blood level | nmol/L | ng/mL |
|---|---|---|
| Deficient | Below 30 | Below 12 |
| Inadequate for some people | 30–50 | 12–20 |
| Adequate for most people | 50 and above | 20 and above |
| Too high, risk of side effects | Above 125 | Above 50 |

Watch the units: labs in Israel and the US usually report ng/mL, while other sources use nmol/L (2.5 times the number). The table uses the thresholds of the US expert committee (IOM) that NIH follows; some labs flag only values above 30 ng/mL as "normal", and the debate over the cut-off is not settled.

### Food sources

| Food | Amount | Vitamin D |
|---|---|---|
| Sockeye [salmon](/en/food/salmon/), cooked | 3 oz (85 g) | about 14 mcg (570 IU) |
| UV-exposed [mushrooms](/en/food/mushrooms/) | ½ cup | about 9 mcg (366 IU) |
| Canned [sardines](/en/food/sardines/) | 2 fish | about 1.2 mcg (46 IU) |
| Fortified milk | 1 cup | about 3 mcg (120 IU) |
| [Egg](/en/food/eggs/) yolk | 1 | about 1.1 mcg (44 IU) |

Only some dairy products in Israel are fortified with vitamin D (check the label), so people who don't eat oily fish and get little sun get only a small amount from food.

### Benefits

- **Bone and muscle when deficient:** vitamin D is needed to absorb [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) from the gut. Long-term deficiency causes rickets in children and osteomalacia (soft bones) in adults, and correcting it prevents both.
- **Groups that benefit:** the Endocrine Society's 2024 guideline recommends supplementing without a blood test for children and teenagers (1–18), adults over 75, pregnant women and people with high-risk prediabetes. [Endocrine Society guideline, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38828931/).
- **Available and cheap:** vitamin D3 in supplement form is well absorbed, especially with a meal that contains fat, and is inexpensive.

### Downsides and side effects

- **For people who aren't deficient, the benefit is smaller than expected.** In the VITAL trial, about 26,000 adults took 2,000 IU a day or a placebo for about five years, and the supplement did not reduce cancer or major cardiovascular events. [VITAL trial — vitamin D](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415629/). A large meta-analysis found that supplements do not reduce fractures or falls in the general population. [Bolland et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30293909/).
- **Mood:** low levels are linked to depression in observational studies, but in the same VITAL trial, years of supplementation did not prevent depression. [VITAL-DEP](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32749491/).
- **Testosterone:** the claim that vitamin D raises testosterone did not hold up in a controlled trial in men with low testosterone — the supplement did not raise it meaningfully. [Lerchbaum et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30460609/).
- **Toxicity at high doses:** the vitamin accumulates in fat tissue. Taking tens of thousands of IU a day for long periods can cause high blood calcium, nausea, vomiting, kidney stones and kidney damage. Most cases come from dosing errors or mislabeled supplements.
- **Medications:** vitamin D can raise calcium levels in people taking thiazide diuretics, and drugs such as steroids and some anticonvulsants lower vitamin D levels. Anyone on regular medication should check with their doctor.
- **[Magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/):** the enzymes that activate vitamin D depend on magnesium, so magnesium deficiency can blunt the response to a supplement. There is no need to take magnesium with every capsule, but your diet should provide enough of it.

### Practical use

- **Test or not?** The 2024 guideline does not recommend routine testing for healthy people under 75. A test makes sense when there is a reason: osteoporosis, gut conditions that impair absorption, bariatric surgery, kidney disease, or very little sun exposure.
- **D3 over D2:** vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) raises blood levels more, and for longer. Vegan D3 made from lichen is also available.
- **With a meal:** absorption is better with a meal that contains fat.
- **Daily or weekly:** a daily dose of 600–2,000 IU suits most adults who have a reason to supplement. Higher therapeutic doses to correct a deficiency should be set by a doctor, with a follow-up test.

### Summary

Vitamin D is essential for bone, and deficiency is common in people who get little sun. The daily recommendation is 600 IU up to age 70 and 800 IU above it, and the safe upper limit is 4,000 IU. For people who aren't deficient, the large trials did not find that supplements prevent cancer, heart disease, depression or fractures — so a supplement corrects a shortfall rather than upgrading anything. A blood test and a matched dose beat "the more the better".

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much vitamin D do you need a day?**
Answer: 600 IU (15 micrograms) for adults up to 70, and 800 IU (20 micrograms) over 70. Infants up to one year need 400 IU.

**Question: What is a normal vitamin D level in a blood test?**
Answer: According to NIH, 20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L) or more is adequate for most people, and below 12 ng/mL (30 nmol/L) is deficient. Some labs only call levels from 30 ng/mL "normal".

**Question: Is it safe to take 4,000 IU a day?**
Answer: 4,000 IU is the tolerable upper limit for adults — the amount up to which side effects are not expected. That doesn't mean it's worth taking that much without a reason, and anything higher should be under a doctor's guidance.

**Question: When is the best time to take vitamin D?**
Answer: With a meal that contains fat, because it is fat-soluble. The time of day matters less than taking it consistently.

**Question: Can you get enough vitamin D from the sun?**
Answer: Often yes, from brief midday exposure of the arms and legs, but production falls with age, with darker skin, in people who cover most of their body, and in winter. Sunscreen and window glass block much of it.

**Question: What is the difference between vitamin D2 and D3?**
Answer: D3 (cholecalciferol) is the form the skin makes, and it raises blood levels more and for longer than D2 (ergocalciferol), which comes from plants and fungi.

**Question: Does vitamin D help with depression?**
Answer: Low levels are linked to depression, but in the VITAL trial, where thousands of adults took 2,000 IU a day for years, the supplement did not prevent depression. For someone with a proven deficiency, correcting it is part of sensible care.
