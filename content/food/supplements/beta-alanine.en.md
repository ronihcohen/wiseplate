## Beta-Alanine: Matching the Type of Effort Matters More Than the Tingling

Beta-alanine (in Hebrew, "בטא־אלנין") is an amino acid used to make carnosine in muscle. Carnosine helps buffer changes in acidity inside muscle cells during intense effort. Taking beta-alanine over time can increase carnosine stores; that is why it has been studied as a performance supplement. [The ISSN position stand on beta-alanine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4501114/).

It sometimes appears in pre-workout blends, but its presence there can create the mistaken expectation of an immediate effect. Unlike [caffeine](/en/food/supplements/caffeine/), its accepted use is aimed at changing stores over weeks. The tingling some users feel is not a measure of its athletic effectiveness.

### In which efforts might there be a benefit?

The possible benefit is most prominent in intense efforts lasting roughly one to four minutes, although the research is not limited to that window. There is no basis for promising improvement in every strength workout or at every running distance. [ISSN — areas of benefit](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4501114/).

A 2017 meta-analysis found a small average effect, depending on the duration of the effort and the type of test. The improvement was clearer in capacity tests to exhaustion than in performance tests with a fixed end point, such as a time trial. [The meta-analysis by Saunders and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27797728/).

The difference is practical: managing to keep pedaling longer in the lab is not the same as cutting time in a race. Both measures are interesting, but only the second directly tests the competitive outcome described. When a product promises "improved endurance", check which of the two was measured.

| Type of activity | What to check before considering the supplement |
|---|---|
| An intense effort of a few minutes | Did the studies test a similar duration and intensity? |
| Training with repeated intense intervals | Are the length of the intervals and the rests similar to the studied protocol? |
| A one-rep max lift | Was an improvement in maximal strength measured, and not just the ability to sustain a different effort? |
| Long continuous running | Is there data on actual running time over a relevant distance? |

The table is a way to read research, not a personal ranking of whether it's worth it. Even when the type of activity fits, you still need to weigh the size of the benefit, the cost and tolerability.

### Dose, duration and timing

The ISSN position stand describes taking about 4–6 grams a day for at least 2–4 weeks. AIS documents also include protocols of up to 6.4 grams a day. The daily amount is usually divided into small doses to improve tolerability. These are studied ranges, not a personal recommendation for every reader. [The ISSN position stand](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4501114/) and [the AIS guide to beta-alanine](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_a/performance-supplements2/b-alanine).

There is no need to tie the whole dose to the time of your workout. The plan is based on accumulation over time, so the daily protocols also include rest days. Sustained-release formulations and splitting the dose can reduce tingling. [AIS — using beta-alanine](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_a/performance-supplements2/b-alanine).

Before starting, consider whether your routine suits sustained intake. Occasional use only before a hard workout is not the same as a daily protocol. And buying a one-week pack is not a way to test research in which participants took the supplement for several weeks.

### Tingling and side effects

A tingling sensation in the skin, called paresthesia, is a well-known side effect, mainly after a large immediate-release dose. It is not a sign that the muscle is getting stronger, and it is not a condition for the supplement to work. In studies at the usual doses it generally passes; reducing the size of each single dose can help. Information on continuous use over years is more limited than information on protocols of weeks and months. [Safety according to ISSN](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4501114/) and [AIS](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_a/performance-supplements2/b-alanine).

Don't automatically interpret every reaction to a blend as "normal tingling". A pre-workout product may also contain caffeine and other ingredients. An unusual or persistent reaction requires stopping use and getting it checked; shortness of breath or facial swelling require urgent medical help.

### Does it clear lactic acid or prevent muscle soreness?

Describing the supplement as a "lactic acid cleaner" is inaccurate. The studied mechanism is increasing carnosine and buffering acidity changes, not clearing a waste product after exercise. Research on the ability to sustain effort is not in itself proof of preventing muscle soreness the next day. [The mechanism of action in the AIS guide](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_a/performance-supplements2/b-alanine).

It's also worth watching out for similar names: beta-alanine is not a BCAA supplement and is not a [complete protein powder](/en/food/protein-powder/). Don't choose it to meet your daily protein target; for that question the discussion of [whey protein](/en/food/supplements/whey-protein/) is the right fit.

### Choosing a product and assessing the result

Check how much beta-alanine is actually in a serving. If only the weight of a blend containing several substances is listed, you can't tell from it whether the product provides the studied amount. Compare the price per planned day of use, not just the price per pack. Competitive athletes are advised to also check certification for the specific batch; independent testing reduces the risk of prohibited substances but does not eliminate it. [AIS guidelines on batches](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/athlete-resources).

Choose a test that recurs in your training plan and is related to your goal. Record the result before the period of use and at its end, along with changes in your plan and in the test conditions. If you changed your diet, caffeine and training load at the same time, you won't be able to tell with confidence which factor contributed. Even the absence of a clear improvement is useful information when deciding whether the expense is justified.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Do you need to feel tingling for the supplement to work?**
Answer: No. Tingling is a side effect and is not a measure of increased carnosine or improved performance.

**Question: Is one dose before a competition enough?**
Answer: That is not the way of use intended to increase carnosine stores; the accepted protocols are based on intake over weeks.

**Question: Is beta-alanine suitable for everyone who trains?**
Answer: No. The benefit depends on the type of effort, and even in suitable situations it is not guaranteed for every person.
