## Zucchini (*Cucurbita pepo*)

Zucchini (in Hebrew "קישוא", also known as courgette) is a "transparent" vegetable — 17 calories per 100 grams, 95% water, and a mild flavor that takes on whatever surrounds it. That is exactly why it is one of the most useful vegetables in the kitchen: it adds volume, texture and moisture with almost no calories, and fits into almost anything.

This page also has a safety warning worth knowing, one most people have never heard: **bitter zucchini should be thrown away, not eaten.**

Zucchini belongs to the gourd family, together with [cucumber](/en/food/cucumber/), [pumpkin](/en/food/pumpkin/) and [watermelon](/en/food/watermelon/). Unlike pumpkin, it is picked **half-ripe** — before the skin hardens and the seeds develop — which is why it is low in carotenoids and sugars.

### Bitter zucchini — the warning nobody talks about

Plants of the gourd family naturally produce compounds called **cucurbitacins**, whose job is to repel pests. In cultivated varieties they have been bred out almost completely — but not always:

* **Back-crossing with wild or ornamental varieties** (for example, when growing from seeds saved from the previous crop) can bring the gene back.
* **Growing stress** — severe drought, extreme temperatures or irregular watering — can raise the concentration.

The result is a **very bitter** vegetable, in a way you can't miss. High amounts of cucurbitacins cause nausea, vomiting and acute diarrhea, and severe cases have been described. The phenomenon is rare and almost always concentrated in home-grown zucchini or ones grown from saved seeds.

**The rule is simple and clear-cut: a zucchini (or cucumber, or pumpkin) that tastes especially bitter — spit it out and throw it away.** No cooking neutralizes it, and the bitterness is not "a matter of taste."

### How much oil zucchini really absorbs

Zucchini has a porous structure full of water. When you heat it, the water evaporates and the cavities open up — and if there is oil in the pan, it goes in. To a lesser degree than [eggplant](/en/food/eggplant/), but significantly:

| Preparation | Calories per 100 g |
|---|---|
| Raw zucchini | 17 |
| Zucchini roasted in the oven with a teaspoon of oil per 100 g | ~55 |
| Pan-fried zucchini | 100-180 |
| Breaded fried zucchini | 200+ |

**The conclusion:** "I ate zucchini" and "I ate fried zucchini" are two completely different things. The low-calorie vegetable turns into a serving of fat.

### Benefits

* **Especially low energy density:** 17 calories per 100 grams. Adding it to a dish increases volume and weight while adding almost no energy — an effective tool for weight control.
* **[Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/):** 20% of the daily value per 100 grams, a respectable figure for such a low-calorie vegetable.
* **[Potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/):** 261 mg — contributes to sodium balance and blood pressure regulation.
* **Easy to digest:** low in coarse fiber and fermentable compounds, so it suits post-surgery diets, active bowel disease and people with irritable bowel. Zucchini is considered **low FODMAP** in normal servings.
* **High water content:** 95% — contributes to hydration, especially in summer.
* **Culinary flexibility:** eaten raw, grated, roasted, stuffed, in soups and in sweet baking.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Low nutrient density.** Apart from vitamin C, zucchini is not a significant source of anything. It fills rather than nourishes — so it is no substitute for dark green or colorful vegetables.
* **Low fiber:** only one gram per 100 grams, less than [broccoli](/en/food/broccoli/), [cabbage](/en/food/cabbage/) or [okra](/en/food/okra/).
* **Absorbs oil** — as described above.
* **Bitter zucchini** — as described above.
* **Wateriness in cooking.** Zucchini releases a large amount of water, which makes stews and baked goods too soft. The fix: salting and squeezing (see below).
* **Sprayed skin.** The skin is eaten with the vegetable rather than peeled, so thorough washing matters more than with a vegetable you peel.

### Practical use

* **Don't peel it.** Most of the vitamins and the color are in the skin, and it is soft and completely edible.
* **Roast, don't fry.** An oven at 220°C (425°F) with a teaspoon of [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) per 100 grams — it browns, concentrates the flavor and keeps the calories low.
* **Salt before using it in batter:** grated zucchini for a cake or an omelet — sprinkle with salt, wait 15 minutes and squeeze by hand or in a cloth. It removes a cup of water for every two zucchini and prevents a soggy result.
* **Zucchini noodles ("zoodles") that don't turn watery:** spiralize, salt for 10 minutes, dry, and toss in a very hot pan for only 60-90 seconds. Cooking longer will turn them to mush.
* **Choose small and firm.** A zucchini 15-20 cm long. Too large means more water, developed seeds and a spongy texture.
* **Stuffed zucchini** — pay attention to the filling: a filling of rice and meat in an oily sauce doubles the calories of the dish, and it is no longer "a vegetable dish."
* **Zucchini flowers** are edible and a delicacy — lightly fried or stuffed with cheese.

### Summary

Zucchini is a workhorse vegetable: 17 calories per 100 grams, 95% water, decent vitamin C, and zero demands. It adds volume to the plate, is easy to digest, low FODMAP and suitable for almost everyone. But make no mistake — **it fills rather than nourishes**, and it is no substitute for colorful vegetables. Two things are worth remembering: **it absorbs oil** and can jump from 17 to 180 calories in a frying pan, and **bitter zucchini should be thrown away** — that is not a quirk of taste but a sign of cucurbitacins.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is the nutritional value of zucchini?**
Answer: 100 grams of fresh zucchini provide just 17 calories, 1.2 grams of protein, 3.1 grams of carbohydrates, 1 gram of fiber, 17.9 mg of vitamin C (20% of the daily value) and 261 mg of potassium. It is 95% water, so its energy density is among the lowest of vegetables — but its nutrient density is modest too.

**Question: What do you do with a bitter zucchini?**
Answer: Throw it away. Strong bitterness in zucchini, cucumber or pumpkin indicates a high concentration of cucurbitacins — natural compounds that can cause nausea, vomiting and acute diarrhea. Cooking does not neutralize them. The phenomenon is rare and appears mainly in home-grown plants or seeds saved from a previous crop, but it is not a matter of personal taste.

**Question: Do you need to peel zucchini?**
Answer: No. The skin is soft, completely edible, and contains a large share of the vitamins and color. Since it is eaten rather than removed, it is worth washing the vegetable well before use.

**Question: How many calories are in fried zucchini?**
Answer: Many more than you'd think. Raw zucchini is 17 calories per 100 grams; pan-fried zucchini reaches 100-180 calories, and breaded over 200. The vegetable's porous structure absorbs oil as soon as the water evaporates. Oven-roasting with a teaspoon of oil comes to only about 55 calories.

**Question: How do you make zucchini noodles that don't turn watery?**
Answer: Spiralize, sprinkle with salt and wait 10 minutes, dry well in a cloth, then toss in a very hot pan for only 60-90 seconds. The problem isn't the zucchini but the cooking time — it releases all its water within a few minutes and turns to mush.

**Question: Is zucchini suitable for people with irritable bowel?**
Answer: Yes. Zucchini is considered low FODMAP in normal servings, low in coarse fiber and fermentable compounds, so it is one of the best-tolerated vegetables for sensitive bowel conditions — including after surgery or during an active flare of inflammatory bowel disease.

**Question: How do you choose a good zucchini?**
Answer: Small and firm, 15-20 cm long, with glossy skin and no soft spots. An overly large zucchini has accumulated water and developed seeds, and its texture is spongy and its flavor weak. A gentle squeeze should meet resistance.

**Question: Can you eat zucchini flowers?**
Answer: Yes, and they are considered a delicacy. It is common to stuff them with cheese and fry them lightly, or add them fresh to a salad or bread. Remove the stamens from inside, rinse gently and use them the same day — the flowers wilt within hours.
