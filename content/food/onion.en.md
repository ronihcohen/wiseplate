## Onion

The onion (*Allium cepa*), in Hebrew "בצל", is a basic ingredient in kitchens around the world and one of the most widely eaten vegetables in human history. Contrary to the idea that it is only a seasoning, the onion is a storehouse of antioxidants, active sulfur compounds and essential prebiotics — and it has been studied extensively in the context of preventing heart disease and cancer and supporting the microbiome.

The onion belongs to the [garlic](/en/food/garlic/) family (*Allium*), which also includes garlic, leeks and green onions. All members of the family share health-promoting sulfur compounds, but each has a unique profile of concentrations and ratios of these components.

### Research-based health benefits

* **Quercetin:** a powerful flavonoid found in high concentrations in the outer layers and skin of the onion. It is linked to anti-inflammatory effects, cardiovascular protection, lower blood pressure and anti-proliferative effects on cancer cells — especially in colon cancer. Yellow onions contain a 2-3 times higher concentration than white onions.

* **Prebiotics and inulin:** contains fructooligosaccharides (FOS) and inulin — soluble fibers that are not digested in the stomach and reach the large intestine intact. There they feed Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus bacteria, strengthening the microbiome and the function of the intestinal barrier.

* **Sulfur compounds (alliin and isoalliin):** the enzyme alliinase, released when an onion is cut, activates reactive sulfur compounds. They support natural blood thinning, lower blood pressure and antimicrobial activity — similar to, but less potent than, garlic.

* **[Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) and [vitamin B6](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b6/):** vitamin B6 is essential for producing serotonin and for amino acid metabolism; vitamin C supports immunity and the synthesis of [collagen](/en/food/collagen/).

* **Chromium:** a mineral associated with [insulin](/en/food/supplements/insulin/) sensitivity — onions are an interesting plant source of it.

* **Population studies:** epidemiological studies have found a consistent link between eating vegetables of the Allium family and a reduced risk of stomach, colon and ovarian cancer.

### Downsides and health considerations

* **Gas and bloating (FODMAPs):** fructooligosaccharides are highly fermentable carbohydrates (FODMAPs). In people with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) they can cause bloating, pain and gas. Cooked onion reduces some of the FODMAPs compared with raw.

* **Breath and skin odor:** the sulfur compounds are absorbed into the blood and excreted through the lungs, which causes a characteristic odor that can last for hours.

* **Eye irritation:** cutting onions releases a gaseous compound (syn-propanethial-S-oxide) that irritates the conjunctiva. Chill the onion beforehand or use a very sharp knife when chopping.

* **Interaction with anticoagulants:** in large amounts it may strengthen anticoagulant effects — something to watch for in patients on warfarin.

### Culinary and practical use

* **Raw:** in salads, pita, gazpacho — maximizes the sulfur compounds and FODMAPs
* **Fried/caramelized:** cooking reduces FODMAPs and adds sweetness; increases quercetin availability
* **Pickled:** in vinegar — adds probiotics and reduces pungency
* **Onion powder:** relatively low concentration; convenient but not equivalent to raw
* **Vegetable stock:** add onion skins to stock — they are very rich in quercetin

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Which onion is healthier — yellow, white or purple?**
Answer: Red-purple onions are richer in anthocyanins (anti-inflammatory pigments); yellow onions are richer in quercetin. For variety, it's worth alternating between both.

**Question: Is cooked onion nutritionally the same as raw onion?**
Answer: Not exactly — cooking reduces some of the vitamin C and some of the sulfur compounds, but it increases quercetin availability and reduces FODMAPs. Both forms are healthy and complement each other.

**Question: Can you eat onion skin?**
Answer: The skin is very rich in quercetin. You can add it to vegetable stock and soups and strain it out before eating.

**Question: How can you reduce bad breath after eating onion?**
Answer: Chewing fresh parsley, [coffee](/en/food/coffee/), [milk](/en/food/milk/) or [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) juice may neutralize some of the odor. The physiological cause comes from the blood — not just from the mouth.

### Summary

The onion is a powerful and inexpensive nutritional ingredient. Using it as a cooking base adds significant nutritional value to any meal, with special benefits for heart health, the microbiome and the prevention of chronic disease.
