## Onion

**A medium onion (about 110 g) provides roughly 44 calories, 1.9 g of fiber and a little vitamin C and B6. It is one of the main dietary sources of quercetin and contains fructans, fibers that feed gut bacteria but cause bloating in people with irritable bowel syndrome. A quercetin extract from onion skins slightly lowered blood pressure in people with hypertension; onion itself has no such proof.**

The onion (*Allium cepa*, in Hebrew "בצל") belongs to the allium family, together with [garlic](/en/food/garlic/), leek and spring onion. It is eaten in almost every cuisine, usually as a small amount inside a dish rather than as a main vegetable. Cutting it releases an enzyme that turns sulfur compounds into pungent substances, among them the gas that makes you cry.

### Benefits

* **Quercetin and blood pressure.** Quercetin is concentrated in the outer layers and skin. In a double-blind controlled trial, 70 overweight adults with prehypertension or hypertension took 162 mg of quercetin a day from onion skin extract, or a placebo, for 6 weeks. Across all participants there was no change, but in the subgroup with hypertension 24-hour systolic pressure fell by about 3.6 mmHg. [Brüll et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26328470/). The dose is higher than what you get from onion in an ordinary meal, so this is a hint, not a recommendation to eat onion as treatment.

* **Fructans: food for gut bacteria.** Onion contains fructans (inulin and fructo-oligosaccharides), which are not digested in the small intestine and are fermented in the colon by bacteria such as bifidobacteria. For most people that is a benefit; for people with irritable bowel syndrome it is exactly what causes symptoms (see below).

* **Flavor without calories.** Onion adds depth to any dish for a few dozen calories, which lets you use less fat and salt.

* **Part of your vegetable intake.** In a meta-analysis of cohort studies, every extra 200 g a day of fruit and vegetables was associated with a lower risk of heart disease and death. [Aune et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28338764/). Onion contributes, even in small amounts.

### Downsides

* **IBS and FODMAPs.** The fructans in onion are among the most common triggers of bloating and pain in people with irritable bowel syndrome. In a controlled trial, a low-FODMAP diet, which temporarily limits foods such as onion, garlic and wheat, reduced symptoms compared with a sham diet. [Staudacher et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28625832/). Fructans dissolve in water, not in oil, so oil in which onion was cooked and then removed is usually tolerated, whereas onion cooked in soup still contains them.

* **Heartburn.** Onion, especially raw, can worsen reflux in some people.

* **Bad breath.** Sulfur compounds are absorbed into the blood and exhaled for hours after the meal. Brushing does not fully remove the smell.

* **Tears when cutting.** The gas syn-propanethial-S-oxide irritates the eyes. Chilling the onion before cutting and using a sharp knife reduce its release.

* **Pets.** Onion, raw or cooked, is toxic to dogs and cats. Do not feed them leftovers containing onion.

### Practical use

* **Raw in salad:** thinly sliced red onion soaked for a few minutes in cold water or lemon juice is less sharp.
* **Caramelized:** slow frying over low heat brings out sweetness; that is the onion's own sugar, not an addition.
* **Keeping the quercetin:** quercetin is fairly stable in stir-frying and baking but leaches into water when boiled. Onion in soup leaves the quercetin in the liquid, which you eat too.
* **For FODMAP-sensitive people:** the green part of spring onions, chives, or oil flavored with onion that has then been removed.
* **In stock:** yellow onion skins add color and flavor to stock; strain them out before serving.
* **Storage:** in a dry, cool, ventilated place, not next to potatoes. Cut onion keeps in the fridge in a closed container for a few days.
* **Freezing:** chopped raw onion freezes well in a sealed bag and goes straight into the pan for cooking, though it loses its crunch for salads.

### Summary

The onion is a cheap staple that adds flavor with almost no calories, and it is an important source of quercetin and fructans. The blood-pressure evidence concerns a high-dose extract, not onion in a meal. In people with irritable bowel syndrome it is one of the most common triggers, and there are practical workarounds.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in an onion?**
Answer: About 40 calories per 100 g, so roughly 44 for a medium onion. Fried onion takes on the calories of the oil it was fried in.

**Question: Which onion is healthiest: yellow, white or red?**
Answer: Yellow and red onions are richer in quercetin, and red onions also contain anthocyanins. White onions are milder and lower in both. Variety is the best choice.

**Question: Why does onion make me bloated?**
Answer: Because of fructans, a type of FODMAP. This is very common in IBS. Try the green part of spring onions or onion-flavored oil.

**Question: Does onion lower blood pressure?**
Answer: A high-dose quercetin extract from onion skins slightly lowered blood pressure in people with hypertension in a controlled trial. Ordinary onion in a meal is not a treatment.

**Question: Is raw or cooked onion better?**
Answer: Both are good. Raw keeps more of the sulfur compounds, and gentle cooking keeps most of the quercetin and is better tolerated by some people.

**Question: How do I cut onions without crying?**
Answer: Chill the onion in the fridge before cutting, use a sharp knife, and cut the root end last.

**Question: Is onion dangerous for dogs?**
Answer: Yes. Onion in any form is toxic to dogs and cats and can damage their red blood cells.
