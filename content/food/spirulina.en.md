## Spirulina (*Arthrospira platensis*)

Spirulina (in Hebrew, "ספירולינה") is a **cyanobacterium** — a photosynthetic bacterium, not an alga in the plant sense — and one of the most nutrient-dense foods ever measured: 57 grams of protein, 28 mg of [iron](/en/food/iron/) and 6 mg of [copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/) per 100 grams of dry weight.

**And that is where the central problem with all its marketing lies.** Nobody eats 100 grams of spirulina. **A typical serving is 3-10 grams.** When you translate the impressive numbers into a realistic serving, the picture changes completely — and it's worth seeing it before you buy.

On this page: what spirulina really provides in a serving, what is proven and what isn't, and three safety caveats worth knowing.

### Nutrition facts — per 100 grams vs. a real serving

| Nutrient | Per 100 g dry | **5-gram serving** | % Daily Value per serving |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calories | 290 | 15 | — |
| **Protein** | **57.5 g** | **2.9 g** | 6% |
| Carbohydrates | 23.9 g | 1.2 g | — |
| Fat | 7.7 g | 0.4 g | — |
| **[Iron](/en/food/iron/)** | **28.5 mg** | **1.4 mg** | **8%** |
| **[Copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/)** | **6.1 mg** | **0.31 mg** | **34%** |
| [Magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) | 195 mg | 9.8 mg | 2% |
| Riboflavin (B2) | 3.67 mg | 0.18 mg | 14% |
| [Vitamin B1](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b1/) | 2.38 mg | 0.12 mg | 10% |
| [Vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/) | ~0 available | **~0 available** | **0%** |

**The numerical conclusion:** a tablespoon of spirulina provides about 3 grams of protein — less than half an egg. It is **not a practical source of protein**, and marketing that touts "60-70% protein" relies on a number nobody consumes.

**What is meaningful in a serving:** [copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/) (34%), riboflavin (14%), [iron](/en/food/iron/) (8%), and **phycocyanin** — the blue pigment that is the truly interesting active ingredient.

### Vitamin B12 — the most dangerous mistake

This is the most important caveat on this page, and it is a matter of health, not of nutritional precision:

**Spirulina contains "pseudo-vitamin B12" — analogues that are inactive in the human body.** In standard lab tests they are counted as B12, which is why an impressive value appears on many packages. But **the body cannot use them**, and there is evidence they may **compete for binding sites** and interfere with the absorption of real B12.

**What this means:** vegans and vegetarians **cannot rely on spirulina as a source of [vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/).** B12 deficiency causes neurological damage that may be irreversible. The only solution is a **dedicated B12 supplement** or fortified food.

### What is proven and what isn't

**Reasonable evidence:**
* **Blood lipid profile.** Meta-analyses of controlled trials found a moderate drop in total cholesterol, LDL and triglycerides with spirulina at doses of 1-8 grams a day. The effect exists but is not dramatic.
* **Blood pressure.** Preliminary evidence of a slight reduction, mainly at relatively high doses.
* **Phycocyanin as an antioxidant.** Its biochemical activity is well documented in the lab.

**Weak or early evidence:**
* **Immune function** — mainly lab and animal studies.
* **Seasonal allergy (rhinitis)** — a few small trials with positive results, but the samples are small.
* **Endurance and sports performance** — mixed results.

**Unsupported:** "detox", "binding heavy metals", general strengthening, weight loss.

### Three safety caveats

* **Microcystins.** Spirulina is grown in open ponds, and such ponds can also be colonized by other cyanobacteria — mainly *Microcystis* — which produce **microcystins**, hepatotoxic toxins. Market testing has found contamination in some products. **The solution: buy from a manufacturer that publishes lab tests for microcystins and heavy metals.**
* **Phenylketonuria (PKU).** Spirulina is very rich in phenylalanine. **People with PKU must not take it.**
* **Autoimmune diseases.** Because of its reported effect on the immune system, there is a theoretical concern about worsening autoimmune diseases. The evidence is limited, but it is a reason to consult a doctor.

### Benefits

* **High micronutrient density for its weight.** [Copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/), riboflavin and [iron](/en/food/iron/) in noticeable amounts even in 5 grams.
* **Phycocyanin:** a unique antioxidant that is also the source of the blue color. It is also used as a natural food coloring.
* **Protein with a good profile** — it contains all the essential amino acids, even though the amount in a realistic serving is small.
* **Reasonable evidence for the lipid profile** — as detailed above.
* **Plant-based [iron](/en/food/iron/) in a reasonable amount** — 1.4 mg in a 5-gram serving.
* **Low environmental footprint** — grown in water, with no farmland and no animal feed.
* **An alternative to [moringa powder](/en/food/moringa-powder/)** in the category, with a different mineral profile.

### Downsides and considerations

* **The gap between marketing and the serving.** Almost every claim about spirulina is framed per 100 grams, an amount nobody consumes.
* **Pseudo-[vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/)** — as detailed above, the most serious caveat.
* **Microcystins, PKU and autoimmunity** — as detailed above.
* **Price.** Very expensive relative to the nutritional value it actually provides. The same money spent on spirulina would buy a large quantity of [lentils](/en/food/lentil/), [eggs](/en/food/eggs/) or leafy greens.
* **Taste and smell.** Strongly earthy and marine, which many people struggle with. Well hidden in a fruit smoothie with [banana](/en/food/banana/).
* **[Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/)** in an amount relevant to people taking anticoagulants — keep intake consistent.
* **A supplement, not a food.** It doesn't replace a vegetable, a protein or a meal, and it doesn't "fix" a poor diet.

### Practical use

* **Typical dose:** 1-5 grams a day (half a teaspoon to a level teaspoon). Higher doses have been studied but are not needed.
* **Choose a brand that publishes tests** for microcystins and heavy metals. This is the most important check in the category.
* **Don't rely on it for [B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/).** Vegans need a dedicated supplement.
* **Mix it into a smoothie** with [banana](/en/food/banana/), [berries](/en/food/blueberries/) or citrus juice — the tartness and sweetness mask the taste.
* **With [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/)** — improves absorption of its plant-based iron.
* **Not close to [coffee](/en/food/coffee/) or [tea](/en/food/green-tea/)** — [tannins](/en/food/tannins/) inhibit iron absorption.
* **Consult a doctor** if you have an autoimmune disease, are on anticoagulant treatment, or are pregnant or breastfeeding.
* **Keep things in proportion.** If the goal is protein — one [egg](/en/food/eggs/) gives twice as much as a tablespoon of spirulina, at a tenth of the price.

### Summary

Spirulina is a dense and genuinely interesting food, mainly thanks to **phycocyanin, copper and riboflavin** — along with reasonable evidence for a moderate improvement in the lipid profile. But it is **not a practical source of protein**: a tablespoon provides about 3 grams, less than half an egg. And the most important caveat of all: **its B12 is not bioavailable** — vegans who rely on it may develop a deficiency with neurological damage. Before buying, it's worth checking one thing: **that the manufacturer publishes tests for microcystins and heavy metals**.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much protein is in spirulina?**
Answer: 57.5 grams per 100 grams of dry weight — but that's an amount nobody consumes. In a realistic 5-gram serving it comes to only 2.9 grams of protein, or 6% of the Daily Value. One egg provides twice that, at a tenth of the price. Spirulina is not a practical source of protein.

**Question: Is spirulina a source of vitamin B12 for vegans?**
Answer: No, and this is a dangerous mistake. Spirulina contains pseudo-vitamin B12 — analogues that are counted in lab tests but are inactive in the human body, and there is evidence they may even compete for binding sites and interfere with the absorption of real B12. B12 deficiency causes neurological damage that may be irreversible, so vegans need a dedicated B12 supplement or fortified food.

**Question: What are microcystins and why do they matter?**
Answer: Hepatotoxic toxins produced by other cyanobacteria, mainly Microcystis, which can colonize spirulina's open growing ponds. Market testing has found contamination in some products. This is the key consideration when choosing a brand: buy only from a manufacturer that publishes lab tests for microcystins and heavy metals.

**Question: What is really proven about spirulina?**
Answer: Meta-analyses of controlled trials found a moderate drop in total cholesterol, LDL and triglycerides at doses of 1-8 grams a day, and preliminary evidence of a slight reduction in blood pressure. The antioxidant activity of phycocyanin is well documented in the lab. Claims about "detox", binding heavy metals or weight loss are unsupported.

**Question: Who should not take spirulina?**
Answer: Three groups. People with phenylketonuria (PKU) — spirulina is very rich in phenylalanine. People with an autoimmune disease — there is a theoretical concern of worsening because of its effect on the immune system. And people taking anticoagulants, because of its vitamin K content — they need consistent intake rather than avoidance. During pregnancy and breastfeeding, it's worth consulting a doctor.

**Question: What is the recommended dose of spirulina?**
Answer: The typical dose is 1-5 grams a day, i.e. half a teaspoon to a level teaspoon. Studies have used doses of up to 8 grams, but there is no evidence that a higher dose is needed. It's best to start low and increase gradually, mainly because of the taste and the mild digestive discomfort some people experience at first.

**Question: How do you drink spirulina without the taste?**
Answer: Mix it into a smoothie with banana, berries or citrus juice — the sweetness and tartness mask the earthy, marine taste well. There are also capsules, which bypass the problem completely. A bonus: pairing it with a source of vitamin C improves absorption of its iron, so a fruit smoothie is also the right nutritional choice.

**Question: Is spirulina worth the price?**
Answer: It depends on the goal. As a protein source or a general "superfood" — no: the same money buys a large quantity of lentils, eggs or leafy greens with far greater nutritional value. As a targeted supplement for the lipid profile, or as a source of phycocyanin and copper — it makes sense, provided you choose a tested product.
