## Chocolate-Coated Cookies

Round chocolate-coated cookies (in Hebrew "עוגיות מצופות שוקולד") — among the most common snacks on the shelf — are built from three layers, each with its own nutritional role: **a base of white flour and sugar, sometimes a layer of filling or cream, and a coating that in most cases is not real chocolate.**

Understanding the third component is the key, and it is also the biggest difference between brands.

This category also includes [Argaliot](/en/food/argaliot/) and other filled cookies: a shortcrust dough based on white flour, a sugar-laden filling and a vegetable-fat coating.

### Nutrition facts

| Component | Per 100 g | Per single cookie (12-15 g) |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 480-520 | 60-78 |
| Sugar | 35-42 g | 4.5-6 g |
| Fat | 22-26 g | 3-4 g |
| Of which saturated | 12-16 g | 1.8-2.4 g |
| Carbohydrates | 62-68 g | 8-10 g |
| Dietary fiber | 1.5-2.5 g | ~0.3 g |
| Protein | 4-6 g | 0.6-0.9 g |
| [Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) | 250-400 mg | 35-55 mg |

**The practical figure:** three or four cookies — an amount easily eaten in front of a screen — come to about 250 calories and 20 grams of sugar, which is already **more than half the daily added-sugar limit** (25-36 grams).

### The coating — why it usually isn't chocolate

Real chocolate contains **cocoa butter**, an expensive fat with a melting point close to body temperature — that is where the melt-in-the-mouth feel comes from. In most coated cookies, the cocoa butter has been replaced with **hydrogenated vegetable fat or palm oil**.

How to spot this on the package:

* The words **"chocolate-flavored coating"**, **"cocoa coating"** or **"chocolate substitute"** — all of them mean there is no cocoa butter.
* **"Chocolate"** on its own requires, by standard, a minimum amount of cocoa solids and cocoa butter.
* In the ingredient list: if "vegetable fat" appears before "cocoa solids", it is the cheap coating.

**Why it matters nutritionally:** palm oil contains about 50% saturated fat, and hydrogenated vegetable fat may contain small residual amounts of trans fat. Cocoa butter is also saturated, but most of it is stearic acid, whose effect on LDL [cholesterol](/en/food/dark-chocolate/) is relatively neutral — unlike the palmitic acid in palm oil.

### Downsides

* **High calorie density with low satiety:** 480-520 calories per 100 grams, with almost no protein or fiber to slow absorption. This is a combination that encourages you to keep eating.
* **Sugar and white flour together:** a fast glycemic response followed by a drop, a well-known mechanism for renewed hunger within an hour or two.
* **Saturated fat from palm oil:** 12-16 grams per 100 grams.
* **An ultra-processed food:** beyond the individual ingredient, large cohort studies link high consumption of [ultra-processed foods](/en/food/ultra-processed-foods/) with increased cardiometabolic risk — partly because of the effect on eating speed and satiety.
* **Engineered for overconsumption:** the ratio of sugar to fat in coated cookies sits right at the point of maximum reward, which is why a single serving is a rare experience. See [food marketing to children](/en/food/marketing-to-children/).
* **Palm oil sustainability:** a leading cause of rainforest deforestation. The RSPO label indicates a certified source.

### Benefits

* **Enjoyment — and that is a legitimate consideration.** Sweet food within reasonable limits is not a nutritional problem, and treating it as "forbidden" tends to lead to overconsumption instead.
* **A measured serving:** a single cookie is 60-78 calories — a fairly small amount on its own. The problem is the package, not the unit.
* **Shelf life, convenience** and a low price.

### Practical choices

* **Real chocolate coating** — look for "chocolate" and not "chocolate-flavored coating", preferably dark chocolate.
* **A whole-wheat or oat base** — adds fiber and slightly improves satiety.
* **Take out a serving and close the package.** By far the most effective measure, not because of discipline but because the eating rate from the package is much higher.
* **Eat them after a meal, not instead of one** — the glycemic response is much milder.
* **Alternatives with a better profile:** [dark chocolate](/en/food/dark-chocolate/) above 70% (less sugar, more flavonoids), [dates](/en/food/dates/) with [peanut butter](/en/food/peanut-butter/), or [walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/) with a square of chocolate.

### Summary

A single coated cookie is 60-78 calories and about 5 grams of sugar — not dramatic in itself. The problem is that the sugar-to-fat ratio and the lack of protein and fiber turn eating one into eating five, and then you are at about 300 calories and over half your daily sugar allowance. The most significant choice when shopping is the coating: **"chocolate" and not "chocolate-flavored coating"** — the difference is between cocoa butter and palm oil. And the most significant habit at home is simply putting a serving on a plate instead of eating from the package.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in a chocolate-coated cookie?**
Answer: 60-78 calories in a single cookie (12-15 grams), which is 480-520 calories per 100 grams. Three or four cookies come to about 250 calories and 20 grams of sugar — more than half the daily added-sugar limit.

**Question: Is the coating on coated cookies real chocolate?**
Answer: In most cases, no. If it says "chocolate-flavored coating", "cocoa coating" or "chocolate substitute" — the cocoa butter has been replaced with palm oil or hydrogenated vegetable fat. Only the word "chocolate" on its own requires, by standard, a minimum amount of cocoa butter and cocoa solids.

**Question: How much sugar is in chocolate-coated cookies?**
Answer: 35-42 grams per 100 grams, which is 4.5-6 grams in a single cookie — close to a teaspoon and a half. The typical intake of 3-4 cookies reaches 20 grams, which is already more than half the recommended daily limit.

**Question: Why is it hard to stop after one cookie?**
Answer: The sugar-to-fat ratio in coated cookies sits at the point of maximum reward, and the absence of protein and fiber means there is almost no satiety signal to stop the eating. The most effective measure is to put a serving on a plate and close the package — the eating rate straight from the package is significantly higher.

**Question: What is a healthier alternative to chocolate-coated cookies?**
Answer: Dark chocolate above 70% provides less sugar and more flavonoids; dates with peanut butter provide fiber and protein alongside the sweetness; and walnuts with a square of chocolate are much more filling for the same number of calories.
