## Turmeric

> This page is about **turmeric as a spice** — what it actually contains, absorption, culinary use and safety. For information on **curcumin supplements** — doses, concentrated forms and clinical effectiveness — see [turmeric and curcumin as a supplement](/en/food/supplements/turmeric/).

**Turmeric**, scientific name *Curcuma longa*, is a root (a rhizome) of the [ginger](/en/food/ginger/) family. In Hebrew it is called "כורכום", and it is also nicknamed "Indian gold" because of its color.

Turmeric is probably **the spice with the most contested gap between hype and research**. Not because the research is negative — it is actually impressive — but because almost all of it was done on **concentrated, high-dose curcumin**, not on the teaspoon you sprinkle over [rice](/en/food/rice/). The gap between the two is the most important thing to understand here.

### How much curcumin is really in the spice

This is a question that almost never gets asked, and the answer surprises many people.

**Turmeric contains about 2-5% curcuminoids** — the group of active compounds, the main one being curcumin.

Let's translate that:

| | Amount | Curcuminoids |
|---|---|---|
| A teaspoon of ground turmeric | ~3 g | **~60-150 mg** |
| A tablespoon of ground turmeric | ~9 g | ~180-450 mg |
| Dose in clinical studies | — | **500-2,000 mg per day** |

In other words, to reach the dose studied in clinical trials on arthritis or inflammatory markers, you would need between **4 and 30 teaspoons of turmeric a day** — an amount no one eats.

**And then comes the second problem:** even from that tiny amount, absorption is very low.

### The bioavailability problem — and the solutions

Curcumin is a problematic molecule pharmacokinetically: it is **almost insoluble in water**, breaks down in the alkaline environment of the small intestine, and whatever is absorbed undergoes rapid metabolism in the liver and gut wall (glucuronidation and sulfation) and is excreted. Its plasma concentration after normal intake is so low that it sometimes can't be detected.

Three factors improve this, and all of them are easy to apply in the kitchen:

* **Black pepper — the most significant factor.** The piperine in pepper inhibits glucuronidation enzymes in the liver and gut, thereby slowing the clearance of curcumin. The classic study on the subject (Shoba et al., 1998) found that 20 mg of piperine increased the bioavailability of curcumin by **about 2,000% — twentyfold**. This is one of the strongest effects documented in a food-active ingredient interaction. **A pinch of black pepper is enough.**

* **Fat.** Curcumin is fat-soluble. Preparing it in a sauce with [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/), [coconut oil](/en/food/coconut/) or full-fat [milk](/en/food/milk/) improves its transfer into micelles and its absorption. That is why in Indian cooking turmeric is almost always fried in fat ("tempering") rather than sprinkled dry at the end.

* **Heat.** Brief heating increases curcumin's solubility. Very long heating actually breaks it down — adding it midway through cooking is better than boiling it for an hour.

**"Golden milk" is not a random trend:** turmeric + fat + black pepper + heat are exactly the four conditions for optimal absorption. The tradition came before the research.

### Nutrition facts — and what isn't there

| Nutrient | Per teaspoon (3 g) | Per 100 g |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 9 | 312 |
| Curcuminoids | 60-150 mg | 2-5 g |
| [Iron](/en/food/iron/) | 1.7 mg | 55 mg |
| [Manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/) | 0.6 mg | 19.8 mg |
| [Potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/) | 62 mg | 2,080 mg |

**An important caveat for reading the table:** the per-100-gram numbers look impressive — 55 mg of iron is a huge amount — but **no one eats 100 grams of turmeric**. That is about 33 teaspoons. At a realistic intake of a teaspoon a day, turmeric's mineral contribution is **marginal**, and all of its value lies in the curcuminoids and polyphenols. Don't treat turmeric as a source of iron.

### Benefits — what is established and what isn't

* **Anti-inflammatory activity — the strongest research base.** Curcumin inhibits the **NF-κB** pathway, a "master switch" that activates transcription of inflammatory genes, as well as the enzymes COX-2 and 5-LOX. Meta-analyses of controlled trials found a significant drop in inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, TNF-α) — **but almost all of them used supplements at doses of 500 mg and up**, not the spice.

* **Osteoarthritis:** several meta-analyses found improvements in pain and function, in some studies of a magnitude similar to NSAID drugs. Again — at supplement doses.

* **Antioxidant:** direct activity, plus activation of the **Nrf2** pathway, which increases production of endogenous protective enzymes such as glutathione.

* **Its contribution as a spice — what can reasonably be claimed.** Even if it is no substitute for a supplement, regularly including turmeric in cooking adds [polyphenols](/en/food/polyphenols/) to the diet, adds color and flavor **in place of salt**, and fits well into a cuisine rich in legumes and vegetables. That is a modest, justified claim — unlike "turmeric heals."

* **Digestion and bile:** turmeric stimulates bile secretion, which may help with fat digestion. This is also the basis for one of the warnings below.

### Downsides and risks

* **Lead contamination — the most serious risk, and the least known.** This is not a theoretical warning. **Lead chromate** is a cheap yellow pigment, and for years its use to color turmeric has been documented, to intensify its yellow color and increase weight — especially in supply chains from Bangladesh and India.

  It is **a documented source of lead poisoning** in humans. Health authorities in various countries have issued alerts and product recalls following findings. Lead is especially harmful to neurological development in children and fetuses, and there is no safe exposure threshold.

  **What to actually do:** buy from well-known brands with quality control and lab testing, prefer packaged, labeled turmeric over anonymous bulk spice at the market, and be wary of turmeric with an unusually glowing yellow-orange color at a particularly low price. **Fresh turmeric root** avoids the problem entirely.

* **Oxalates and kidney stones.** Turmeric is rich in oxalate — about 2 grams per 100 grams, among the highest of the spices. In a teaspoon that is a small amount, but **high intake or supplements** increase urinary oxalate excretion. People prone to calcium oxalate kidney stones ([calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/)) should be careful.

* **Inhibiting [iron](/en/food/iron/) absorption.** Curcumin binds iron (it is an iron chelator), and cases have been described in which taking curcumin supplements worsened iron deficiency. Paradoxically — the spice that contains iron also interferes with its absorption. Mainly relevant at supplement doses and for people with existing [iron deficiency](/en/food/iron/).

* **Anticoagulant effect.** Curcumin has antiplatelet activity. At spice doses this is negligible, but **stop curcumin supplements about two weeks before surgery**, and consult a doctor if you take warfarin, clopidogrel or other anticoagulants.

* **Gallbladder — a real contraindication.** Turmeric stimulates contraction of the gallbladder. In people with **gallstones or bile duct obstruction** this may trigger an attack of sharp pain. Do not take turmeric supplements in these conditions without medical advice.

* **Liver injury from supplements.** In recent years, **case reports of liver injury (DILI)** from high-dose curcumin supplements have been accumulating — especially from "enhanced bioavailability" formulations with piperine, which substantially raise blood curcumin concentrations. The phenomenon is rare, but documented and recognized by the authorities. **The spice in your kitchen has nothing to do with it.**

* **Digestive effects:** in large amounts — heartburn, nausea, diarrhea and stomach discomfort.

* **Pregnancy:** as a spice in food — safe and accepted. **Supplements at medicinal doses are not recommended** during pregnancy because of missing data.

* **Staining:** turmeric stubbornly stains utensils, countertops, nails and clothing. The color is light-sensitive — exposing a stained utensil to sunlight helps.

### Fresh vs. ground turmeric

| | Fresh root | Ground powder |
|---|---|---|
| Flavor | Fresh, earthy, slightly pungent | Deep, earthy, concentrated |
| Curcumin per unit weight | Lower (high water content) | Higher |
| Risk of lead contamination | **Almost zero** | Exists in some supply chains |
| Use | Grated into tea, marinades, smoothies | Stews, curry, rice |

Fresh is better for safety and freshness; ground is more convenient and concentrated. Combining the two is reasonable.

### Practical use

* **Always with a pinch of black pepper** — twentyfold bioavailability, at zero cost.
* **Always with fat** — [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/), [tahini](/en/food/tahini/), [coconut milk](/en/food/coconut/) or yogurt.
* **Fry lightly in oil** at the start of cooking, using the Indian "tempering" method, rather than sprinkling it dry at the end.
* **Golden milk:** a teaspoon of turmeric + a pinch of black pepper + [milk](/en/food/milk/) or a plant-based drink + a little fat, gently heated. An optimal combination for absorption.
* **A reasonable daily amount:** a teaspoon to a tablespoon in cooking. There is no need for, and no benefit in, overdoing it.
* **Choosing a product:** a known brand, labeled packaging, preference for fresh root. Avoid anonymous bulk spice with an unusually glowing color and a suspiciously low price.
* **Don't use it just for color** — turmeric added late in cooking contributes only color.
* **Storage:** an airtight, dark, cool container. Fresh root — in the fridge for up to two weeks, or in the freezer.
* **A supplement instead of the spice** — only when there is a defined clinical goal, and with medical advice. See [turmeric as a supplement](/en/food/supplements/turmeric/).

### Summary

Turmeric is an excellent spice with real research behind it — but **almost all of that research was done on concentrated curcumin, not on the spice**. A teaspoon of turmeric provides about 60-150 mg of curcuminoids, compared with 500-2,000 mg in clinical studies, and only a fraction of that is absorbed. The claim that "a teaspoon of turmeric a day replaces an anti-inflammatory drug" is not supported.

What is entirely justified: turmeric as **a regular spice in the kitchen** — it contributes polyphenols, replaces salt, and goes wonderfully with legumes and vegetables. And if you're using it anyway, **two steps multiply the benefit at zero cost: a pinch of black pepper (twentyfold absorption) and some fat alongside.**

And the warning worth taking seriously is not nutritional but about safety: **lead chromate contamination** in imported turmeric is a documented phenomenon with serious health consequences. A known brand, labeled packaging, or fresh root — solves it.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much curcumin is in a teaspoon of turmeric?**
Answer: About 60-150 mg. Turmeric contains only 2-5% curcuminoids, and a teaspoon is about 3 grams. For comparison, clinical studies use 500-2,000 mg a day — that is, 4 to 30 teaspoons. This is the key gap between the spice and a supplement.

**Question: Why do you need black pepper with turmeric?**
Answer: The piperine in pepper inhibits the breakdown enzymes in the liver and gut that clear curcumin quickly. The classic study on the subject found that adding piperine increased bioavailability by about 2,000% — twentyfold. A pinch of black pepper is enough, and it is the cheapest and most significant step.

**Question: Is turmeric as a spice enough, or do you need a supplement?**
Answer: It depends on the goal. As a regular spice in the kitchen it is a reasonable, justified addition of polyphenols, and it replaces salt. But achieving the anti-inflammatory effect measured in clinical studies requires a dose of 500 mg of curcuminoids or more — an impossible amount from the spice. See [turmeric as a supplement](/en/food/supplements/turmeric/).

**Question: What are the downsides of turmeric?**
Answer: As a spice it is very safe. The main considerations: possible lead chromate contamination in certain supply chains, high oxalate content (relevant for people prone to kidney stones), inhibition of iron absorption, gallbladder stimulation (a contraindication with gallstones), and an anticoagulant effect. Most of the risks only become significant at supplement doses.

**Question: Is there lead in turmeric?**
Answer: In some products — yes, and this is a real warning. Lead chromate has been used as a cheap pigment to intensify the yellow color and increase weight, mainly in supply chains from Bangladesh and India, and it is a documented source of lead poisoning. The solution: well-known brands with quality control, labeled packaging, or fresh turmeric root, which avoids the problem entirely.

**Question: What is golden milk and does it really help?**
Answer: A drink of turmeric, milk or a plant-based drink, fat and black pepper, gently heated. In terms of absorption it is an optimal combination — it provides exactly the four conditions that improve curcumin's bioavailability: fat, piperine, heat and solubility. The amount is still lower than a research dose, but it is the most effective way to consume turmeric as food.

**Question: Does turmeric raise or lower iron?**
Answer: It lowers absorption, even though it contains iron itself. Curcumin is an iron chelator — it binds iron and prevents its absorption, and cases have been described in which curcumin supplements worsened iron deficiency. Don't treat turmeric as a source of iron: a teaspoon provides 1.7 mg, and the impressive figure of 55 mg is per 100 grams — about 33 teaspoons.

**Question: Who should not consume turmeric?**
Answer: As a spice in food — almost everyone can. Supplements at medicinal doses are not suitable for people with gallstones or bile duct obstruction, people taking anticoagulants, people prone to oxalate kidney stones, pregnant women, and before surgery (stop about two weeks in advance). Consult a doctor.

**Question: Which is better — fresh or ground turmeric?**
Answer: Fresh is safer (no risk of lead contamination) and tastes fresher, but it has less curcumin per unit weight because of its water content. Ground is more concentrated and more convenient for cooking. Combining the two is reasonable: fresh for tea, smoothies and marinades, ground for stews and curry.

**Question: Is turmeric bad for the liver?**
Answer: The spice — no. But in recent years case reports of liver injury from high-dose curcumin supplements have been accumulating, especially from "enhanced bioavailability" formulations with piperine that greatly raise blood curcumin concentrations. The phenomenon is rare but recognized by the authorities. It has no connection to normal culinary use.
