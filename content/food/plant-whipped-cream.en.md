## Plant-Based Whipped Cream

Plant-based (non-dairy) whipped cream — in Hebrew "קצפת צמחית" — comes with a common assumption worth turning on its head: **"plant-based" and "pareve" (kosher non-dairy) are not synonyms for "low in saturated fat"** — and in the case of plant-based whipped cream, the opposite is true.

Dairy whipped cream is based on milk fat, which is about 65% saturated. Most plant-based whipped creams are based on **coconut oil or palm kernel oil**, which are **about 85-92% saturated** — among the most saturated fats found in nature.

In addition, unlike heavy cream, which contains a single ingredient, **plant-based whipped cream contains added sugar** — usually 15-20 grams per 100 ml.

That doesn't mean it has no place. It has three very good reasons to exist; they just aren't health reasons.

### Nutrition facts — a comparison

| Per 100 ml of the liquid product | Plant-based whipped cream | Heavy cream 38% |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 290-320 | 350 |
| Fat | 24-26 g | 37 g |
| **Of which saturated** | **20-23 g (100-115%)** | **23 g (115%)** |
| **Added sugar** | **15-20 g** | **0** |
| Protein | 0-1 g | 2 g |
| [Calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) | ~0 | 65 mg |
| Cholesterol | 0 | 110 mg |
| Ingredient list | 10-15 ingredients | 1 ingredient |

**The key point:** in terms of **saturated fat**, the two options are very similar. Plant-based whipped cream contains less fat overall, but **a much higher percentage of it is saturated** — so the gap in saturated fat almost closes. And on top of that it has sugar, which cream does not.

**In the whipped product** (the volume doubles), the numbers per 100 ml are roughly halved — but the same is true of whipped cream, and the comparison stays the same.

### What's inside

The ingredient list of a typical plant-based whipped cream: **water, coconut oil or palm kernel oil, sugar or glucose syrup, sodium caseinate or soy protein, emulsifiers, stabilizers, flavorings, food coloring.**

Two notes:

* **"Pareve" isn't always "dairy-free."** Many products contain **sodium caseinate** — a milk protein derivative that is permitted as kosher pareve at certain levels, but **is not suitable for people allergic to milk protein**. Anyone whose reason for choosing plant-based is an allergy must read the label and look for the allergen statement.
* **Trans fats — a closed chapter.** In the past, plant-based whipped creams were made from partially hydrogenated oil and contained trans fat. Regulation changed this, as happened with [margarine](/en/food/margarine/). It's still worth looking for "hydrogenated" in the ingredient list.

### The real reasons it exists

* **Kashrut.** A pareve dessert after a meat meal — the most common reason in Israel.
* **Allergy and intolerance.** Milk protein allergy or lactose intolerance — subject to the note on caseinate above.
* **Veganism.**
* **Technical stability.** Plant-based whipped cream whips easily, holds its shape for hours and doesn't "collapse" — which is why it is common in pastry shops. Heavy cream requires cold, care and serving soon after.

**What is not a reason: health.** There is no nutritional parameter on which plant-based whipped cream beats heavy cream.

### Benefits

* **A pareve, vegan and lactose-free solution** — as detailed above.
* **Stability and shape retention** without the need for added stabilizing.
* **Long shelf life** and simple storage; some products are sold frozen.
* **Slightly less fat and fewer calories per 100 ml** than dairy whipped cream — a real but modest difference.
* **Zero dietary cholesterol.**
* **A relatively low price** compared with quality cream.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Very high saturated fat as a percentage.** 85-92% of its fat — among the highest of any fat source, plant or animal.
* **Added sugar** — 15-20 grams per 100 ml, which cream has none of.
* **Zero protein and zero [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/).** Unlike dairy whipped cream, which provides a little of both.
* **An [ultra-processed food](/en/food/ultra-processed-foods/).** 10-15 ingredients, including emulsifiers, stabilizers, coloring and flavorings — compared with a single ingredient in cream.
* **Sodium caseinate** — as detailed above, a trap for people allergic to milk protein.
* **Palm oil and the environmental aspect** — rainforest clearing.
* **Empty calories.** Unlike almost any other food on these pages, it's hard to find any nutritional contribution here.
* **The misconception itself.** The product's biggest harm is that people choose it **believing it is healthier**, and so allow themselves more of it.

### Practical use

* **Use it for a reason, not out of belief.** Kashrut, allergy, veganism or stability — yes. "Healthier" — no.
* **Read the allergen statement** if the reason is a milk allergy. Look for **sodium caseinate** in the ingredient list.
* **Look for "hydrogenated"** and prefer a product without it.
* **Better vegan alternatives:**
  * **Whipped coconut cream** — a can of coconut milk chilled overnight, the solid part whipped. Still saturated, but **a single ingredient with no added sugar** — sweeten to taste.
  * **Aquafaba** — the liquid from cooking [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/), whipped into a stable foam. **Almost zero calories and zero fat.**
  * **[Cashew](/en/food/cashews/) cream** — soaked cashews blended with a little water and a date. Monounsaturated fat, protein and fiber.
  * **Strained [Greek yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/)** (not pareve) — tangy, rich in protein, and about 59 calories per 100 grams.
* **Measure.** A reasonable serving of whipped cream on a dessert is 20-30 grams, about 60-90 calories. A generous heap is 200.
* **Don't use it as a regular "topping"** for fruit or coffee — that's where it turns from an occasional dessert into a daily habit.

### Summary

Plant-based whipped cream is a product with three excellent reasons to exist — **pareve kashrut, milk allergy and veganism** — and none of them is a health reason. In terms of saturated fat it is no better than heavy cream, because coconut oil and palm kernel oil are 85-92% saturated, compared with 65% for milk fat. **On top of that, it contains 15-20 grams of added sugar and zero protein and calcium.** If you choose it for a good reason — go ahead, in a measured portion. And if you're looking for a better vegan alternative: **whipped coconut cream or aquafaba** give a similar result with a one-item ingredient list.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Is plant-based whipped cream healthier than dairy whipped cream?**
Answer: No. It contains slightly less fat and fewer calories per 100 ml, but its percentage of saturated fat is much higher — coconut oil and palm kernel oil are 85-92% saturated, compared with about 65% for milk fat — so the actual amount of saturated fat is similar. In addition, it contains 15-20 grams of added sugar, which cream has none of, and zero protein and calcium.

**Question: Is pareve whipped cream suitable for people allergic to milk?**
Answer: Not necessarily, and this is an important trap. Many products labeled "pareve" contain **sodium caseinate**, a milk protein derivative that is permitted under pareve kashrut at certain concentrations but is dangerous for people allergic to milk protein. If your reason is an allergy, you must read the allergen statement and the ingredient list, not just the "pareve" label.

**Question: How many calories are in plant-based whipped cream?**
Answer: 290-320 calories per 100 ml of the liquid product, before whipping. Because whipping doubles the volume, the value per 100 ml whipped is roughly halved. A reasonable serving on a dessert is 20-30 grams, about 60-90 calories; a generous heap easily reaches 200.

**Question: Why do pastry shops use plant-based whipped cream?**
Answer: For a technical rather than nutritional reason. It whips easily, holds its shape for many hours and doesn't "collapse" or weep liquid, unlike heavy cream, which requires cold, care in whipping and serving soon after. It is also pareve, which allows it to be served after a meat meal.

**Question: What is a good alternative to plant-based whipped cream?**
Answer: Two stand out. Whipped coconut cream — chill a can of coconut milk overnight, scoop out the solid part and whip it — still saturated, but a single ingredient with no added sugar. And aquafaba, the liquid from cooking chickpeas whipped into a stable foam, with almost zero calories and fat. Cashew cream and strained Greek yogurt are other options.

**Question: Does plant-based whipped cream contain trans fat?**
Answer: Hardly at all, nowadays. In the past it was made from partially hydrogenated oils and contained trans fats, but global regulation changed this, as happened with margarine. It's still worth looking for the word "hydrogenated" in the ingredient list and preferring a product without it.

**Question: What exactly is in plant-based whipped cream?**
Answer: A typical list includes water, coconut oil or palm kernel oil, sugar or glucose syrup, sodium caseinate or soy protein, emulsifiers, stabilizers, flavorings and food coloring — 10 to 15 ingredients. For comparison, heavy cream contains a single ingredient.

**Question: Which is better for dessert — whipped cream or yogurt?**
Answer: Nutritionally, strained Greek yogurt is clearly better: about 59 calories per 100 grams with 10 grams of protein and calcium, compared with 290-320 calories and zero protein in plant-based whipped cream. Its taste is tangy rather than sweet, so it isn't a full substitute in every dessert — but on fruit, in a granola bowl or alongside cake it works great.
