## Sardines

Sardines are probably **the food with the best ratio of nutritional value to price** on the entire shelf. In Hebrew they are called "סרדינים". A single can provides complete protein, [omega-3](/en/food/supplements/omega-3/) in amounts close to [salmon](/en/food/salmon/), [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) in amounts that rival milk, and a surplus of [vitamin D](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-d/) and [vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/) — for just a few shekels.

The reason for their special status is biological: the sardine is a small fish that feeds on plankton and lives only 3-5 years. It sits at the bottom of the food chain, so it **barely accumulates any mercury** — unlike the predatory, long-lived [tuna](/en/food/tuna/).

### Calcium — the standout figure

382 mg of [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) per 100 grams is more than a glass of [milk](/en/food/milk/) (about 300 mg), and that is an unusual figure for an animal food that is not a dairy product.

The source is **the small bones**, which soften completely during canning and are eaten with the fish without being noticed. This is calcium with good bioavailability, accompanied by [vitamin D](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-d/) and [vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/), which are needed to direct it to bone — a combination that is hard to find in a single food.

**The practical takeaway:** boneless sardines lose this key advantage almost entirely. It is worth choosing the whole version.

### Benefits

* **Omega-3 in amounts that rival salmon:** 1,400-1,500 mg per 100 grams, about one third to two thirds of [salmon](/en/food/salmon/) — and five times more than canned light [tuna](/en/food/tuna/). One can covers the weekly recommendation.
* **Among the lowest mercury levels measured:** about 0.013 ppm, almost ten times less than light tuna. Sardines are considered safe for frequent consumption, including during pregnancy — and thanks to their DHA they are even recommended.
* **Complete protein:** 24.6 grams per 100 grams.
* **A surplus of [vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/):** 370% of the Daily Value per 100 grams.
* **[Selenium](/en/food/supplements/selenium/):** almost 100% of the Daily Value. Selenium also plays a role in binding mercury and reducing its availability.
* **Sustainability:** sardines are among the most sustainable fish. They reproduce quickly, are caught with targeted nets with little bycatch, and their stocks are stable in most regions.
* **Price and shelf life:** unbeatable. A can costs a few shekels and needs no refrigeration.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Purines and gout:** this is the most significant downside. Sardines are among the most purine-rich foods there are (over 400 mg per 100 grams), and they appear explicitly on the list of foods to avoid with active gout or high uric acid.
* **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/):** 505 mg per 100 grams. Reduced-sodium versions exist, and draining and rinsing removes some of it.
* **Oil vs. water — not like tuna.** With [tuna](/en/food/tuna/) the rule is simple: water is better. With sardines it is more complicated. Good-quality [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) does no harm, but **soybean or sunflower oil adds omega-6 and worsens the ratio**, and also draws away some of the fish's omega-3 when you drain it. The recommended order: in water or [tomato](/en/food/tomato/) sauce, then in olive oil, and only last in another vegetable oil.
* **Strong taste and smell:** the main practical obstacle for most people. [Lemon](/en/food/lemon/), vinegar, red [onion](/en/food/onion/) or mustard tone it down considerably.
* **Histamine:** like all mackerel-type and sardine-family fish, they can develop histamine if not chilled properly before canning. Histamine is heat-stable and is not destroyed by cooking.
* **Fish allergy** and Anisakis sensitivity.
* **BPA in the can lining:** most manufacturers have switched to BPA-free linings, but not all. Cans marked "BPA-free" are preferable for anyone who cares about the issue.

### Practical use

* **Frequency:** 1-2 cans a week cover the full omega-3 recommendation at negligible cost.
* **Choose bone-in** — that is where the calcium is.
* **On sourdough bread** with [lemon](/en/food/lemon/), olive oil and onion — the simplest and most common way.
* **If you find the taste hard:** mash them into a tomato sauce for pasta. They melt in completely and add umami depth without being recognizable.
* **Fresh sardines** on the grill are a classic Mediterranean dish, and very cheap in season.
* **Pair with a source of [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/)** to improve iron absorption.

### Summary

A can of sardines provides 24.6 grams of protein, about 1,450 mg of omega-3, 382 mg of calcium, 370% of the Daily Value of B12 and almost 100% of selenium — with mercury among the lowest measured in any fish, good sustainability and a price of a few shekels. Two real caveats: people with gout should avoid them because of their high purine content, and it is worth choosing sardines in water, tomato sauce or olive oil — not in cheap vegetable oil. If you had to pick one food that gives the highest nutritional return per shekel, this is probably the candidate.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is the nutritional value of sardines?**
Answer: 100 grams provide 208 calories, 24.6 grams of protein, 1,400-1,500 mg of omega-3, 382 mg of calcium, 8.9 mcg of vitamin B12 (370% of the Daily Value) and 52.7 mcg of selenium. It is one of the most nutrient-dense profiles of any affordable, widely available food.

**Question: How much omega-3 is in sardines?**
Answer: 1,400-1,500 mg of EPA+DHA per 100 grams — about one third to two thirds of salmon, but five times more than canned light tuna. One can covers the weekly omega-3 recommendation.

**Question: Do sardines contain mercury?**
Answer: Very little — about 0.013 ppm, almost ten times less than light tuna. The sardine is a small fish that feeds on plankton and lives 3-5 years, so it does not accumulate mercury up the food chain. It is considered safe for frequent consumption, including during pregnancy.

**Question: Should you eat the bones in sardines?**
Answer: Yes, and this is the important point. The bones soften completely during canning and are the source of the 382 mg of calcium per 100 grams — more than a glass of milk. Boneless sardines lose this advantage almost entirely.

**Question: Sardines in oil or in water?**
Answer: Unlike tuna, the answer depends on the type of oil. Good-quality olive oil does no harm; soybean or sunflower oil adds omega-6, worsens the ratio and draws away some of the omega-3 when drained. The recommended order: in water or tomato sauce, then in olive oil.

**Question: Who should avoid sardines?**
Answer: Mainly people with gout or high uric acid — sardines are among the most purine-rich foods there are (over 400 mg per 100 grams) and appear explicitly on avoidance lists. Also people allergic to fish and anyone who needs strict sodium restriction.
