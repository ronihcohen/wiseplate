## Sugary Soft Drinks

**A single 330 ml can of sugar-sweetened soft drink, such as cola, contains about 35 g of sugar — roughly 9 teaspoons — which is more than the World Health Organization's ideal limit for a whole day.** Sugar-sweetened beverages are the largest source of added sugar for many people, and regular consumption is linked in research to weight gain, type 2 diabetes and tooth decay. In Hebrew they are called "משקאות מוגזים ממותקים".

The category includes colas, fruit-flavoured sodas, sweetened iced tea, energy drinks and "fruit drinks" that are not real juice. Some also contain [caffeine](/en/food/supplements/caffeine/).

### Nutrition facts

A regular cola (approximate; values vary between brands):

| Nutrient | Per 100 ml | Per can (330 ml) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calories | about 42 | about 140 |
| Sugars | about 10.6 g | about 35 g |
| Protein, fat, fibre | 0 | 0 |
| Sodium | about 5 mg | about 15 mg |
| Caffeine (in cola) | about 10 mg | about 32 mg |

The drink contains no vitamins, minerals or fibre — only sugar, water, acid (usually phosphoric or citric), flavourings and colour.

### Benefits

* **Fast energy:** during a run or ride of more than about 90 minutes, sugar in liquid form is a quickly absorbed fuel. Sports drinks are designed for exactly that.
* **Certain medical situations:** people with diabetes treated with insulin sometimes use a sugary drink to treat low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia) quickly.
* **Enjoyment and social life:** as an occasional treat, it is a legitimate pleasure.

### Downsides

* **Type 2 diabetes:** in a meta-analysis of 11 cohort studies, people who drank 1–2 servings a day had a 26% higher risk of type 2 diabetes than those who rarely drank them. [Malik 2010, Diabetes Care](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20693348/) A later meta-analysis found a 13% higher risk per daily serving, even after accounting for body weight. [Imamura 2015, BMJ](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26199070/)
* **Weight:** in an 18-month trial in children, replacing a daily sugar-sweetened drink with an identical sugar-free one reduced gains in weight and body fat. [de Ruyter 2012, NEJM](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22998340/) Liquid calories are probably less well compensated for by eating less later.
* **Tooth decay and erosion:** the sugar feeds bacteria that produce acid, and the drink itself is acidic. A systematic review found that caries is lower when free sugars are below 10% of calories. [Moynihan 2014, J Dent Res](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24323509/)
* **Liver and blood lipids:** excess fructose from any source, including [corn syrup](/en/food/corn-syrup/) and white sugar, raises liver fat and triglycerides when it comes with surplus calories.
* **Caffeine:** in large amounts, and in the evening, it can disturb sleep, especially in children.

### How it compares with the recommendation

The WHO recommends limiting free sugars to less than 10% of calories, and ideally less than 5% — on a 2,000-calorie diet, under 50 g a day and ideally about 25 g. [WHO — Guideline: sugars intake](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241549028) One can exceeds the stricter target, and two cans approach the upper limit.

### Policy: what works

* **Israel:** a tax on sugary drinks took effect in January 2022 and was repealed a year later. Sales of large taxed bottles fell by about 24–31% during the tax year, then crept back after repeal while staying below earlier levels. [Fliss Isakov 2026, Public Health](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41518863/)
* **Chile:** a package of laws that included warning labels, limits on marketing to children and a ban on sales in schools reduced purchases of "high-in-sugar" drinks by about 24%. [Taillie 2020, PLoS Med](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32045424/)

### Practical use

* **Water as the default:** water, soda water, water with lemon or mint, unsweetened [tea](/en/food/green-tea/).
* **Step down gradually:** if quitting is hard, a temporary switch to [diet drinks](/en/food/artificial-sweeteners-trap/) does cut sugar on the way to water.
* **Don't stock it:** what isn't in the fridge won't be drunk out of habit.
* **Fruit juice is not an alternative:** even natural juice counts as free sugar.
* **Sport:** a sugary or sports drink suits long efforts, not a half-hour workout.

### Summary

Sugary soft drinks supply sugar and calories with no other nutritional value, and one can exceeds the recommended daily sugar target. Cohort studies, a controlled trial in children and policy data from Israel and Chile all point the same way: fewer sugary drinks means less sugar, less tooth decay and a lower risk of diabetes. The practical step is to make water the default.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How much sugar is in a can of cola?**
Answer: About 35 g in a 330 ml can — roughly 9 teaspoons of sugar and about 140 calories.

**Question: Is one can a day harmful?**
Answer: One can a day exceeds the WHO's stricter sugar target (about 25 g), and in studies one serving a day was linked to a higher risk of type 2 diabetes.

**Question: Is a diet drink better?**
Answer: In terms of sugar and calories, yes. A trial in children found that switching to a sugar-free drink reduced weight gain. Still, the WHO does not recommend relying on sweeteners for weight loss, and the end goal is water.

**Question: Is natural fruit juice healthier?**
Answer: It has some vitamins, but its sugar counts as free sugar, just like a soft drink. Eating the whole fruit is better.

**Question: Why are calories from drinks more fattening?**
Answer: Liquids are less filling than solid food, and people usually don't eat less at the next meal to make up for the calories they drank.

**Question: Did Israel's sugary drinks tax work?**
Answer: According to a Ministry of Health study, sales of large taxed bottles fell by about 24–31% during the tax year. After the tax was repealed, sales rose gradually but stayed below earlier levels.
