## Sugary Soft Drinks

Sugar-sweetened carbonated drinks (known as soda or soft drinks; in Hebrew, "משקאות מוגזים ממותקים") are drinks with a high sugar content and sometimes [caffeine](/en/food/supplements/caffeine/) and flavorings. They are common around the world and raise many health questions.

### Benefits
- A fast, accessible energy source: they provide quick calories, especially in sports settings or short-term fatigue.
- Sensory and cultural appeal: popular as part of social and leisure customs, and they add to the enjoyment of a meal.
- Dietary options: there are low-sugar or sugar-free versions that offer a low-calorie alternative (although these are a subject of debate regarding the long-term effects of [artificial sweeteners](/en/food/artificial-sweeteners-trap/)).

### Downsides and risks
- Risk of obesity and metabolic disease: regular consumption of sweetened drinks is linked to rates of obesity, type 2 diabetes, [insulin](/en/food/supplements/insulin/) resistance and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).
- Liquid calories without satiety: calories in drinks do not contribute to satiety signals to the same extent as solid food — contributing to excess calorie intake.
- Tooth decay and dental erosion: acidity and sugar cause cavities and erosion of the enamel.
- Additional ingredients: [caffeine](/en/food/supplements/caffeine/) and acids (phosphoric/citric acid) can affect various systems and cause effects such as insomnia, and with a poor diet may even harm the [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) balance in bone.

### Important biological mechanisms
- Fructose metabolism: high-fructose [corn syrup](/en/food/corn-syrup/) (HFCS) and free fructose feed fat synthesis processes in the liver, contributing to triglycerides and insulin resistance.
- Effect on reward centers: sugars can activate reward pathways in the brain, leading to repeat consumption and increased eating patterns.

### Practical findings and evidence-based recommendations
- Limit intake: aim to reduce or avoid sweetened drinks as much as possible as part of a healthy diet.
- Prefer unsweetened drinks: water, unsweetened tea and unsweetened sparkling water are preferable alternatives.
- Low-sugar options: diet versions should be considered with caution; there is mixed research on the effects of artificial sweeteners on the microbiota and metabolic regulation.
- Public policy: reducing consumption can be supported by policy measures such as taxes on sweetened drinks, changing availability in schools and educational campaigns.

### Limits of knowledge and research
Some of the research focuses on causal links that are hard to prove in dietary observational studies, but prospective and randomized studies point to harmful effects of high consumption of sweetened drinks.

### Summary
Sugary soft drinks provide energy and immediate pleasure but contribute to a high risk of obesity, diabetes, metabolic problems and dental problems. Preferring water and unsweetened drinks, along with limiting intake, contributes to public and individual health.
