## Popped Amaranth: The Tiny Grain That Pops Without Oil

[Amaranth](/en/food/amaranth/) seeds (*Amaranthus*) are smaller than a grain of sand — about one millimeter in diameter — but they behave exactly like [corn](/en/food/corn/) kernels: when you heat them in a very hot, dry pan, the moisture trapped inside turns into steam, pushes against the seed coat and bursts it from the inside. The result is a tiny white flake with a nutty, toasted flavor.

This isn't a new trend: in Mexico, *alegría* (Spanish for "joy") has been made this way for hundreds of years — a traditional snack of popped amaranth bound together with honey or cane syrup. The plant was a staple food of the Aztecs, and popping was its main method of preparation.

100 grams of popped amaranth provides about 370 calories, 14 grams of protein, 7 grams of fat, 65 grams of carbohydrates and 6.7 grams of dietary fiber — almost identical to the raw seed, since popping adds or removes nothing except water. But this is a misleading measure: the popcorn is very airy, and a realistic serving is only 15–20 grams (a full small bowl), meaning about 55–75 calories.

### Health benefits

* **Complete protein — rare in the world of grains:** Unlike wheat, [corn](/en/food/corn/) and [rice](/en/food/rice/), amaranth contains all nine essential amino acids, especially **lysine** — the amino acid that is almost always lacking in grains. 14 grams of protein per 100 grams is almost twice that of corn popcorn.
* **Naturally [gluten-free](/en/food/gluten/):** Amaranth is a pseudo-grain and is not related to wheat, rye or [barley](/en/food/barley/). It is suitable for people with celiac disease — provided the package declares production free of cross-contamination with gluten grains.
* **Exceptional mineral density:** 100 grams provide about 248 mg of [magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) (about 60% of the daily intake), 7.6 mg of [iron](/en/food/iron/) and 159 mg of [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) — several times more than any common grain. Even in a small snack serving, it's a significant contribution.
* **A snack with no oil at all:** The popping is done in a completely dry pan. This is one of the few cases where a crunchy snack doesn't involve frying or added fat — unlike commercial corn popcorn, where most of the calories come from oil and butter.
* **Squalene and tocotrienols:** Amaranth oil is rich in squalene — a compound being studied in connection with skin health and cholesterol metabolism — and in tocotrienols from the [vitamin E](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-e/) family, antioxidants that have been linked to a beneficial effect on the blood lipid profile.
* **The heat reduces some of the antinutrients:** Popping at high heat somewhat reduces the phytic acid and saponin content of the seed, and improves mineral availability compared with the raw seed.

### Downsides and considerations

* **High glycemic index:** Popping gelatinizes the starch and opens it up to digestive enzymes. Popped amaranth is digested quickly and ranks high on the glycemic index — higher than cooked amaranth. Combining it with [tahini](/en/food/tahini/), yogurt or nuts moderates the glycemic response.
* **Commercial versions are very sweet:** Alegría bars, [energy bars](/en/food/processed-energy-bars/) and breakfast cereals based on popped amaranth are usually bound with honey, silan (date syrup) or glucose syrup. In some products sugar is the first ingredient on the list — which cancels out the nutritional advantage.
* **Easy to eat a lot:** The airy volume is deceptive. A large bowl looks like nothing, but 60 grams of popped amaranth is already more than 200 calories.
* **Burnt, bitter grains:** Amaranth is small and pops within seconds; a second or two of inattention and some grains burn and make the whole batch bitter.
* **Partial popping rate:** Even with good technique, about 15–25% of the seeds won't pop and will stay hard as sand. They are edible but not especially pleasant.

### How to pop amaranth at home

1. **A dry, heavy pan:** Heat a thick-bottomed pan (no oil, no water) over high heat for 2–3 minutes.
2. **Heat test:** Toss in 3–4 grains. If they pop within 2–3 seconds — the pan is ready. If they turn black without popping — it's too hot.
3. **A small amount at a time:** Only one to two tablespoons, in a thin layer. A large amount lowers the pan's temperature and produces more burnt grains than popped ones.
4. **Shake constantly:** Shake the pan nonstop for 8–15 seconds, until most of the grains have popped.
5. **Empty immediately:** Pour into a cold bowl as soon as the popping slows down — the residual heat in the pan burns whatever is left in it.

### Uses in the kitchen

The simplest: sprinkle it over yogurt, [oatmeal](/en/food/oatmeal/) porridge or fruit salad. You can mix it with [tahini](/en/food/tahini/) and [honey](/en/food/honey/) and let it set in the fridge for an alegría-style snack, add it to homemade granola, sprinkle it on soup as a crunchy substitute for croutons, or use it as a gluten-free coating for schnitzel and patties.

### Summary

Popped amaranth is one of the most nutritious crunchy snacks there is: complete protein, magnesium and iron in exceptional amounts, gluten-free and without a drop of oil. The only two traps are the sweetened commercial versions and the high glycemic index of the popped grain — both solved by making it at home and pairing it with a source of protein or fat.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Can you pop amaranth in the microwave like popcorn?**
Answer: It's less recommended. The seeds are too small to heat evenly in a bag, and the result is usually a mix of burnt grains and unpopped grains. A dry pan over high heat gives much better control.

**Question: What's the nutritional difference between popped and cooked amaranth?**
Answer: The dry composition is almost identical, but cooked amaranth absorbs water, so its calorie density per serving is much lower (about 100 calories per cooked cup). Popped amaranth is digested faster and has a higher glycemic index.

**Question: Is popped amaranth suitable for children?**
Answer: Yes, and it's a good alternative to sweetened cereal snacks. However, because of its tiny size and dry texture, it's best served to toddlers mixed into yogurt or a spread, not as a dry powder on its own.

**Question: How long does it keep?**
Answer: In an airtight container at room temperature, it stays crunchy for about 5–7 days. Amaranth oil is rich in unsaturated fat and oxidizes relatively quickly — so it's better to pop a small amount at a time rather than store it for weeks.
