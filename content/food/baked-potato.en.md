## Baked Potato: the basic vegetable that doesn't get enough credit

The potato (*Solanum tuberosum*) is one of the most important food plants in human history, ranking fourth among the world's major food crops after wheat, rice and [corn](/en/food/corn/). It is often seen as a "starchy food" to be avoided, but when eaten baked (not fried) and with its skin, the potato is a very nutrient-rich vegetable that is cheap and accessible.

A medium baked potato with skin (about 170 grams) contains about 160 calories, 4.3 grams of protein, 0.2 grams of fat and 37 grams of carbohydrates (of which 3.8 grams are dietary fiber), and is an excellent source of [potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/), vitamin C, vitamin B6, [manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/) and copper.

### How many carbs are in a potato?

This is the most common question about it, so it is worth answering with exact numbers:

| | Per 100 grams baked with skin | Medium potato (170 grams) | Large potato (300 grams) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Carbohydrates** | **21.5 grams** | **37 grams** | **64 grams** |
| Of which fiber | 2.2 grams | 3.8 grams | 6.6 grams |
| **Net carbs** | **19.3 grams** | **33 grams** | **57 grams** |
| Calories | 93 | 160 | 278 |
| Protein | 2.5 grams | 4.3 grams | 7.5 grams |
| Fat | 0.1 grams | 0.2 grams | 0.3 grams |

**For a quick comparison:** the 37 grams of carbohydrates in a medium potato are roughly equivalent to two slices of [bread](/en/food/sourdough/), or about 100 grams of cooked [rice](/en/food/rice/).

### Research-based health benefits

* **Exceptionally rich in potassium:** a medium potato contains about 926 mg of [potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/) — more than a [banana](/en/food/banana/) (about 422 mg) and the vast majority of common foods. Potassium is an essential electrolyte for regulating blood pressure, normal heart function and nerve conduction. A potassium-rich diet is linked in the scientific literature to a lower prevalence of high blood pressure and a reduced risk of stroke.

* **Resistant starch after cooling:** when a potato is cooked and then cooled, some of the regular starch undergoes retrogradation and becomes resistant starch — a kind of prebiotic fiber that is not absorbed in the small intestine but reaches the large intestine intact. There it is fermented by beneficial gut bacteria, producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) such as butyrate, and helps maintain a healthy gut lining and prevent inflammatory diseases.

* **A high concentration of vitamin C:** a potato baked with its skin contains about 17 mg of vitamin C (about 19% of the recommended daily intake). Historically, potatoes served as a key source of vitamin C for sailors on long voyages and for poor populations, and helped eradicate scurvy.

* **Vitamin B6 for brain health:** one serving provides about 0.5 mg of B6 (about 27% of the daily intake), a vitamin involved in the synthesis of neurotransmitters such as serotonin, dopamine and GABA. B6 deficiency is linked to depression, irritability and impaired memory.

* **Antioxidant content in the skin:** the potato skin is the part richest in antioxidants — flavonoids, chlorogenic acid and carotenoids. Studies have shown that eating it with the skin significantly raises the antioxidant profile of the serving. It also raises the fiber content from 1.5 grams (without skin) to 3.8 grams (with skin).

* **Quality plant protein:** unlike most vegetables, the potato contains protein of relatively high biological value. Its main protein, patatin, has a respectable PDCAAS score and a high content of lysine — an essential amino acid that is sometimes lacking in plant-based diets.

* **Exceptional satiety:** a study published in the *European Journal of Clinical Nutrition* (1995) ranked boiled potato as the most filling food (satiety index) of 38 foods tested — more than bread, pasta, [eggs](/en/food/eggs/) and beef. The high satiety comes from its water and fiber content and its large volume relative to its calorie density.

### Downsides and health considerations

* **High glycemic index when baked:** a baked potato that has not been cooled ranks among the foods with the highest glycemic index (GI of about 85–111 depending on the variety and cooking method). This causes a relatively rapid rise in blood glucose after eating and can be problematic for people with diabetes or [insulin](/en/food/supplements/insulin/) resistance.

* **Solanine — a natural toxin:** potatoes contain glycoalkaloids (mainly solanine and chaconine), which are the plant's defense compounds. Their concentration is high in green skin and sprouts — the green color signals exposure to light and a sharp rise in solanine. Remove green parts and sprouts before eating, and discard extensively green or sprouted tubers.

* **"Empty" starch without the right accompaniment:** a baked potato on its own, without protein and fat, produces a relatively large sugar spike. Pairing it with olive oil, cottage cheese, an egg or legumes helps slow digestion and keep glucose levels stable.

* **The carb load doesn't suit every plan:** although a baked potato is healthy in the context of an overall diet, it is not suitable for ketogenic diets, the Atkins diet or "low-carb" approaches of under 50 grams a day, since one serving already provides about 37 grams of carbohydrates.

* **Nightshade alkaloids:** for people with rheumatoid arthritis, there is an unresolved scientific debate about whether vegetables in the nightshade family (potato, tomato, eggplant, pepper) worsen inflammation because of small amounts of alkaloids. Anecdotal evidence suggests improvement in some patients who limit these vegetables, but the clinical data are still inconclusive.

### Cooking methods and their nutritional effect

| Preparation method | Calories (100 grams) | Glycemic index | Fat | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baked with skin | ~93 | ~85 | 0.1 grams | Nutritionally optimal |
| Boiled in water (peeled) | ~87 | ~78 | 0.1 grams | Water-soluble vitamins lost to the cooking water |
| Mashed with butter | ~115 | ~85 | 4 grams | Added calories and saturated fat |
| Deep-fried fries | ~310 | ~75 | 15 grams | High in calories and fat, contains acrylamide |
| Boiled and cooled | ~87 | ~55 | 0.1 grams | Increased resistant starch |

### Summary

The baked potato is a classic example of a food that has fallen victim to a bad reputation, with real nutritional value hiding behind it. Eaten with its skin and without frying oil, it provides exceptional potassium, vitamin C, B6, fiber and resistant starch — at a moderate calorie level. The problem is not the potato itself, but what is added to it (cream, butter, cheese) and how it is prepared.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many carbs are in a potato?**
Answer: 21.5 grams per 100 grams baked with skin. A medium potato (170 grams) has about 37 grams of carbohydrates, of which 3.8 grams are fiber, meaning about 33 grams of net carbs. A large potato (300 grams) has about 64 grams. For comparison, 37 grams are roughly equivalent to two slices of bread or about 100 grams of cooked rice.

**Question: How many calories are in a baked potato?**
Answer: 93 calories per 100 grams baked with skin, meaning about 160 calories in a medium potato (170 grams) and about 278 in a large one. That is before toppings — a tablespoon of butter adds about 100 calories, and sour cream about 30 per tablespoon.

**Question: How many calories are in a boiled potato?**
Answer: About 87 calories per 100 grams boiled and peeled — slightly less than the baked version. The difference comes mainly from water content: in baking, moisture evaporates from the surface and the flesh concentrates slightly, while in boiling the moisture is retained and even rises slightly, which slightly dilutes the calorie density. In addition, some of the water-soluble vitamins (such as vitamin C and B6) leach into the cooking water, as detailed in the preparation methods table above. A boiled potato that has cooled (for example, for potato salad) also contains more resistant starch, which slightly lowers its glycemic impact compared with a hot boiled one.

**Question: Do potatoes accelerate weight gain?**
Answer: Not on their own. A baked potato without fatty toppings is low in fat and very filling. Weight gain associated with potatoes usually comes from preparing them as fries, mashed with butter, or from very large servings.

**Question: Which is better — baking or boiling?**
Answer: Baking with the skin preserves more nutrients than boiling in water (which causes water-soluble vitamins such as C and B6 to leach into the cooking water). Cooling after boiling/baking adds the benefit of resistant starch.

**Question: Is it safe to eat potato skin?**
Answer: Yes, as long as the skin is not green and there are no sprouts. Green skin contains high concentrations of solanine — remove it and throw it away. Regular brown skin is completely safe and rich in nutrients.
