## Red Meat

"Red meat" refers to the meat of mammals — beef, [lamb](/en/food/lamb/), pork and veal — so called because of the high myoglobin content of the muscle tissue. Nutritionally it is one of the densest foods in bioavailable nutrients, and at the same time one of the most controversial foods in public health.

100 grams of cooked lean beef provide about 215-250 calories, 26 grams of protein, 9-17 grams of fat and zero carbohydrates.

### The key distinction: fresh meat versus processed meat

This is the distinction that shapes almost every discussion of the subject, and it is often blurred.

In 2015 the World Health Organization's International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified:

* **Processed meat** — [sausages](/en/food/sausages/), [salami](/en/food/salami/), bacon, kabanos — as **Group 1**: carcinogenic to humans. The classification is based on consistent evidence for colorectal cancer.
* **Fresh red meat** — as **Group 2A**: "probably carcinogenic", based on limited evidence.

A point that is almost always misunderstood: **Group 1 describes the strength of the evidence, not the size of the risk**. Smoking is in the same group as processed meat — but the size of the risk differs by orders of magnitude. The accepted estimate is that every 50 grams of processed meat a day raises the relative risk of colorectal cancer by about 18%; because the baseline risk is low, the absolute increase is small.

The common recommendation of health bodies: limit fresh red meat to about 350-500 grams cooked per week, and cut down on processed meat as much as possible.

### Health benefits

* **Heme [iron](/en/food/iron/) — the clearest benefit:** the iron in red meat is in heme form, which is absorbed at 15%-35% compared with 2%-20% for plant iron. Just as important, its absorption is barely affected by inhibitors such as phytate or [tannins](/en/food/tannins/). This is a clinically significant advantage for women of reproductive age, pregnant women and [endurance runners](/en/run/running-zones/) — groups at high risk of iron deficiency.

* **[Vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/):** 100 grams provide about 2.5 mcg — close to the entire daily requirement. B12 is found almost exclusively in animal foods, and a deficiency causes irreversible neurological damage if left untreated.

* **Highly available [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/):** about 5-6 mg per 100 grams, in a form that is absorbed better than plant zinc (which phytate inhibits). Zinc is a cofactor for about 300 enzymes and is essential for the immune system and DNA synthesis.

* **High-quality complete protein:** a PDCAAS score close to 1.0, rich in leucine, and highly digestible.

* **Creatine and carnosine:** found almost exclusively in meat — even printed alternatives such as [Redefine Meat flank](/en/food/redefine-meat-flank/) or [tempeh](/en/food/tempeh/) do not provide them. [Creatine](/en/food/supplements/creatine/) supports the phosphagen energy system, and carnosine acts as an acid buffer in muscle. Vegetarians show lower concentrations of both.

* **[Selenium](/en/food/supplements/selenium/), phosphorus and other B vitamins:** niacin, B6 and riboflavin in significant amounts.

### Downsides and risks

* **High-heat cooking byproducts — HCAs and PAHs:** when meat is exposed to high temperatures, especially on a charcoal grill or with direct searing, **heterocyclic amines (HCAs)** form from a reaction between amino acids and creatine, and **polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)** form from the smoke of dripping fat. Both are classified as possible carcinogens. **This is a factor you can control**: marinating the meat in an acidic marinade with herbs, cooking at a lower temperature, avoiding charring and reducing direct exposure time over the coals significantly reduce their formation.

* **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) and nitrites in processed meat:** processed products contain nitrites for preservation, which with heat and in the presence of amines can form nitrosamines. This is the main suspected mechanism behind the Group 1 classification.

* **Saturated fat:** fatty cuts contain significant amounts of saturated fat, which is linked to a rise in LDL. The difference between cuts is large — round or tenderloin contain about 9 grams of fat per 100 grams, ribeye or ribs more than double that.

* **TMAO:** gut bacteria convert carnitine and choline from meat into TMA, which the liver converts into TMAO — a metabolite linked in studies to atherosclerosis. Interestingly, the conversion depends on the makeup of the microbiome: in vegetarians who eat meat rarely, production is significantly lower. The evidence on causation is still developing.

* **Purines and gout:** red meat is rich in purines, which break down into uric acid. People with gout or prone to uric acid kidney stones are advised to limit it.

* **Environmental impact:** cattle farming is one of the significant contributors to greenhouse gas emissions (mainly methane), water use and land use. This is a growing consideration in dietary decisions.

### Practical use

**Amount:** up to about 350-500 grams cooked per week, according to the recommendations of leading health bodies. That translates into 2-3 servings a week.

**Choosing a cut:** round, tenderloin, sirloin and ossobuco are significantly leaner than ribeye, ribs and asado (short ribs). Trimming visible fat before cooking noticeably reduces saturated fat.

**Cooking method — the factor in your control:**
* Prefer moist cooking (stew, broth), baking or roasting at moderate heat over direct grilling on coals.
* A marinade with [garlic](/en/food/garlic/), [lemon](/en/food/lemon/), rosemary and turmeric has been shown to significantly reduce HCA formation.
* Avoid charring and remove burnt parts.
* Flip the meat often on the grill — this reduces HCA formation.

**Cut down on processed:** the most important distinction. A fresh cut of beef and a [sausage](/en/food/sausages/) are not in the same health category.

**Best pairing:** with vegetables rich in fiber and antioxidants. Fiber binds some of the byproducts and shortens transit time through the colon.

### Summary

Fresh red meat provides heme iron, B12, zinc, creatine and complete protein with a bioavailability that is hard to get from other sources — so it has real value, especially for women of reproductive age and athletes. The risks are real too, but they divide clearly: **processed meat is classified as carcinogenic (Group 1)** and should be cut down as much as possible, while fresh meat is classified only as probably carcinogenic (2A) and is best limited to about 350-500 grams per week. Two levers in the consumer's control make a big difference: choosing a lean cut, and cooking at moderate heat instead of on a charring grill.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is red meat, and do chicken and turkey count as red meat?**
Answer: Red meat is the meat of mammals: beef, veal, lamb and also pork. Its color comes from the high myoglobin content of the muscle. Chicken, turkey and fish are not red meat, even when parts of them (such as chicken thighs) are darker.

**Question: Does red meat cause cancer?**
Answer: A distinction is needed. **Processed meat** (sausages, salami, bacon) is classified in Group 1 — carcinogenic, mainly for colorectal cancer. **Fresh red meat** is classified in Group 2A — probably carcinogenic, based on limited evidence. The classification describes the strength of the evidence, not the size of the risk.

**Question: How much red meat is OK per week?**
Answer: Leading health bodies recommend up to 350-500 grams cooked per week of fresh red meat — about 2-3 servings — and cutting down on processed meat as much as possible.

**Question: What is the big advantage of red meat?**
Answer: Heme [iron](/en/food/iron/), which is absorbed at 15%-35% compared with 2%-20% for plant iron, and is barely affected by absorption inhibitors. Plus B12, highly available zinc, creatine and carnosine — nutrients that are hard to get from plant sources.

**Question: How can you reduce the risk when cooking?**
Answer: Avoid charring and prolonged direct grilling over coals, which create HCAs and PAHs. An acidic marinade with herbs, cooking at moderate heat, frequent flipping and removing burnt parts significantly reduce their formation.

**Question: Which beef cut is the leanest?**
Answer: Round, tenderloin, sirloin and ossobuco. They contain about 9 grams of fat per 100 grams, compared with more than double that in ribeye, ribs and asado.

**Question: What is TMAO?**
Answer: A metabolite formed when gut bacteria convert carnitine and choline from meat, and the liver processes the product. It has been linked in studies to atherosclerosis, although the evidence for causation is still developing. Production depends on the makeup of the microbiome and is lower in people who eat meat rarely.
