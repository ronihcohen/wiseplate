## Oysters (*Ostreidae*)

In Hebrew oysters are called "צדפות" or simply "אויסטרים". They hold a record that is hard to challenge: **they are the most powerful dietary source of [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/) in the world**. 100 grams of Pacific oysters provides about 16.6 mg of zinc — more than 150% of the daily value, and five times as much as [beef](/en/food/red-meat/). In some oyster species, such as the Eastern oyster, the values are even much higher than that.

Alongside zinc, they provide [vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/) in amounts several times the daily value, [selenium](/en/food/supplements/selenium/), [copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/) and [iron](/en/food/iron/) — all for 81 calories per 100 grams.

And alongside all this — **oysters are also one of the foods involved in the largest number of poisoning cases**, which is why half of this page deals with safety.

**A kashrut note:** the oyster is not kosher, since it has neither fins nor scales. A relevant consideration for some readers.

### Why zinc in particular

The oyster is a **living filter**: it passes dozens of liters of seawater through its body every day and filters micro-algae out of it. In the process it concentrates metals and minerals that are present in the water at tiny concentrations — first and foremost zinc, which it needs to build its enzyme systems.

This is the reason for both its strength and its weakness: **the same concentration mechanism also applies to what you don't want** — viruses, bacteria and heavy metals.

**The zinc in oysters is especially important for former vegetarians, people recovering from surgery or a wound, and men** — zinc is needed for testosterone production, immune function, tissue healing, the senses of taste and smell, and DNA synthesis. And yes, the oyster's reputation as an aphrodisiac food also rests on that same zinc — although the link between zinc supplementation and sexual function has been proven only in states of **deficiency**, not in a population with normal levels.

### Safety — the important chapter

Oysters are traditionally eaten **raw and alive**, and that is where most of the risk lies:

* **Vibrio vulnificus and Vibrio parahaemolyticus.** Bacteria that occur naturally in warm, brackish water and multiply in summer. They are not related to pollution or poor sanitation, and cannot be detected by taste, smell or appearance. In a healthy person they usually cause gastroenteritis; in people at risk they can cause **fatal blood infection**. The mortality rate from invasive Vibrio vulnificus infection is especially high.
* **Norovirus.** The oyster concentrates it from the water; it is the most common cause of foodborne outbreaks from oysters.
* **The "R months" rule** — eat oysters only in months whose English name contains an R (September through April) — is not a superstition. It accurately reflects the seasonality of Vibrio in warm water. Modern refrigeration and oversight have reduced the risk but not eliminated it.

**Who must avoid raw oysters completely:**
* People with liver disease — cirrhosis, chronic hepatitis, advanced fatty liver. This is the most serious risk group.
* Immunocompromised people, cancer patients in treatment, transplant recipients.
* People with **iron overload** (hemochromatosis) — Vibrio multiplies especially well in the presence of free iron.
* People with diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or those taking drugs that suppress stomach acid.
* Pregnant women and young children.

**Cooking to an internal temperature of 63°C (145°F) kills Vibrio and most pathogens.** Lemon juice, Tabasco or alcohol — do not.

### Benefits

* **The world's most powerful source of zinc** — as detailed above.
* **[Vitamin B12](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b12/):** 667% of the daily value per 100 grams — essential for nerve function, DNA synthesis and the formation of red blood cells.
* **[Selenium](/en/food/supplements/selenium/) and [copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/)** in very high amounts.
* **Heme [iron](/en/food/iron/):** 5.1 mg per 100 grams in a form that is absorbed much better than plant iron.
* **High-quality protein in few calories:** 9.5 grams of protein for 81 calories.
* **[Omega-3](/en/food/supplements/omega-3/):** about 0.7 grams of EPA and DHA per 100 grams — less than [salmon](/en/food/salmon/) or [sardines](/en/food/sardines/), but a real contribution.
* **[Vitamin D](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-d/):** 40% of the daily value — rare in food.
* **A low environmental footprint:** farmed oysters filter water and require no feeding, and are considered among the animal proteins with the lowest environmental impact.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Vibrio and norovirus when eaten raw** — as detailed above, the most significant downside.
* **Heavy metals.** The same filtering mechanism also concentrates cadmium and, to a lesser extent, lead. Regulated sources and countries with monitoring reduce the risk, but it still justifies moderate rather than daily consumption.
* **Excess zinc.** High, chronic zinc intake (above 40 mg per day from all sources) interferes with [copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/) absorption and can cause copper deficiency. **A 100-gram serving of oysters exceeds the daily value several times over**, so anyone who eats oysters frequently **does not also need a zinc supplement**.
* **Shellfish allergy.** A common allergy, sometimes severe, and usually lifelong. An allergy to mollusks is not the same as an allergy to crustaceans (shrimp, crabs) — you can be allergic to one and not the other.
* **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/).** An oyster is served in its own seawater; its sodium content is significant.
* **Price and availability.** An expensive product, available in Israel mainly in restaurants and specialty stores.
* **Kashrut** — as noted above.

### Practical use

* **A live oyster = a closed shell.** If it is open, tap it lightly; it should close. An oyster that does not close is not alive and should be discarded. After cooking the rule is reversed: **an oyster that did not open during cooking** should be discarded.
* **Store refrigerated, flat side down**, covered with a damp towel, not in a sealed bag and not in fresh water — the oyster is alive and needs air.
* **Eat within two days** of purchase, and make sure of a regulated source with documented origin.
* **Cook them for anyone in a risk group.** Steam until they open plus another 3-5 minutes, or grill — 63°C (145°F) inside.
* **Making the most of the [iron](/en/food/iron/):** oysters with [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) are not just tradition — the [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) improves iron absorption.
* **Don't take them together with a [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/) supplement** — the supplement is unnecessary and may be harmful.
* **A reasonable serving:** 6-12 oysters (85-170 grams), and not every day.
* **Avoid eating them raw in summer** — following the logic of the "R months."

### Summary

Oysters are one of the highest nutrient densities in any food table: 151% zinc, 667% vitamin B12, 176% copper and 140% selenium — for 81 calories per 100 grams. No other food comes close. But the same filtering mechanism that gives them their value also concentrates viruses, Vibrio bacteria and heavy metals. **For a healthy person, oysters from a regulated source in the right season are a small, calculated risk. For people with liver disease, the immunocompromised, pregnant women and people with iron overload — a raw oyster is a real risk, and the solution is simple: cook it.**

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What are the nutrition facts of oysters?**
Answer: 100 grams of raw Pacific oysters provides 81 calories, 9.5 grams of protein, 16.6 mg of zinc (151% of the daily value), 16 mcg of vitamin B12 (667%), 1.6 mg of copper (176%), 77 mcg of selenium (140%) and 5.1 mg of iron. Six medium oysters cover the daily value of four minerals for 70 calories.

**Question: Are oysters really the best source of zinc?**
Answer: Yes. 16.6 mg per 100 grams in the Pacific species — five times as much as beef, and in other species much more. The oyster filters dozens of liters of seawater a day and concentrates zinc from it. Anyone who eats oysters frequently does not also need a zinc supplement — a chronic excess interferes with copper absorption.

**Question: Is it dangerous to eat raw oysters?**
Answer: For a healthy person, from a regulated source and in the cold season — the risk is small but not zero. The main dangers are Vibrio bacteria, which multiply in warm water and cannot be detected by taste or smell, and norovirus. For people with liver disease, the immunocompromised, people with iron overload, pregnant women and children — a raw oyster is a real risk, and cooking is the solution.

**Question: What is the "R months" rule?**
Answer: The tradition of eating oysters only in months whose English name contains the letter R — September through April. It is not a superstition: it reflects the seasonality of Vibrio bacteria, which multiply in the warm water of summer. Modern refrigeration and oversight have reduced the risk but not eliminated it, and the rule still makes sense.

**Question: How do you know if an oyster is fresh?**
Answer: Before cooking — the shell should be closed. If it is open, tap it lightly: a live oyster closes. An oyster that does not close is not alive and should be discarded. After cooking the rule is reversed — an oyster that did not open during cooking is the one to discard.

**Question: Are oysters an aphrodisiac?**
Answer: The reputation rests on their zinc content, which is needed for testosterone production. The link between zinc and sexual function is proven, but only in states of **deficiency** — in a person with normal zinc levels there is no evidence that extra zinc improves anything. In other words, if you are low in zinc it will help; for everyone else it is mainly a good meal.

**Question: How do you store oysters?**
Answer: Refrigerated, flat side down, covered with a damp towel. Not in a sealed bag, not in a closed container and not in fresh water — the oyster is alive and needs oxygen, and fresh water kills it. Eat them within two days of purchase.

**Question: Do oysters contain heavy metals?**
Answer: Yes, to some extent. The same filtering mechanism that concentrates zinc also concentrates cadmium and, to a lesser extent, lead, depending on water quality. Regulated sources in countries with monitoring significantly reduce the risk, but it is a good reason to eat oysters as an occasional serving of 6-12 rather than as an everyday food.
