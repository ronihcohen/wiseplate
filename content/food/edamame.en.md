## Edamame

**The name edamame comes from the Japanese *枝豆* — "beans on the branch"** (in Hebrew it is written "אדממה"). These are **young soybeans**, picked while still green and unripe, unlike the mature, dry soybeans used to make [tofu](/en/food/tofu/), [soy milk](/en/food/soy-milk/) and oil.

This is a fundamental difference, not just a stage of ripeness: **the young bean is sweeter and softer, and its nutritional profile is different** — less fat and more water than the mature bean, and an exceptional amount of [folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/).

Edamame has a unique place on the plate: **it is almost the only case of a "vegetable" that is also a complete, full-fledged protein source.** Culinarily it is seen as an appetizer or side dish, while nutritionally it functions as a complete legume.

### Folate — the standout figure

**311 mcg per 100 g, that is, 78% of the daily intake.** This is one of the densest plant sources of [folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/) found in a whole food, and it exceeds most leafy greens.

Why it matters: folate is needed for DNA synthesis and cell division, so the requirement rises in any state of rapid growth. Deficiency in the early stages of pregnancy is linked to neural tube defects — which is why the official recommendation is supplementation for women of childbearing age.

**A caveat worth stating explicitly:** edamame is an excellent dietary addition, but it is **not a substitute for a folic acid supplement in pregnancy**. The medical recommendation to supplement (400-800 mcg a day) stands on its own, because it relies on regular, precise intake at an early stage — sometimes before a woman knows she is pregnant.

### Complete protein — what makes it exceptional

11.9 g of protein per 100 g, in just 121 calories. But **the quantity is not the interesting figure — the quality is.**

Soy protein is one of the few plant proteins that provides **all nine essential amino acids** in sufficient amounts, with a PDCAAS score of about 0.9-1.0 — comparable to [egg](/en/food/eggs/) and [milk](/en/food/milk/) protein. Most other legumes ([lentils](/en/food/lentil/), [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/), [beans](/en/food/black-beans/)) are low in methionine and need to be complemented by grains.

Compared with other soy products, edamame has a unique advantage: **the bean is eaten whole**, so it retains **5.2 g of fiber** per 100 g — an amount that [tofu](/en/food/tofu/) (0.3-1 g) and [soy milk](/en/food/soy-milk/) barely provide, because the fiber is filtered out during their production.

### Why you should never eat edamame uncooked

This is a safety point that is almost absent from the discussion, and it matters.

**Raw soybeans contain trypsin inhibitors and lectins** — proteins that protect the plant, which in humans interfere with digestive enzyme activity and can cause nausea, stomach pain and vomiting. They also impair the digestibility of the protein itself.

**Heat neutralizes them.** Steaming or boiling for 5 minutes is entirely sufficient.

In practice, **commercial frozen edamame is blanched before freezing**, so it is already safe at a basic level — but the recommendation is still to cook it. Do not eat completely fresh soybeans without cooking.

This is also the answer to the common question "can you eat edamame straight from the bag?" — better not, even if it has thawed.

### Other benefits

* **Exceptional nutrient density per calorie.** 121 calories that provide 12 g of protein, 5 g of fiber and 78% of daily folate. A rare ratio.

* **Double satiety.** Protein and fiber together are the most filling combination. In addition, **eating edamame from the pod slows you down** — each bean is squeezed out separately — and that is a real behavioral advantage in an appetizer.

* **[Manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/) and [copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/):** 44% and 38% of the daily intake per 100 g.

* **[Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/):** 22% — needed for blood clotting and bone mineralization.

* **Non-heme [iron](/en/food/iron/):** 2.3 mg per 100 g. Pairing it with a source of [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) improves absorption.

* **A very low glycemic index:** relatively low carbohydrate content alongside protein and fiber.

* **A minimally processed food:** at best, the ingredient list is "soybeans." Unlike industrial "meat substitutes."

* **Soy and hormones:** as with other soy products — meta-analyses of controlled trials **found no effect on testosterone or estrogen in men** at dietary amounts. Isoflavones bind mainly to the beta estrogen receptor, with a potency orders of magnitude lower than human estrogen. In the context of breast cancer, cohort studies lean toward a protective or neutral effect.

### Downsides and considerations

* **[Sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) — the main practical downside.** Edamame itself contains 6 mg of sodium per 100 g — that is, nothing. But it is almost always served **generously sprinkled with coarse salt on the pods**, and in restaurants also with soy sauce or ready-made seasoning.

  A serving at a Japanese restaurant may provide **500-900 mg of sodium** — up to about half the daily allowance — before the rest of the meal. And that is before the soy sauce on the side, which adds about 900 mg per tablespoon.

  The important point: **the salt sits on the pod, not in the bean.** If you squeeze the bean into your mouth without sucking the pod, you consume much less of it. This is a practical distinction that changes the number significantly.

* **The pod is not edible.** It is tough, fibrous and indigestible. Squeeze the beans out and discard it. A common question for people encountering edamame for the first time.

* **Soy allergy:** one of the leading allergens in children, and requires complete avoidance.

* **FODMAPs — galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS).** Edamame contains a moderate amount of them, and they can cause gas and bloating in people with irritable bowel syndrome. A small serving (about 90 g of beans) is considered relatively well tolerated on a low-FODMAP protocol.

* **Trypsin inhibitors and lectins in the raw bean:** as detailed above — they require cooking.

* **Phytate:** reduces absorption of [iron](/en/food/iron/) and [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/). Cooking reduces some of it, and pairing with [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) compensates.

* **Thyroid and medication:** separate taking thyroxine from eating soy products by at least 4 hours. With normal thyroid function and sufficient [iodine](/en/food/supplements/iodine/) intake there is no effect.

* **Purines:** moderate content. Relevant for people with gout.

* **Flavored edamame products:** ready-made "flavored" versions — chili, garlic, teriyaki — usually add significant amounts of sodium, sugar and oil.

### Practical use

* **Steam for 5 minutes** — the best method. Boiling works too, but washes out some of the water-soluble nutrients.
* **Frozen beats "fresh" from the shelf.** Edamame is picked and frozen soon after harvest, and its sugars are preserved. A young bean loses sweetness quickly at room temperature.
* **Season after cooking, yourself** — a pinch of salt, sesame, [garlic](/en/food/garlic/), chili or lemon zest. That way you control the sodium.
* **A typical serving:** 100-150 g of beans — 12-18 g of protein and 5-8 g of fiber.
* **Squeeze out the bean rather than sucking the pod** — if you want the taste without most of the salt.
* **Shelled beans for cooking:** excellent in salads, rice, pasta, miso soup or stir-fries. You can also mash them into a hummus-like spread.
* **With [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/)** — [lemon](/en/food/lemon/), [tomato](/en/food/tomato/) or [red pepper](/en/food/red-pepper/) — to improve iron absorption.
* **Don't eat it uncooked**, not even thawed from the bag.
* **As a protein snack:** one of the best options — dense in protein, low in calories and filling, and unlike most snacks it also slows down your eating pace.

### Summary

Edamame is **young soybeans**, and a rare case of a food seen as a vegetable that functions as a complete legume: 11.9 g of **complete protein** — with all nine essential amino acids, at a PDCAAS score comparable to egg — in just 121 calories, alongside 5.2 g of fiber that is retained because the bean is eaten whole.

The truly standout figure is **[folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/): 78% of the daily intake per 100 g** — one of the densest sources in a whole food, though not a substitute for a supplement in pregnancy.

Two practical points: **the sodium is not in the bean but in the salt on the pod** — a restaurant serving reaches 500-900 mg, and squeezing out the bean instead of sucking the pod cuts that dramatically. And **always cook it** — raw soybeans contain trypsin inhibitors and lectins that heat neutralizes, and five minutes of steaming is enough.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is edamame?**
Answer: Young soybeans picked while still green and unripe, unlike the dry soybeans used to make tofu and oil. The name is Japanese — "beans on the branch." The young bean is sweeter and softer, and contains less fat and more folate than the mature bean.

**Question: How much protein is in edamame?**
Answer: 11.9 g per 100 g of cooked beans, in just 121 calories. More importantly — it is a complete protein with all nine essential amino acids, with a PDCAAS score of about 0.9-1.0, comparable to egg and milk. Most other legumes are low in methionine and need to be complemented by grains.

**Question: What is the nutritional value of edamame?**
Answer: 100 g of cooked beans provides 121 calories, 11.9 g of protein, 5.2 g of fiber and 8.9 g of carbohydrates, as well as 311 mcg of folate (78% of the daily intake), 1.0 mg of manganese, 2.3 mg of iron and 26.7 mcg of vitamin K.

**Question: Can you eat edamame raw?**
Answer: Better not. Raw soybeans contain trypsin inhibitors and lectins that interfere with digestion and can cause nausea and stomach pain. Heat neutralizes them — five minutes of steaming is enough. Commercial frozen edamame is blanched before freezing and is therefore safe at a basic level, but cooking is still recommended.

**Question: Do you eat the edamame pod?**
Answer: No. The pod is tough, fibrous and indigestible. Squeeze the beans into your mouth and discard it. Note that the salt actually sits on the pod — if you squeeze without sucking it, you consume much less sodium.

**Question: How much sodium is in edamame?**
Answer: In the bean itself — 6 mg per 100 g, that is, nothing. But a restaurant serving sprinkled with coarse salt may provide 500-900 mg — up to about half the daily allowance. The solution: buy it frozen and season at home, or squeeze out the bean without sucking the pod.

**Question: Is edamame healthier than tofu?**
Answer: They complement each other. Edamame wins in folate (78% of the daily intake), fiber (5.2 vs. 0.3-1 g) and low calories. Tofu wins in calcium — if it was set with calcium sulfate — and in culinary flexibility. Tempeh wins in protein. Combining them beats choosing one.

**Question: Is edamame good for weight loss?**
Answer: Yes, it is one of the most suitable foods for it. 121 calories that provide 12 g of protein and 5 g of fiber — the most filling combination. It also has a behavioral advantage: eating from the pod slows down your eating pace, since each bean is squeezed out separately.

**Question: Does the soy in edamame affect hormones?**
Answer: No, according to the evidence. Meta-analyses of dozens of controlled trials found no effect on testosterone, estrogen or sperm quality at dietary amounts. Isoflavones bind mainly to the beta estrogen receptor with a potency orders of magnitude lower than human estrogen.

**Question: Is edamame good during pregnancy?**
Answer: It is an excellent addition thanks to its high folate content — 78% of the daily intake per 100 g — which is needed for cell division and normal development. However, it is **not a substitute for a folic acid supplement**, for which the medical recommendation (400-800 mcg a day) stands on its own. Also make sure it is well cooked.

**Question: Frozen or fresh edamame?**
Answer: Frozen, usually. It is picked and frozen soon after harvest, which preserves its sweetness and nutritional value. A young soybean loses sweetness quickly at room temperature, so "fresh" edamame that has sat on the shelf is usually inferior to frozen.
