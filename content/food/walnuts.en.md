## Walnuts

The walnut (*Juglans regia*; in Hebrew "אגוזי מלך") is unusual among nuts. While most nuts are rich in monounsaturated fat, the walnut is the only one in which most of the fat is **polyunsaturated**, and it is the only nut that is a significant source of alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) — plant omega-3.

100 grams of walnuts provide about 654 calories, 65 grams of fat, 15 grams of protein and 6.7 grams of fiber. Of the fat, about 9 grams are ALA — an amount unmatched by other nuts. A handful (about 30 grams, 7-9 walnut halves) provides about 2.5 grams of ALA, more than the recommended daily intake.

### Understanding the omega-3 in walnuts — and its limitation

This is a point worth getting exactly right, because it is a common source of confusion.

Omega-3 fatty acids come in three kinds: **ALA** (plant), and **EPA** and **DHA** (marine). Most of the proven health benefits — especially for brain and retinal health — are attributed to EPA and DHA. The body can convert ALA to EPA and DHA, but **the conversion efficiency is very low**: about 5-8% to EPA and only about 0.5-4% to DHA. In men the efficiency is even lower than in women.

The practical meaning is twofold:

1. Walnuts **are not a substitute** for [oily fish](/en/food/salmon/) or an [omega-3](/en/food/supplements/omega-3/) supplement for anyone looking for EPA and DHA.
2. Still, ALA has value of its own. Studies link ALA intake to reduced cardiovascular risk in a way that does not depend on conversion — in other words, it is not just "raw material" but has independent activity.

Another ratio worth knowing: the omega-6 to omega-3 ratio in walnuts is about 4:1 — significantly lower than the ratio in the average Western diet (10:1 to 20:1), so eating them improves the overall ratio in your diet.

### Research-based health benefits

* **The PREDIMED study:** in the large randomized trial testing a Mediterranean diet, one of the two intervention arms received an extra 30 grams of nuts a day (half of them walnuts). This group showed a drop of about 28% in major cardiovascular events compared with the control group. The study was republished in 2018 after correcting flaws in randomization at some centers, and the results stood. This is one of the strongest bases for recommending nut consumption.

* **Blood lipid profile:** meta-analyses of controlled trials show that eating walnuts significantly lowers total and LDL cholesterol, with no negative effect on HDL or body weight.

* **[Polyphenols](/en/food/polyphenols/) — the highest content among nuts:** walnuts contain ellagitannins at high concentration. Gut bacteria convert them into urolithins — metabolites with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity. Interestingly, this production capacity varies between people depending on their microbiome composition, which may explain variation in response. Worth knowing: the polyphenols are concentrated in the **brown skin** that wraps the nut — the bitter skin many people remove.

* **Endothelial function:** several controlled trials showed an improvement in FMD — a measure of blood vessel wall function — after eating walnuts.

* **Brain and cognition:** observational studies link walnut consumption to better cognitive performance in adults, and the WAHA trial (Walnuts and Healthy Aging) showed mixed results — no significant improvement in the general population, but a hint of benefit in at-risk subgroups. The evidence here is weaker than for heart health, and the link should not be presented as established.

* **Satiety and weight:** despite the high calorie density, long-term studies do not find that eating nuts causes weight gain. The combined explanation: high satiety, the energy spent on digestion, and incomplete absorption — some of the fat is trapped in the plant cell wall matrix and excreted in the stool, so the calories actually absorbed are about 20% lower than those on the label.

* **[Magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) and minerals:** a good source of magnesium, [copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/), [manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/) and [phosphorus](/en/food/supplements/phosphorus/).

### Downsides and health considerations

* **Calorie density:** 654 calories per 100 grams. Although research does not show weight gain with controlled intake, eating "out of the bag" in front of the TV is an efficient way to take in 400 calories without noticing. The amount studied is a handful a day.

* **Oxidation and rancidity:** this is the downside unique to walnuts. Their high polyunsaturated fat content — their nutritional advantage — makes them very sensitive to oxidation. An oxidized walnut takes on a bitter, acrid taste ("tastes like paint"), and the fat in it is no longer beneficial. **Store them in the fridge or freezer**, not in a cupboard at room temperature. Walnuts in the shell keep better than shelled pieces.

* **Aflatoxins:** nuts stored in humid conditions may develop mold that produces aflatoxin — a known liver toxin and carcinogen. Avoid nuts that look or smell suspicious and buy from a source with fast stock turnover.

* **Tree nut allergy:** walnut is one of the common and severe allergens, and it can cause an anaphylactic reaction. This allergy usually does not go away with age.

* **Phytic acid:** as with other nuts and seeds, phytate may reduce the absorption of [iron](/en/food/iron/) and [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/). Soaking reduces it to some extent.

* **The bitter skin:** many people remove it because of its taste. This is a nutritional mistake — about 90% of the nut's polyphenols are concentrated in it.

### Practical use

**How much:** a handful a day — about 30 grams, 7-9 walnut halves. This is the amount tested in studies, and it provides about 2.5 grams of ALA.

**How to store:** in the fridge in an airtight container for up to about six months, or in the freezer for up to a year. Storing at room temperature dramatically shortens shelf life and oxidizes the fat. A walnut that tastes bitter or acrid — throw it out.

**Roasting:** light roasting improves the flavor but speeds up oxidation and partly damages the heat-sensitive ALA. Raw, unroasted walnuts are nutritionally better.

**Don't remove the skin:** the bitterness is the taste of the polyphenols.

**Pairings:** excellent in salads, [oatmeal](/en/food/oatmeal/), yogurt and as part of a rich "pesto." For vegetarians and vegans, walnuts are a key ingredient for getting ALA, alongside [flax seeds](/en/food/flax-seeds/) and [chia seeds](/en/food/chia-seeds/).

### Summary

Walnuts are the nut with the strongest research base for heart health, thanks to a combination of ALA, polyphenols and a unique fat profile — and the results of the PREDIMED study give this support from a randomized trial, not just from observation. It is important to understand the limited conversion of ALA to EPA/DHA and not to see them as a substitute for fish. Two practical points make a big difference: store them in the fridge, and don't remove the bitter skin.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many walnuts a day?**
Answer: A handful — about 30 grams, 7-9 walnut halves. This is the amount tested in the PREDIMED study and other trials, and it provides about 2.5 grams of ALA.

**Question: Can walnuts replace fish as a source of omega-3?**
Answer: No. They provide plant ALA, which the body converts to EPA and DHA with very low efficiency (0.5-8%). For vegetarians and vegans they are an excellent source of ALA, but anyone looking for EPA/DHA needs oily fish or a supplement (including algae supplements for vegans).

**Question: Why should walnuts be stored in the fridge?**
Answer: Their high polyunsaturated fat content makes them very sensitive to oxidation. At room temperature they spoil within weeks and take on a bitter, acrid taste. In the fridge they keep for about six months, and in the freezer for about a year.

**Question: Should you remove the bitter brown skin?**
Answer: No. About 90% of the nut's polyphenols are concentrated precisely in this skin. The bitterness is their taste.

**Question: Do walnuts make you gain weight?**
Answer: Long-term studies do not find weight gain with eating a handful a day. They are very filling, and about 20% of the calories on the label are not actually absorbed because the fat is trapped in the plant cell structure. The problem starts with eating freely out of the bag.

**Question: What is the difference between a walnut and an almond?**
Answer: Walnuts are rich in plant omega-3 (ALA) and polyphenols; almonds are rich in monounsaturated fat, [vitamin E](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-e/) and [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/), and contain more protein and fiber. Both are excellent and complement each other.
