## Walnuts

**Walnuts are the only nut that supplies a meaningful amount of plant omega-3 (ALA): a 30 g handful provides about 195 calories and about 2.7 g of ALA, more than the recommended daily intake. In a meta-analysis of 26 controlled trials they lowered LDL cholesterol by about 5.5 mg/dL without causing weight gain.** They are not a substitute for the EPA and DHA in fish.

The walnut (*Juglans regia*; in Hebrew "אגוזי מלך") is unusual among nuts. Most nuts are rich in monounsaturated fat, but in walnuts most of the fat is **polyunsaturated**, and they are the only nut in which ALA reaches several grams per handful. The adequate intake of ALA is 1.6 g a day for men and 1.1 g for women. [NIH ODS: Omega-3](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/).

### Understanding the omega-3 in walnuts — and its limitation

This is a point worth getting exactly right, because it is a common source of confusion.

Omega-3 fatty acids come in three kinds: **ALA** (plant), and **EPA** and **DHA** (marine). Most of the proven health benefits — especially for brain and retinal health — are attributed to EPA and DHA. The body can convert ALA to EPA and DHA, but **the conversion is limited**: only a small share of ALA becomes EPA, and an even smaller share becomes DHA. [NIH ODS: Omega-3](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/).

The practical meaning is twofold:

1. Walnuts **are not a substitute** for [oily fish](/en/food/salmon/) or an [omega-3](/en/food/supplements/omega-3/) supplement for anyone looking for EPA and DHA.
2. Still, ALA may have value of its own: in observational studies, higher ALA intake is associated with lower cardiovascular risk, although observational studies do not prove cause and effect.

Another ratio worth knowing: the omega-6 to omega-3 ratio in walnuts is about 4:1 — significantly lower than the ratio in the average Western diet (10:1 to 20:1), so eating them improves the overall ratio in your diet.

### Benefits

* **The PREDIMED study:** in the large randomized trial testing a Mediterranean diet, one of the two intervention arms received an extra 30 grams of nuts a day (half of them walnuts). This group showed a drop of about 28% in major cardiovascular events compared with a control group advised to follow a low-fat diet. The study was republished in 2018 after correcting flaws in randomization at some centers, and the results were similar. [Estruch et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29897866/). The effect is that of a whole diet with nuts, not of walnuts alone.

* **Blood lipid profile:** a meta-analysis of 26 controlled trials (1,059 participants) found that walnut-enriched diets lowered total cholesterol by about 7 mg/dL, LDL by about 5.5 mg/dL and triglycerides too, with no adverse effect on body weight or blood pressure. [Guasch-Ferré et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29931130/).

* **[Polyphenols](/en/food/polyphenols/) — the highest content among nuts:** walnuts contain ellagitannins at high concentration. Gut bacteria convert them into urolithins — metabolites with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity. Interestingly, this production capacity varies between people depending on their microbiome composition, which may explain variation in response. Worth knowing: most of the polyphenols are in the **brown skin** that wraps the nut — the bitter skin many people remove.

* **Endothelial function:** several small controlled trials showed an improvement in FMD — a measure of blood vessel wall function — after eating walnuts. This is an intermediate finding, not proof of fewer heart attacks.

* **Brain and cognition:** observational studies link walnut consumption to better cognitive performance in adults, but in the WAHA trial (Walnuts and Healthy Aging), in which 708 adults aged 63–79 ate 30–60 g of walnuts a day for two years, there was no difference in cognitive function compared with controls. A secondary analysis hinted at benefit in one subgroup only. [Sala-Vila et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31912155/). The evidence here is weaker than for heart health, and the link should not be presented as established.

* **Satiety and weight:** despite the high calorie density, long-term studies do not find that eating nuts causes weight gain. The combined explanation: high satiety, the energy spent on digestion, and incomplete absorption — some of the fat is trapped in the plant cell wall matrix and excreted in the stool, so the calories actually absorbed are lower than those on the label. [Adv Nutr, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36934832/).

* **[Magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) and minerals:** a good source of magnesium, [copper](/en/food/supplements/copper/), [manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/) and [phosphorus](/en/food/supplements/phosphorus/).

### Downsides

* **Calorie density:** 654 calories per 100 grams. Although research does not show weight gain with controlled intake, eating "out of the bag" in front of the TV is an efficient way to take in 400 calories without noticing. The amount studied is a handful a day.

* **Oxidation and rancidity:** this is the downside unique to walnuts. Their high polyunsaturated fat content — their nutritional advantage — makes them very sensitive to oxidation. An oxidized walnut takes on a bitter, acrid taste ("tastes like paint"), and the fat in it is no longer beneficial. **Store them in the fridge or freezer**, not in a cupboard at room temperature. Walnuts in the shell keep better than shelled pieces.

* **Aflatoxins:** nuts stored in humid conditions may develop mold that produces aflatoxin — a known liver toxin and carcinogen. Avoid nuts that look or smell suspicious and buy from a source with fast stock turnover.

* **Tree nut allergy:** walnut is one of the common and severe allergens, and it can cause an anaphylactic reaction. This allergy usually does not go away with age.

* **Phytic acid:** as with other nuts and seeds, phytate may reduce the absorption of [iron](/en/food/iron/) and [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/). Soaking reduces it to some extent.

* **The bitter skin:** many people remove it because of its taste. This is a nutritional mistake — most of the nut's polyphenols are concentrated in it.

### Practical use

**How much:** a handful a day — about 30 grams, 7-9 walnut halves. This is the amount tested in studies, and it provides about 2.7 grams of ALA.

**How to store:** in the fridge in an airtight container for up to about six months, or in the freezer for up to a year. Storing at room temperature dramatically shortens shelf life and oxidizes the fat. A walnut that tastes bitter or acrid — throw it out.

**Roasting:** light roasting improves the flavor but speeds up oxidation and partly damages the heat-sensitive ALA. Raw, unroasted walnuts are nutritionally better.

**Don't remove the skin:** the bitterness is the taste of the polyphenols.

**See also:** [Brazil Nuts](/en/food/brazil-nuts/), [Almonds](/en/food/almonds/)

**Pairings:** excellent in salads, [oatmeal](/en/food/oatmeal/), yogurt and as part of a rich "pesto." For vegetarians and vegans, walnuts are a key ingredient for getting ALA, alongside [flax seeds](/en/food/flax-seeds/) and [chia seeds](/en/food/chia-seeds/).

### Summary

Walnuts are the nut with the strongest research base for heart health, thanks to a combination of ALA, polyphenols and a unique fat profile — and the PREDIMED study and a meta-analysis of cholesterol trials give this support from randomized trials, not just from observation. For memory and cognition, the large trial found no benefit. It is important to understand the limited conversion of ALA to EPA/DHA and not to see them as a substitute for fish. Two practical points make a big difference: store them in the fridge, and don't remove the bitter skin.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many walnuts a day?**
Answer: A handful — about 30 grams, 7-9 walnut halves. This is the amount tested in the PREDIMED study and other trials, and it provides about 2.7 grams of ALA.

**Question: Can walnuts replace fish as a source of omega-3?**
Answer: No. They provide plant ALA, which the body converts to EPA and DHA with low efficiency. For vegetarians and vegans they are an excellent source of ALA, but anyone looking for EPA/DHA needs oily fish or a supplement (including algae supplements for vegans).

**Question: Why should walnuts be stored in the fridge?**
Answer: Their high polyunsaturated fat content makes them very sensitive to oxidation. At room temperature they spoil within weeks and take on a bitter, acrid taste. In the fridge they keep for about six months, and in the freezer for about a year.

**Question: Should you remove the bitter brown skin?**
Answer: No. Most of the nut's polyphenols are concentrated precisely in this skin. The bitterness is their taste.

**Question: Do walnuts make you gain weight?**
Answer: Long-term studies do not find weight gain with eating a handful a day. They are very filling, and some of the calories on the label are not actually absorbed because the fat is trapped in the plant cell structure. The problem starts with eating freely out of the bag.

**Question: What is the difference between a walnut and an almond?**
Answer: Walnuts are rich in plant omega-3 (ALA) and polyphenols; almonds are rich in monounsaturated fat, [vitamin E](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-e/) and [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/), and contain more protein and fiber. Both are excellent and complement each other.
