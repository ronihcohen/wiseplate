## Pumpkin (*Cucurbita* spp.)

Pumpkin is one of the vegetables with the best ratio of nutritional value to calories: 26 calories per 100 grams, containing almost half the daily value of [vitamin A](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-a/) in the form of beta-carotene. The deep orange color is not decoration — it is the nutrient itself. In Hebrew it is called "דלעת" (dla'at).

But there is a detail that determines how much of it actually gets into the body: **carotenoids are poorly absorbed from raw vegetables and without fat.** Pumpkin roasted with [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) and raw pumpkin in a salad do not deliver the same amount of vitamin A in practice, even if the nutrition label is identical.

### Nutrition facts per 100 grams

| Nutrient | Fresh (raw) | Canned (pure puree) |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | 26 | 34 |
| Water | 92 g | 90 g |
| Protein | 1.0 g | 1.1 g |
| Carbohydrates | 6.5 g | 8.1 g |
| Dietary fiber | 0.5 g | **2.9 g** |
| Fat | 0.1 g | 0.3 g |
| **[Vitamin A](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-a/)** | **426 mcg RAE (47%)** | **778 mcg RAE (86%)** |
| Beta-carotene | 3,100 mcg | 6,900 mcg |
| [Vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) | 9 mg (10%) | 4 mg (4%) |
| [Potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/) | 340 mg (7%) | 206 mg (4%) |
| [Vitamin E](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-e/) | 1.06 mg (7%) | 1.06 mg (7%) |

**Note the canned column:** canned pumpkin puree (pure, with no additions) is more concentrated than the fresh vegetable — almost twice the beta-carotene and six times the fiber, because some of the water has been removed. This is one of the few cases where the canned product is nutritionally better than the fresh one.

### Beta-carotene — and why preparation matters

Beta-carotene is a **provitamin A**: the body converts it into retinol as needed, so there is no risk of toxicity from it as there is from vitamin A supplements. The problem runs the other way — how much of it is absorbed at all.

Carotenoids are trapped inside chromoplasts in the plant cell wall, and they are fat-soluble only. That leads to two practical conditions:

* **Heat breaks down the cell wall** and releases the carotenoid. Cooked or roasted pumpkin provides far more available beta-carotene than raw pumpkin.
* **Fat in the meal is needed for absorption.** Studies on carotenoids show that adding a fat source to a meal raises absorption dramatically — not by a few percent. A tablespoon of [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/), [tahini](/en/food/tahini/), [avocado](/en/food/avocado/) or seeds alongside the dish is enough.

The implication: **pumpkin soup with a tablespoon of olive oil or a little cream is worth more, nutritionally, than a fat-free raw pumpkin salad** — even when on paper both contain the same amount of beta-carotene.

### Benefits

* **A large amount of [vitamin A](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-a/) for 26 calories:** 47% of the daily value per 100 grams, and 86% in the canned version. Essential for vision in low light, for healthy epithelial tissue and for immune function.
* **Beta-carotene with no toxicity risk:** unlike retinol (vitamin A from animal sources or supplements), the body regulates the conversion, so there is no toxicity.
* **Very low energy density:** 92% water — filling volume for few calories.
* **[Potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/):** 340 mg per 100 grams — more than a [banana](/en/food/banana/) per 100 grams, at a third of the calories.
* **Lutein and zeaxanthin** alongside the beta-carotene — carotenoids that accumulate in the macula of the eye.
* **Fiber in the concentrated version:** 2.9 grams per 100 grams in canned puree.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Low in fiber in the fresh version.** Only 0.5 grams per 100 grams — pumpkin is mistakenly thought of as a high-fiber food, and it is not. Get your fiber from [legumes](/en/food/lentil/), [oats](/en/food/oatmeal/) or other vegetables.
* **Low in protein.** 1 gram per 100 grams. Pumpkin soup on its own is not a meal — it needs added protein.
* **"Pumpkin puree" versus "pumpkin pie filling".** Two products that look identical on the shelf: the first is pumpkin only; the second contains sugar, syrup and spices and reaches about 100 calories and 19 grams of sugar per 100 grams. This is the most common shopping mistake in the category.
* **Carotenodermia.** A very high, sustained intake of beta-carotene-rich foods can tint the palms and soles orange. It is completely safe and goes away on its own when you cut back, but it is worth knowing about so you don't panic.
* **High glycemic index, low glycemic load.** Pumpkin is sometimes presented as problematic for diabetics because of its high glycemic index — but because of its low carbohydrate content (6.5 grams per 100 grams), the glycemic load of a normal serving is low. Both numbers are real, and the second is the relevant one.
* **Pumpkin desserts.** Pies, cakes and sweets based on pumpkin are not a vegetable — the sugar and fat in them outweigh the contribution of the vegetable itself.

### Practical use

* **Roast, don't boil.** Roasting at 200°C (392°F) concentrates flavor, breaks down cell walls and releases carotenoids. Boiling dilutes the flavor and leaches some of the vitamin C into the water.
* **Always with a fat source.** A tablespoon of olive oil, tahini, toasted [pumpkin seeds](/en/food/pumpkin-seeds/) or a little [Greek yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/) — not as a flavor extra but as a condition for absorption.
* **No need to peel.** The skin of kabocha and young butternut squash is soft and edible after roasting, and adds fiber.
* **The seeds are a completely separate food.** [Pumpkin seeds](/en/food/pumpkin-seeds/) provide 559 calories, 30 grams of protein and plenty of [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/) and [magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) per 100 grams. Rinse, dry and roast them — don't throw them away.
* **When buying canned: read the ingredients.** "100% pumpkin" and nothing else.
* **Add protein:** [pumpkin soup](/en/recipes/soups/pumpkin-soup/) with [lentils](/en/food/lentil/), roasted [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/) or [Greek yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/) turns a side dish into a meal.

### Summary

Pumpkin provides almost half the daily value of vitamin A for just 26 calories — a ratio that is hard to beat. But two points determine whether that benefit actually arrives: **cooking and fat**, without which most of the beta-carotene simply passes through the body, and **reading the label** on canned products, where the gap between "pumpkin puree" and "pie filling" is 19 grams of sugar. And what it is not: a source of fiber or protein. For those you need to bring something else to the plate.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is the nutritional value of pumpkin?**
Answer: 100 grams of fresh pumpkin provide 26 calories, 1 gram of protein, 6.5 grams of carbohydrates, 0.5 grams of fiber and 426 mcg of vitamin A in the form of beta-carotene — 47% of the daily value. It also contains 340 mg of potassium, more than a banana per 100 grams, at a third of the calories.

**Question: Why should you eat pumpkin with fat?**
Answer: Because beta-carotene is fat-soluble and trapped inside the plant cell wall. Heat breaks down the wall and releases it, and fat in the meal is needed for it to be absorbed in the gut. Adding a tablespoon of olive oil, tahini or avocado raises absorption dramatically — which is why pumpkin soup with olive oil is nutritionally worth more than raw pumpkin without fat.

**Question: Is canned pumpkin less healthy than fresh?**
Answer: The opposite, if it is pure pumpkin puree. Removing some of the water concentrates the nutrients: 778 mcg of vitamin A (86% of the daily value) and 2.9 grams of fiber per 100 grams, compared with 426 mcg and 0.5 grams in fresh. It is important to distinguish between "pumpkin puree" and "pumpkin pie filling" — the latter contains sugar and reaches about 19 grams of sugar per 100 grams.

**Question: Is pumpkin suitable for people with diabetes?**
Answer: Yes, in normal servings. Its glycemic index is high, but its carbohydrate content is very low (6.5 grams per 100 grams), so the actual glycemic load of a serving is low. Both numbers are correct, and the glycemic load is the one relevant to the plate. It is best combined with protein and fat.

**Question: Why have my palms turned orange?**
Answer: This is carotenodermia — a buildup of beta-carotene in the outer layer of the skin, especially common on the palms and soles. It is caused by a high, sustained intake of pumpkin, carrots or sweet potatoes, is completely safe, and is unrelated to jaundice (in jaundice the whites of the eyes turn yellow too). It goes away on its own within weeks when you cut back.

**Question: Can you eat pumpkin skin?**
Answer: It depends on the variety. The skin of kabocha and young butternut squash softens when roasted and is completely edible, adding fiber. The skin of a large, mature pumpkin is too hard and better peeled. Either way, wash it well before roasting.

**Question: Is pumpkin high in fiber?**
Answer: In the fresh version — no. 0.5 grams per 100 grams is a very low amount, and its reputation as a high-fiber food is not justified. Canned pumpkin puree reaches 2.9 grams per 100 grams, which is a reasonable contribution. For real fiber, it is better to rely on legumes, oats or other vegetables.

**Question: What is the difference between pumpkin and pumpkin seeds?**
Answer: Two completely different foods. Pumpkin is a vegetable with 26 calories per 100 grams whose main value lies in beta-carotene; pumpkin seeds are oily seeds with 559 calories per 100 grams, rich in protein (30 grams), zinc and magnesium. The seeds do not count as a serving of vegetables and are no substitute for one.
