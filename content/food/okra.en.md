## Okra (*Abelmoschus esculentus*)

Okra (in Hebrew "במיה", bamia) is the vegetable more people say they dislike than any other — almost always because of **the slime**. That's a shame, because that very slime is the most interesting thing about it nutritionally, and there are simple, proven ways to get rid of it on the plate without giving up its benefits in the body.

Nutritionally, okra is a thoroughly dense vegetable: 33 calories per 100 grams, 3.2 grams of fiber and a good amount of minerals — especially [magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) and [manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/).

### The slime — what it actually is

The sticky substance released by okra is called **mucilage**, or "plant slime." It is not a defect or a sign of spoilage: it is a mixture of **water-soluble polysaccharides and glycoproteins** — in nutritional terms, **soluble dietary fiber**.

Soluble fibers of this kind have well-known roles:

* They form a viscous gel in the small intestine that slows stomach emptying and glucose absorption, and so moderates the rise in blood sugar after a meal.
* They bind bile acids in the gut and increase their excretion, a mechanism linked to lower LDL.
* They are fermented by bacteria in the large intestine and serve as a prebiotic substrate.

There is preliminary research on okra extract and glycemic regulation, including work on water in which okra has been soaked. **The evidence is early, mostly in animals and small human studies, and does not justify treating okra as a treatment.** As a vegetable rich in soluble fiber within a balanced diet — it is fully justified.

### How to get rid of the slime on the plate

The slime is released when okra cells are damaged and come into contact with water. That gives four methods that work, and they can be combined:

1. **Cook it whole.** A whole pod, without cutting the ends down to the flesh, releases almost no slime. That's why small, young okra is served whole in many cuisines.
2. **High, dry heat.** Roasting in the oven at 220°C (428°F) or quick frying in a hot pan dries out the polysaccharide before it has time to spread. This is the most effective method.
3. **Acid.** [Lemon](/en/food/lemon/), vinegar, tamarind or [tomatoes](/en/food/tomato/) in a stew break down some of the slime. Not by coincidence, almost every traditional cuisine that cooks okra — [Iraqi bamia](/en/recipes/iraq/bamia/), Egyptian bamia, gumbo — does so in a tomato or lemon sauce.
4. **Dry it before cooking.** Rinse early and let it dry completely, and don't cut a wet vegetable. Water on the surface speeds up the release.

**What doesn't work:** long cooking over low heat in lots of liquid. That is exactly the way to maximize the slime.

### Benefits

* **Exceptionally soluble fiber:** mucilage is a high-quality soluble fiber, and its contribution to satiety, glycemic regulation and blood lipid profile is established at the category level.
* **[Magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/):** 57 mg per 100 grams — among the best vegetable sources, for a nutrient many people get less of than recommended.
* **[Manganese](/en/food/supplements/manganese/):** 34% of the daily value.
* **[Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) and [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/):** 26% of the daily value each, for 33 calories.
* **[Folate](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-b9/):** 15% — significant during the reproductive years and pregnancy.
* **Polyphenols:** quercetin and its derivatives, and in red varieties also anthocyanins in the skin (which disappear with cooking as the pod turns green).
* **Very low in calories** with volume and a sense of fullness.

### Downsides and considerations

* **Oxalates.** Okra is considered a high-oxalate food. People who have had calcium-oxalate kidney stones should limit it and eat it with a source of [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/) in the same meal — that way the oxalate binds in the gut and not in the kidney. The rest of the population need not worry.
* **[Vitamin K](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-k/) and anticoagulants.** 26% of the daily value per 100 grams. People taking Coumadin don't need to avoid it — they need **steady, consistent** intake, so that the drug dose is calibrated to it.
* **Fructans and FODMAPs.** In large servings okra may trigger symptoms in sensitive people; a serving of about 75 grams is generally considered low-FODMAP.
* **Mature pods are fibrous and inedible.** Okra that has grown too large becomes woody. The rule: **up to 7-8 cm** — beyond that it is already tough.
* **The slime itself** — for some people it is an absolute deal-breaker. The solutions above work, but it's worth starting with the oven-roasted version rather than the stew.
* **Skin irritation from the fuzz.** The pods are covered with tiny hairs that can itch. Rinsing or gloves solve it.

### Practical use

* **Choose small.** Pods of 5-8 cm, bright green, that snap cleanly rather than bend. A pod that bends is old and fibrous.
* **Roast in the oven:** halve lengthwise, toss with [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/), salt and paprika, and roast at 220°C (428°F) for about 20 minutes until browned. This version is almost completely slime-free, and it changes the mind of most people who "don't like okra."
* **In a stew:** use whole pods, sear them first in a dry pan, and only then add them to a [tomato](/en/food/tomato/) sauce with [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) — as in [Iraqi bamia](/en/recipes/iraq/bamia/).
* **Rinse and dry well** before cutting, not after.
* **Frozen works great** in stews, and is sometimes even less slimy than fresh. Don't thaw — add it directly to the hot pot.
* **For people prone to kidney stones:** combine it with [yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/), cheese or [tahini](/en/food/tahini/) in the same meal, and drink water.

### Summary

Okra provides 3.2 grams of fiber, 57 mg of magnesium and 26% of the daily value of vitamin K and vitamin C — for 33 calories. The slime that repels so many people is **soluble dietary fiber**, and from a health standpoint it is the vegetable's strength, not its weakness. The practical recommendation: **high heat, acid, and small whole pods** — these three remove almost all the slime from the plate while keeping its benefit in the gut. People prone to kidney stones should keep the oxalates in mind.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What are the nutrition facts of okra?**
Answer: 100 grams of fresh okra provides 33 calories, 1.9 grams of protein, 3.2 grams of fiber, 57 mg of magnesium (14% of the daily value), 0.79 mg of manganese (34%), 31.3 mcg of vitamin K (26%) and 23 mg of vitamin C (26%). Its magnesium content is higher than that of most vegetables.

**Question: What is the slime in okra?**
Answer: Mucilage — a mixture of water-soluble polysaccharides and glycoproteins, which is essentially soluble dietary fiber. It forms a gel that slows glucose absorption, binds bile acids in the gut and serves as a prebiotic substrate for bacteria in the large intestine. In other words, from a health standpoint it is an advantage, not a defect.

**Question: How do you cook okra so it isn't slimy?**
Answer: Four methods that work and can be combined — cook the pods whole without cutting, use high, dry heat (roasting at 220 degrees (428°F) or quick frying), add acid such as lemon or tomatoes, and make sure the vegetable is completely dry before cutting. What increases the slime is exactly the opposite: long cooking over low heat in lots of liquid.

**Question: Does okra lower blood sugar?**
Answer: Its soluble fiber moderates the rise in blood sugar after a meal, like any food rich in soluble fiber. There are also preliminary studies on okra extract and on water in which okra has been soaked, but they are early — mostly in animals and small human studies — and do not justify treating okra as a treatment. As a vegetable within a balanced diet it is definitely beneficial.

**Question: Who should be careful with okra?**
Answer: Mainly people who have had calcium-oxalate kidney stones, since okra is high in oxalates. People taking anticoagulants such as Coumadin need steady, consistent intake because of its vitamin K content, not avoidance. People with irritable bowel syndrome may react to fructans in large servings; up to about 75 grams is considered a low-FODMAP serving.

**Question: How do you choose good okra?**
Answer: Small pods, 5-8 cm long, bright green, that snap cleanly. A pod that bends instead of snapping is old and fibrous, and okra that has grown beyond 8 cm becomes woody and almost inedible. Brown spots are a sign of overly long storage.

**Question: Is frozen okra as good as fresh?**
Answer: Yes, and in stews it is sometimes even more convenient — it is blanched and frozen soon after harvest, and sometimes releases less slime than fresh. It's important not to thaw it but to add it directly to the hot pot, otherwise it becomes too soft and slimy.

**Question: Can you eat okra raw?**
Answer: Yes. Young, small pods are edible raw, thinly sliced in a salad, and their texture is crisp, not slimy, when cut just before serving. This is also the way to preserve their full vitamin C content, which is sensitive to heat.
