## Msemen (*مسمّن*)

**In Moroccan Arabic: *مسمّن*, from a root meaning "greased."** The name describes the technique: the dough is spread with oil and semolina before it is folded, and that is what creates the layers. In Hebrew it appears as "מסמן," "מוסמן," "משמנה" or "רגייף" (rghaif), and most often simply as "Moroccan pan bread." Its closest relative in Israeli cooking is Yemeni [malawach](/en/food/malawach/).

Msemen is a square flatbread cooked in a dry pan rather than in the oven. It is eaten mainly at breakfast and at afternoon tea time, served with [honey](/en/food/honey/) and butter or with salty [cheese](/en/food/cheese/), and at street stalls in Morocco it is sold hot straight from the pan. It also has a round sibling — *baghrir*, the "thousand-hole bread" — which is a completely liquid batter cooked on one side only.

**Prep time:** 30 minutes · **Resting time:** 20 minutes · **Cook time:** 25 minutes · **Total:** about an hour and a half · **Yield:** 10 pieces · **Difficulty:** medium

### Lamination: what actually happens between the layers

Msemen, [malawach](/en/food/malawach/), Indian paratha and the French croissant all belong to one technical family, even if nothing about them looks alike: in all of them a layer of fat separates layers of dough, and in all of them heat is what turns that separation into texture.

The mechanism is simple. The fat layer blocks the dough from touching itself, preventing it from fusing into a single mass. As the pan heats up, the water trapped in each layer of dough turns to steam; the steam pushes the layers apart while the fat fries them from the inside at the same time. The result is a bread that is soft in the middle and crisp at the edges at once.

The difference between msemen and a croissant is the type of fat. A croissant uses cold butter, which must stay solid until it reaches the oven; msemen uses liquid oil and fine semolina, whose granules do the mechanical job of physically separating the layers. That is why msemen can be made on a warm countertop without chilling anything — and why semolina is not an ingredient you can skip here.

### Ingredients (for 10 pieces)

**For the dough:**
* 400 grams white flour
* 100 grams fine semolina
* 1 teaspoon salt
* 1 teaspoon sugar
* 1 teaspoon dry yeast — optional, adds softness
* About 300 ml lukewarm water

**For folding and cooking:**
* 60 ml neutral vegetable oil or light [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/)
* 30 grams melted butter (or *smen*, Moroccan fermented butter)
* 60 grams additional fine semolina, for sprinkling

**To serve:** [honey](/en/food/honey/), butter, or salty cheese.

### Choosing ingredients and substitutes

Regular supermarket white flour and semolina work; fine semolina gives a smoother dough. Ordinary melted butter is the default, and there is no need to hunt down smen, the Moroccan fermented butter. Semolina is not cornstarch and not quick-cooking couscous.

### Method

1. **Knead a soft dough.** Mix the flour, semolina, salt, sugar and yeast, and add the water gradually. Knead for 8-10 minutes until the dough is smooth, elastic and very soft — softer than regular bread dough, almost sticky. A stiff dough won't stretch later.
2. **Rest.** Divide into 10 balls, oil them lightly, and cover for 20 minutes. Resting relaxes the gluten, and that is what makes stretching possible.
3. **Stretch.** Oil the work surface well (don't flour it — oil it) and flatten each ball with your palms into a rectangle as thin as possible. **This is the step that determines the number of layers:** the thinner and more nearly translucent the dough, the more layers you get.
4. **Oil and sprinkle.** Brush the rectangle with the oil and melted butter mixture and sprinkle a teaspoon of semolina over it. The semolina is what mechanically separates the layers.
5. **Fold twice.** Fold both sides into the center to make a long strip, brush again with oil and semolina, and fold the strip crosswise to make a square. Set aside for 10 minutes.
6. **Flatten again.** Gently flatten each square to about 15x15 cm. Don't press hard — hard pressing glues the layers together and undoes all the work.
7. **Cook in a pan.** Heat a heavy pan over medium heat **with no oil** and cook each msemen for about 2 minutes per side, until golden spots appear. Press lightly with a spatula while it cooks so the center cooks too.
8. **Serve hot.** Msemen is a dish of the moment — it is best in the first minutes after leaving the pan, with [honey](/en/food/honey/) and butter or with salty cheese.

### Nutrition facts and calorie estimate

The recommended serving is one piece of about 85 grams.

| Component | Per piece (85 grams) |

| **Calories** | **about 270** |

| Fat | about 9 grams |
| Protein | about 6 grams |

This number is misleading only if you forget how msemen is eaten. The piece itself, without toppings, is roughly like two slices of bread — about 270 calories, of which about 70 come from the oil and butter of the folding and the rest from the flour. The trouble starts on the plate: a tablespoon of [honey](/en/food/honey/) adds about 64 calories, and a tablespoon of butter about 100 more. One msemen "done properly" easily reaches 430 calories, and most people eat two.

Nutritionally this is a white-flour pastry: readily available carbohydrate, little fiber, and almost no micronutrients. The only upgrade that really works is replacing some of the white flour with whole-wheat flour — see [the comparison of wheat, spelt and rye flours](/en/compare/wheat-spelt-rye-flour/). It is important to say here that a full swap doesn't work: whole-wheat flour absorbs more water and tears when stretched, and without stretching there are no layers.

### Variations and tips

* **Stuffed msemen:** Scatter fried [onion](/en/food/onion/) and cilantro, or seasoned ground meat, before the final fold. This turns it into a main course — about 380-450 calories per piece.
* **Partial whole-wheat flour:** Replace up to a third of the white flour with whole-wheat flour. Adds about 2 grams of fiber per piece and still allows stretching.
* **Less fat in the folding:** 40 ml of oil instead of 60 cuts about 15 calories per piece, at the cost of less distinct layers.
* **Freezing:** Freeze them **uncooked**, folded, with baking paper between the pieces. Cook straight from the freezer in a hot pan. Cooked msemen that has been frozen loses its crispness completely.

### Summary

Msemen is an exercise in lamination done without a fridge or an oven: a soft dough stretched until translucent, oil and semolina between the layers, and a dry pan. About 270 calories per piece — roughly like two slices of bread — until the honey and butter arrive, which can double the number. It is served in the morning and alongside mint tea, and it completes a meal that opens with [harira](/en/recipes/morocco/harira/). More recipes in [Moroccan cuisine](/en/recipes/morocco/).

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in msemen?**
Answer: A piece of about 85 grams is about 270 calories. With a tablespoon of [honey](/en/food/honey/) and a tablespoon of butter, the way it is usually served, the serving reaches about 430 calories. Msemen stuffed with meat or onion ranges from 380 to 450 calories per piece.

**Question: What is the difference between msemen and baghrir?**
Answer: These are two completely different breads, even though they are served together. Msemen is a kneaded dough stretched and folded into a multi-layered square; *baghrir* is a liquid yeasted batter cooked on one side only that develops hundreds of holes on its surface. Both are eaten with honey and butter.

**Question: My msemen came out tough — what went wrong?**
Answer: Almost always a dough that was too stiff or a rest that was too short. The dough should be soft and almost sticky, and rest for 20 minutes before stretching. A second common cause is pressing hard during the final flattening, which glues the layers together.

**Question: Can you make msemen with whole-wheat flour?**
Answer: Only partly. Up to a third of the flour can be whole wheat, which adds about 2 grams of fiber per piece. A full swap doesn't work because whole-wheat flour absorbs more water and tears when stretched, and without thin stretching there are no layers.

**Question: Can you freeze msemen?**
Answer: Yes, but **before** cooking. Freeze the folded squares with baking paper between the pieces, and cook straight from the freezer in a hot pan, adding a minute per side. Cooked msemen that has been frozen goes soft and loses its crispness.
