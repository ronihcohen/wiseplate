## Matbucha (*مطبوخة*)

**In Moroccan Arabic: *مطبوخة*, literally "cooked."** The name is exactly the description: this is a salad whose whole distinction is that it is cooked rather than fresh. In Hebrew it appears as "מטבוחה," "מטבוחא" or "סלט מרוקאי חריף" (spicy Moroccan salad), and all three names refer to the same dish.

Matbucha is built from just two ingredients — [tomatoes](/en/food/tomato/) and [red peppers](/en/food/red-pepper/) — and three flavorings: [garlic](/en/food/garlic/), paprika and hot pepper. Everything else is time. The vegetables cook over low heat for about an hour until the water has almost completely evaporated, and the result is a dense, concentrated spread that bears no resemblance to the vegetable mix that went into the pot. In Morocco it is one of the standard starters; in Israel it has become a fixture of the Shabbat table and of [hummus](/en/food/chickpeas/) restaurants, usually without anyone remembering where it came from.

**Prep time:** 20 minutes · **Cook time:** 60-75 minutes · **Total:** about an hour and a half · **Yield:** 8 servings · **Difficulty:** easy

### The cooked salad table, and why they're all in olive oil

In Morocco a festive meal opens with *kemia* — a table of six to twelve small salad bowls, the vast majority of them cooked rather than fresh. Matbucha, [zaalouk](/en/recipes/morocco/zaalouk/), *taktouka* (roasted peppers in tomatoes), [carrots](/en/food/carrot/) with cumin, [beets](/en/food/beets/) with cumin, [lentils](/en/food/lentil/) with cilantro and pickled hot peppers — all are eaten at room temperature, all are based on [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/), and all improve after a night in the fridge.

The reason is historical and practical: before home refrigeration, a vegetable cooked in oil and salt kept for days while a fresh vegetable kept for hours. Cooking drives off the water, oil slows oxidation, and the acidity of the tomato adds protection. The same principle is behind the Turkish *zeytinyağlılar* family — see [imam bayildi](/en/recipes/turkey/imam-bayildi/) — and behind Iranian [mirza ghasemi](/en/recipes/iran/mirza-ghasemi/).

Nutrition gets an unintended bonus here: the lycopene in [tomatoes](/en/food/tomato/) is a fat-soluble carotenoid, and its bioavailability rises significantly when the tomato is cooked in the presence of oil. Matbucha — tomatoes cooked for a whole hour in olive oil — provides almost exactly those conditions.

### Ingredients (for 8 servings)

* 1 kg very ripe [tomatoes](/en/food/tomato/), peeled and chopped (or grated)
* 700 grams [red peppers](/en/food/red-pepper/), weighed before roasting and peeling
* 5-6 cloves [garlic](/en/food/garlic/), sliced
* 80 ml [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/)
* 1-2 green hot peppers, whole or chopped
* 1 teaspoon sweet paprika
* 1 teaspoon salt, 1/2 teaspoon sugar (only if the tomatoes are tart)

### Choosing ingredients and substitutes

Any ripe tomato works; out of season you can use a kilogram of canned peeled and chopped tomatoes, including the juice, and reduce in the pot to the desired thickness. Shushka (long red) or bell peppers are suitable for roasting. Weigh the peppers before roasting, since the number of units varies a lot with their size.

### Method

1. **Roast the peppers.** Roast them in the oven at 220 degrees Celsius (428°F) or directly over a flame until the skin is charred, transfer to a covered bowl for 10 minutes, then peel and cut into strips. **This is the step that sets the flavor** — a roasted, peeled pepper gives a smoky sweetness a raw pepper never will.
2. **Peel the tomatoes.** Score an X in the bottom of each tomato, dip in boiling water for 30 seconds and transfer to cold water. The skin comes off easily. Skins left in the pot roll up into tough threads.
3. **Start the cooking.** Heat half of the olive oil in a wide pot, add the garlic and fry for just 30 seconds — burnt garlic makes the whole pot bitter.
4. **Cook long.** Add the tomatoes, the roasted peppers, the hot pepper, paprika, salt — and the sugar, if the tomatoes are tart. Cook over low heat for 60-75 minutes in an **uncovered** pot, stirring every 10 minutes. The open pot is what allows evaporation.
5. **Add the rest of the oil at the end.** In the last ten minutes, add the remaining olive oil and stir. Oil added at the end keeps its fruity taste; oil cooked for an hour loses it.
6. **Check the thickness.** The matbucha is ready when you drag a spoon across the bottom of the pot and the channel it leaves doesn't close right away. If it closes — it needs another quarter hour.
7. **Cool.** Matbucha is eaten at room temperature or cold, never hot from the pot. The flavor settles after a few hours in the fridge.

### Nutrition facts and calorie estimate

The recommended serving is a small bowl of about 100 grams — about one-eighth of the pot. Note that about 1.7 kg of raw vegetables reduce to about 800 grams of matbucha, meaning **the calories are twice as concentrated** relative to weight.

| Component | Per small bowl (100 grams) |
|---|---|
| **Calories** | **about 130** |
| Fat | about 9 grams (almost all olive oil) |
| Carbohydrates | about 11 grams |
| Of which fiber | about 3 grams |
| Protein | about 2 grams |

Here lies the most common calorie mistake around this dish. Matbucha is seen as a "salad," and salad is seen as something you don't count. In practice **about two-thirds of its calories come from the olive oil**: 80 ml of oil is about 640 calories divided among eight servings. The vegetables themselves, before touching the oil, are only about 45 calories per serving.

That doesn't mean the oil is unnecessary — the fat profile of [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) is what the Mediterranean diet rests on, and it is also what enables lycopene absorption. But three spoonfuls of matbucha with a slice of bread are a small meal, not a garnish.

### Variations and tips

* **Less oil:** 50 ml instead of 80 brings the serving down to about 100 calories. The flavor changes a little, the texture more.
* **Smooth vs. chunky matbucha:** The northern version (Tangier, Tetouan) tends toward distinct pepper strips; the inland version tends toward a uniform spread. Both are authentic — the difference is in the cut size and the time.
* **Without peeling the peppers:** Possible, but the skins remain as translucent threads in the dish. There is no way around this step.
* **Making ahead and freezing:** Keeps a week in the fridge in a sealed container with a layer of oil on top, and about three months frozen. It is one of the only dishes on this page that freezes without losing anything.

### Summary

Matbucha is two spoonfuls of technique: roast the peppers, and give the pot an uncovered hour. Those two steps turn cheap vegetables into a concentrated spread that keeps for a week and improves every day. Nutritionally it is a dish of vegetables and olive oil, about 130 calories per small bowl, most of them from the oil — a fact worth remembering when the bowl sits on the table next to five others. The full salad table starts with [zaalouk](/en/recipes/morocco/zaalouk/), and the rest of the dishes are in [Moroccan cuisine](/en/recipes/morocco/).

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in matbucha?**
Answer: A 100-gram small bowl is about 130 calories, of which about 80 come from the olive oil. In the version with 50 ml of oil instead of 80, the serving drops to about 100 calories. The vegetables themselves contribute only about 45 calories per serving.

**Question: What is the difference between matbucha and zaalouk?**
Answer: The main vegetable. Matbucha is built on [red peppers](/en/food/red-pepper/) and tomatoes, and [zaalouk](/en/recipes/morocco/zaalouk/) on [eggplant](/en/food/eggplant/) and tomatoes. Both cook long in olive oil and are served cold at the same salad table, but the flavor is completely different — matbucha is sweet and spicy, zaalouk smoky and soft.

**Question: Why do you have to peel the peppers and the tomatoes?**
Answer: The skins of both don't soften in long cooking but roll up into tough, translucent threads in the dish. With the pepper, peeling also comes together with roasting, which is the source of the smoky sweetness — which is why it is the most important step in the recipe.

**Question: How long does matbucha keep?**
Answer: About a week in the fridge in a sealed container, preferably with a thin layer of olive oil on top to slow oxidation. Frozen it keeps about three months and thaws without losing texture, unlike most other cooked salads.

**Question: Is matbucha a Moroccan or an Israeli dish?**
Answer: Moroccan in origin, from the traditional *kemia* table. It came to Israel with immigration from Morocco in the 1950s and 1960s, became part of the local Shabbat table and hummus menus, and along the way also became spicier than the average Moroccan version.
