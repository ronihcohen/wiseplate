## Dafina / S'hina

**In Moroccan Arabic: *الدفينة*, from the root d-f-n, meaning "buried."** The name describes the method — a pot buried in the fire. In various regions of Morocco it is also called *s'hina*, from a root meaning "hot," and that is the more common name among Moroccan-born Jews in Israel, where it is written "דפינה" or "סחינה." Both names refer to the same dish.

Dafina is the Shabbat dish of Moroccan Jews: a single pot that goes onto a low flame on Friday evening and comes off it at midday the next day, after 12-16 hours. It is built in layers — [meat](/en/food/red-meat/) on the bottom, [wheat berries](/en/compare/wheat-spelt-rye-flour/) or [rice](/en/food/rice/) in a cloth bag, [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/), [potatoes](/en/food/baked-potato/), [sweet potatoes](/en/food/sweet-potato/), [dates](/en/food/dates/) and eggs in their shells — and every component takes on the flavor of all the others.

**Prep time:** 45 minutes · **Cook time:** 12-16 hours · **Total:** a whole night · **Yield:** 8 servings · **Difficulty:** medium

### Three Shabbat dishes, one constraint

Ashkenazi cholent (hamin), Iraqi [tbit](/en/recipes/iraq/tbit/) and Moroccan dafina were born of the same problem: the ban on lighting a fire on Shabbat alongside the wish for a hot meal on Shabbat day itself. The solution was the same in all three communities — a pot set on a constant heat source before Shabbat begins that keeps cooking on its own — and the differences in content reflect what was available and cheap in each place.

The Ashkenazi version was built around beans and barley, the Iraqi version around [chicken stuffed with rice](/en/recipes/iraq/tbit/), and the Moroccan version around [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/), wheat and root vegetables — and above all around **sweetness**. [Dates](/en/food/dates/), [sweet potato](/en/food/sweet-potato/) and a spoonful of honey or silan (date syrup) are the hallmark of dafina, and they have no counterpart in the other two. The eggs in their shells, on the other hand, are shared by all three.

What happens in the pot overnight is not just "more of the same." At 90-100 degrees Celsius (194-212°F) over hours, the collagen in the connective tissue breaks down into gelatin and gives the sauce a body you can't get in two hours, and the starch in the wheat and legumes is fully gelatinized. The eggs are the clearest demonstration: after 12 hours they are completely brown, because iron from the yolk and sulfur from the white react over time, and the white itself becomes creamy rather than rubbery.

### Ingredients (for 8 servings)

**For the meat and base:**
* 1 kg [beef](/en/food/red-meat/) for long cooking — shoulder roast, shank or brisket
* 2 large [onions](/en/food/onion/), sliced
* 60 ml oil
* 1 teaspoon [turmeric](/en/food/turmeric/), 1 teaspoon paprika, 1/2 teaspoon cinnamon, 1/2 teaspoon allspice, black pepper
* 4 whole cloves of [garlic](/en/food/garlic/)
* Salt

**For the layers:**
* 300 grams dried [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/), soaked overnight
* 300 grams whole wheat berries (or [rice](/en/food/rice/) — see the note below), in a loosely tied cloth bag
* 6 medium [potatoes](/en/food/baked-potato/) (about 900 grams), whole and peeled
* 2 [sweet potatoes](/en/food/sweet-potato/) (about 400 grams), cut into large chunks
* 8 [eggs](/en/food/eggs/) in their shells, washed
* 4-6 pitted [dates](/en/food/dates/)
* 1 tablespoon [honey](/en/food/honey/) or silan (see [the honey, silan and maple comparison](/en/compare/honey-silan-maple/))
* Boiling water to cover

### Choosing ingredients and substitutes

Wheat berries for hamin are sold in supermarkets and in legume shops; bulgur is not a substitute in the same long cooking. If you choose rice, use the instructions in the variations section. The cloth bag should be made for cooking food, and stay loosely tied so the grains can swell. Date silan works in place of the honey in the same amount.

### Method

1. **Sear the meat.** Heat oil in a large heavy pot and sear the pieces of meat on all sides until deeply browned — about 10 minutes. This is the only step in the recipe that uses high heat, and it is what sets the color of the whole pot.
2. **Fry the onion and spices.** Remove the meat, add the onion and fry for 8 minutes until golden, then add the whole garlic cloves and the spices and stir for half a minute until fragrant.
3. **Build the layers.** Return the meat, and arrange around it the soaked chickpeas, the bag of wheat, the whole potatoes and the sweet potatoes. Scatter the dates and drizzle the honey.
4. **Arrange the eggs.** Set the eggs in their shells among the vegetables so they are covered by liquid. They need full contact with the liquid to darken evenly.
5. **Pour in the liquid.** Add **boiling** water to cover everything by about 3 cm. Cold water at this stage stops the cooking and toughens the meat. Adjust the salt carefully — the liquid will reduce and the saltiness will concentrate.
6. **Bring to a boil and lower the heat.** Boil for 15 minutes uncovered, skim off any foam, cover tightly, and lower to the lowest possible flame — or move to the oven at 100-110 degrees Celsius (212-230°F).
7. **Leave for 12-16 hours.** Don't open it, don't stir. **Opening the pot partway lets out steam and lowers the temperature**, and that is also what burns the bottom.
8. **Serve in layers.** Remove carefully and arrange on the plate: a slice of meat, a brown egg, a potato, wheat from the bag and a spoonful of sauce.

### Nutrition facts and calorie estimate

The recommended serving is one-eighth of the pot — about 125 grams of meat, one egg, a potato, some of the wheat and chickpeas and a little sauce.

| Component | Per serving |
|---|---|
| **Calories** | **about 850** |
| Protein | about 43 grams |
| Carbohydrates | about 88 grams |
| Of which fiber | about 15 grams |
| Fat | about 36 grams |

This is the heaviest dish on this page, and it was built that way on purpose — as the single meal of a whole day. Two-thirds of the calories come from the combination of meat and starch: the meat alone contributes about 310 calories per serving, the wheat about 130 and the potato about 87.

On the other hand, there is a side here that almost no other meat dish offers: **15 grams of fiber per serving**, more than half the daily recommendation, from the [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/), the whole wheat berries and the root vegetables — alongside a significant amount of [iron](/en/food/iron/) and [zinc](/en/food/supplements/zinc/) from the meat and [potassium](/en/food/supplements/potassium/) from the vegetables. The effective way to bring the number down is not to give up a component but to change the proportions: 700 grams of meat instead of a kilo, and one tablespoon less oil, bring the serving down to about 740 calories. A double serving — a quarter of the pot — is about 1,700 calories, and that is the more common mistake.

### Variations and tips

* **Dafina with rice instead of wheat:** Very common and quicker to cook. Tie the rice bag very loosely — rice swells to three times its size.
* **Chicken version:** Replace the beef with a whole chicken (about 1.5 kg) **without skin**. It drops to about 700 calories per serving, but the sauce comes out thinner without the collagen. A whole chicken **with** the skin saves almost nothing — about 850 calories, exactly like the beef.
* ***Kouclas*:** A homemade dough dumpling buried in the pot, very traditional. Adds about 120 calories per serving.
* **Safety:** Dafina that stays on a **continuous** flame is safe, because the temperature is too high for bacteria to multiply. The risk starts after the meal, when the pot sits on the counter for hours — see [the danger in reheated rice](/en/food/reheated_rice_dangers/).

### Summary

Dafina is defined by time: 12-16 hours that turn tough meat tender, chickpeas creamy and eggs brown. At about 850 calories per serving it is a day's single meal rather than a dish on a menu, but its 15 grams of fiber and 43 grams of protein are a ratio most meat dishes never reach. The only two things that can ruin it are too high a flame and lifting the lid partway through. The equivalent Shabbat dish on the site is Iraqi [tbit](/en/recipes/iraq/tbit/); more recipes in [Moroccan cuisine](/en/recipes/morocco/).

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in dafina?**
Answer: A serving of one-eighth of the pot is about 850 calories, with about 43 grams of protein and 15 grams of fiber. In the skinless chicken version the serving drops to about 700 calories, while chicken with skin saves almost nothing. A double serving — a quarter of the pot, and the amount many people actually take — reaches about 1,700 calories.

**Question: What is the difference between dafina and cholent (hamin)?**
Answer: The same idea, three communities. Ashkenazi cholent is built on beans and barley and tastes salty and rich; Moroccan dafina is built on chickpeas, wheat, root vegetables and [dates](/en/food/dates/), and tastes slightly sweet, spiced with cinnamon and allspice; and Iraqi [tbit](/en/recipes/iraq/tbit/) is built around chicken stuffed with rice. The brown eggs are common to all three.

**Question: Why do the eggs turn brown in dafina?**
Answer: During long cooking, iron from the yolk and sulfur from the white react with each other, and the brown color forms gradually from the outside in. At the same time, flavors and color from the sauce seep into the egg through the porous shell. After 12 hours the texture changes too — the white becomes creamy instead of rubbery.

**Question: The dafina burned on the bottom — what went wrong?**
Answer: Too high a flame, too little liquid at the start, or opening the lid during the night and letting out steam. The safe fix is an oven at 100-110 degrees Celsius (212-230°F) instead of a flame.

**Question: Can you make dafina ahead and freeze it?**
Answer: The stew freezes well for up to three months, but potatoes crumble when thawed and the eggs turn rubbery. It is better to freeze only the meat, sauce, chickpeas and wheat, and add fresh vegetables and eggs when reheating.
