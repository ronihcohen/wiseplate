## Turkish Red Lentil Soup (Mercimek Çorbası)

**In Turkish: *Mercimek Çorbası*, literally "lentil soup."** In Hebrew it is called "מרק עדשים כתומות טורקי" or transliterated as "מרסימק צ'ורבסי". It is the most common soup in Turkey — served in every tea house, every roadside stop and every *lokanta* (workers' restaurant), from morning to night, and usually as the first course of the meal.

The soup is built on hulled [red lentils](/en/food/lentil/), which fall apart completely during cooking and create a velvety texture with no need for cream. What makes it Turkish rather than a generic lentil soup are two things: a little puréed [red pepper](/en/food/red-pepper/) or pepper paste fried at the start, and *kırmızı biber yağı* — melted butter with paprika and dried mint that is poured over at the end and marks the soup with red rings.

**Prep time:** 10 minutes · **Cooking time:** 35 minutes · **Total:** about 45 minutes · **Yield:** 6 servings · **Difficulty:** Easy

### Why every Turkish meal starts with soup

In Turkish, *çorba* isn't just the name of a dish but an entire category on the menu, and it's almost always the first item on it. The reason is both historical and practical: soup was the dish that could be served immediately to anyone who walked in, at any hour, and it was what people broke the daily fast with during Ramadan. Even today, the *iftar* meal in Turkey almost always opens with lentil soup — not out of religious custom, but because a warm, light liquid is the right thing to put into an empty stomach before a heavy dish.

From a nutritional point of view it's a smart order: a serving of legume soup before the meal supplies fiber and fluid, slows the pace of eating and reduces the amount eaten afterward — an effect measured again and again in satiety studies. The same idea is behind a vegetable salad eaten before a main course, which is why [cacık](/en/recipes/turkey/cacik/) and soup appear side by side in the same Turkish meal.

### Ingredients (for 6 servings)

**For the soup:**
* 1.5 cups (about 300 g) [red lentils](/en/food/lentil/), rinsed well
* 1 large [onion](/en/food/onion/), finely chopped
* 1 medium [carrot](/en/food/carrot/), chopped
* 1 medium potato, diced (it's what gives the thickness)
* 2 [garlic](/en/food/garlic/) cloves, crushed
* 2 tablespoons [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/)
* 1 tablespoon Turkish pepper paste (*biber salçası*) or tomato paste
* 1.5 liters water or vegetable stock
* 1 teaspoon [turmeric](/en/food/turmeric/) — optional, gives color and a little [curcumin](/en/food/supplements/turmeric/)
* 1 teaspoon cumin, salt and black pepper to taste

**For the pepper butter (on top):**
* 2 tablespoons butter or [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/)
* 1 teaspoon sweet paprika (or Turkish pul biber)
* 1 teaspoon dried mint

**To serve:** [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) wedges — not a garnish, but part of the dish.

### Choosing ingredients and substitutes

Regular split red lentils from the supermarket work. Instead of a tablespoon of Turkish pepper paste, use a tablespoon of tomato paste and half a teaspoon of sweet paprika. Sweet paprika also works for the butter on top, so there's no need to buy pul biber specially.

### Method

1. **Fry the flavor base.** Heat the olive oil in a wide pot, add the onion and fry 5-6 minutes until translucent. Add the carrot and garlic and fry another minute.
2. **Fry the paste.** Add the pepper paste and stir 30-60 seconds. This step is critical — briefly frying the paste brings out its sweetness and deepens the color.
3. **Cook.** Add the rinsed lentils, potato, spices and water. Bring to a boil, lower to a low heat and cook 25-30 minutes with the pot partially covered, until the lentils fall apart completely.
4. **Blend.** Purée with an immersion blender until completely smooth. If the soup is too thick add boiling water, because it keeps thickening as it cools.
5. **The pepper butter.** In a small pan melt the butter over low heat, turn off the heat and only then add the paprika and mint — paprika burns and turns bitter within seconds over a live flame.
6. **Serve.** Ladle the soup into bowls, drizzle the red butter on top and squeeze over lemon. In Turkey it's served with fresh white bread.

The values are an estimate for the recipe as written. Two tablespoons of butter divided among six servings add about 25 calories per serving; replacing them with [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) barely changes the number but does change the fat profile.

Nutritionally this is one of the best-value dishes in Turkish cuisine: about 13 g of protein and 7 g of fiber in 290 calories. The lemon isn't just flavor — its [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) significantly improves absorption of the plant [iron](/en/food/iron/) in the lentils, which on its own is poorly absorbed.

### Variations and tips

* **A fully vegan version:** Replace the butter with olive oil. The rest of the recipe is vegan when cooked with water or vegetable stock.
* **Thickness:** The potato is responsible for the texture. You can replace it with half a cup of [rice](/en/food/rice/) or bulgur, which will also give the soup body.
* **Without an immersion blender:** Cook 10 more minutes and mash with a fork — the result is coarser, and that's how it's served in the villages.
* **Making ahead:** The soup improves on the second day and keeps 4 days in the fridge and about 3 months in the freezer. When reheating, add water, because it thickens.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in Turkish lentil soup?**
Answer: A 300 ml bowl is about 290 calories, with about 13 g of protein and 7 g of fiber. A restaurant serving tends to be larger and comes with white bread, so it actually reaches 450-550 calories.

**Question: Do red lentils need soaking before cooking?**
Answer: No. Red lentils are hulled, so they cook within 20-30 minutes and don't require soaking. A rinse in cold water until it runs clear is enough to remove starch and dust.

**Question: Can the soup be made without an immersion blender?**
Answer: Yes. Cook 10 minutes longer than the recipe's time and mash with a fork or a potato masher. Red lentils break down on their own, and the result is slightly coarse but completely authentic.

**Question: Why is the soup served with lemon?**
Answer: Beyond the acidity that balances the legume, the [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) in lemon boosts absorption of the plant iron from the lentils several times over. It's a successful nutritional pairing that tradition created by chance rather than by design.

**Question: The soup came out too thick — what do I do?**
Answer: Add boiling water gradually and stir, not cold water that would stop the cooking. Remember that the soup thickens significantly as it cools and when reheated, so it's worth leaving it a little thinner than seems right in the pot.
