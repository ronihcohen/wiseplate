## Cacık

**In Turkish: *Cacık*, pronounced "jah-jik" (in Hebrew it is written "ג'אג'יק").** It is the Turkish relative of Greek tzatziki, and the difference between the two is not trivial: Turkish cacık is much more liquid, almost a cold soup, and is served in a small bowl with a spoon alongside meat dishes. Greek tzatziki is thick and is used as a dip.

It is the simplest dish to make in Turkish cuisine, and also the most nutritionally balanced: [yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/), [cucumber](/en/food/cucumber/), [garlic](/en/food/garlic/) and mint — almost no calories and real protein and [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/). In the Turkish summer it is served ice cold next to [Adana kebab](/en/recipes/turkey/adana-kebab/), where its job is to cool the heat and balance the fat of the meat.

**Prep time:** 15 minutes · **Chilling time:** 30 minutes · **Total:** about 45 minutes · **Yield:** 4 small bowls · **Difficulty:** Very easy

### A whole family of yogurt, cucumber and garlic

The idea of thinned yogurt with cucumber and garlic spans the whole region, and almost every cuisine has its own name for it: *cacık* in Turkey, tzatziki in Greece, *tarator* in Bulgaria and Albania (where [walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/) are added), and [mast-o-khiar](/en/recipes/iran/mast-o-khiar/) in Iran, which gets raisins and nuts. Linguists agree that the Greek name was borrowed from the Turkish, but the dish itself probably predates both names — yogurt, cucumber and garlic were available throughout the region long before it had borders.

The logic behind the dish is the same in every version: a sour, cooling dish that accompanies a fatty or spicy meat dish. In Turkey that's [Adana kebab](/en/recipes/turkey/adana-kebab/) and [manti](/en/recipes/turkey/manti/), in Greece grilled meat, and in Iran [rice](/en/food/rice/) with [lamb](/en/food/lamb/). This isn't a tradition of garnish but of balance.

### Ingredients (for 4 small bowls)

* 500 g plain [yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/) (3% or 5%; thick Greek yogurt will need more water)
* 2 medium [cucumbers](/en/food/cucumber/)
* 2 [garlic](/en/food/garlic/) cloves, crushed with a pinch of salt
* 1 tablespoon dried mint, or 3 tablespoons chopped fresh mint
* 100-150 ml cold water (or ice water)
* 1 tablespoon [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) for serving
* Salt, and a pinch of sumac or pul biber for garnish

### Choosing ingredients and substitutes

Plain 3% or 5% yogurt and regular supermarket cucumbers work. Add the water gradually, since thickness varies between products. For garnish you can use sumac, or a pinch of sweet paprika instead of pul biber, the Turkish pepper flakes.

### Method

1. **Prepare the cucumber.** Grate on a coarse grater or chop into tiny cubes. Cubes are more authentic and give some bite; grating gives an even texture.
2. **Remove the water — the critical step.** Salt the cucumber with 1 teaspoon of salt, let it stand 10 minutes in a colander and squeeze by hand. Without this step the cucumber will release water for an hour and the dish will separate into layers in the bowl.
3. **Mix.** Whisk the yogurt with the crushed garlic and mint until smooth, add the squeezed cucumber and thin with the cold water to a liquid texture — cacık should flow off the spoon, not sit on it.
4. **Adjust the seasoning** with salt, and chill for at least 30 minutes. Cacık must be very cold.
5. **Serve** in a small bowl, with a drizzle of [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) and a sprinkle of dried mint or sumac on top.

### Nutrition facts and calorie estimate

The recommended serving is a small bowl of about 150 g, a quarter of the recipe.

| Component | Per small bowl (150 g) |
|---|---|
| **Calories** | **About 120** (including the tablespoon of olive oil on top) |
| Protein | About 5 g |
| Fat | About 7 g |
| Carbohydrates | About 9 g |
| Calcium | About 150 mg (about 15% of the daily value) |

Without the olive oil on top, a serving is only about 90 calories; with 0% yogurt it drops to about 60, and with full-fat [Greek yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/) it rises to about 145. The tablespoon of olive oil drizzled on top is about 120 calories for the whole recipe, or about 30 per bowl — roughly a quarter of the number.

This is one of the few cases where a traditional dish is both nutrient-dense and low in calories: about 5 g of protein and about 15% of daily calcium in about 120 calories. The yogurt also provides probiotic bacteria, similar to [kefir](/en/food/kefir/) but in a narrower variety.

### Variations and tips

* **The Antalya version** adds crushed [almonds](/en/food/almonds/) and a little vinegar, and serves cacık almost as a cold soup.
* **Ayran:** The same idea without cucumber — yogurt, water and salt, whipped. Turkey's national lunchtime drink, about 60 calories per glass.
* **Lactose-free:** Well-fermented yogurts are low in lactose, and most lactose-sensitive people handle them well. Alternatively you can use [soy milk](/en/food/soy-milk/)-based yogurt, though the calcium content differs.
* **Garlic:** Crushing the garlic with salt breaks the cells and releases allicin. If you want a milder flavor, crush only one clove and make the dish an hour ahead.

### Summary

Cacık is proof that a four-ingredient dish can be a complete dish: it cools, adds protein and calcium, and balances fatty meat dishes at a cost of 120 calories. It's meant to be served alongside [Adana kebab](/en/recipes/turkey/adana-kebab/) or [pide](/en/recipes/turkey/pide/), not on its own. More recipes in [Turkish cuisine](/en/recipes/turkey/).

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What's the difference between cacık and tzatziki?**
Answer: Texture and role. Turkish cacık is thinned with water and liquid, and is served in a small bowl with a spoon alongside a meat dish. Greek tzatziki is thick, based on strained yogurt, and is used as a dip for bread and vegetables. The ingredients themselves are almost identical.

**Question: How many calories are in cacık?**
Answer: A 150 g bowl made with 3% yogurt is about 120 calories including the tablespoon of olive oil on top, with about 5 g of protein. Without the oil it's about 90 calories, and with full-fat Greek yogurt it reaches about 145.

**Question: Why did my cacık turn watery after an hour?**
Answer: The cucumber wasn't salted and squeezed before mixing. Cucumber is about 95% water, and the salt in the yogurt gradually draws it out. Salting for 10 minutes and squeezing by hand solves the problem completely.

**Question: Can cacık be made ahead?**
Answer: Yes, and it even improves after a few hours in the fridge as the garlic mellows. It keeps up to two days; after that it starts to sour and release liquid. Stir again before serving.

**Question: Is cacık suitable for people sensitive to lactose?**
Answer: Usually, yes. Fermentation breaks down much of the lactose in yogurt, and its live bacteria keep helping to break it down in the gut, so many people who can't tolerate milk do well with yogurt. Very sensitive people can use [soy](/en/food/soy-milk/)-based yogurt, but then it's worth checking that it's fortified with [calcium](/en/food/supplements/calcium/).
