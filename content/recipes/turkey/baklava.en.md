## Baklava

**In Turkish: *Baklava*.** The dessert on which the city of Gaziantep in southeastern Turkey built its name — Gaziantep baklava is even registered as a product with a protected origin (PGI) in the European Union. Dozens of layers of paper-thin dough sheets (*yufka*), ground nuts, clarified butter, and sugar syrup poured at exactly the right moment.

Authentic Turkish baklava is distinguished from its two sisters — the Greek and the Levantine — by two details: it is sweetened with sugar syrup only, not [honey](/en/food/honey/), and it isn't spiced with [cinnamon](/en/food/cinnamon/) or cloves. It should taste of [pistachio](/en/food/pistachios/) and butter, and nothing else.

**Prep time:** 45 minutes · **Baking time:** 50 minutes · **Soaking time:** 4-6 hours · **Yield:** about 30 squares · **Difficulty:** High

### From the palace baklava procession to the European quality label

At the Ottoman court, baklava wasn't just a dessert but an instrument of rule. On the 15th of every Ramadan, a procession called the *Baklava Alayı* was held at Topkapı Palace: trays of baklava prepared in the palace kitchens were distributed to the Janissary units, and each unit carried its trays in a procession back to its barracks. Handing out the trays was a gesture of the sultan toward the army, and its absence was seen as a sign of tension.

In our time the symbol has moved to Gaziantep: in 2013 *Antep baklavası* was registered as a product with a protected geographical indication in the European Union — the first Turkish product to receive such recognition. The registration defines both ingredients (clarified butter, semolina, Antep pistachios) and technique, chief among them stretching the *yufka* sheets by hand to a thickness of less than half a millimeter. A baklava master in Gaziantep specializes in this stage for years before touching the syrup.

### Ingredients (for a pan of 30 squares)

* 400 g phyllo sheets (about 20-24 sheets)
* 250 g coarsely ground [pistachios](/en/food/pistachios/) — or [walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/) in the versions from central Anatolia and further east
* 200 g clarified butter (ghee), or regular butter, melted
* 2-3 tablespoons flour or fine semolina (sprinkled between the bottom layers to prevent sogginess)

**For the syrup:**
* 400 g sugar
* 350 ml water
* 1 tablespoon [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) juice — prevents the sugar from crystallizing

### Choosing ingredients and substitutes

Frozen phyllo sheets from the supermarket work; thaw them in the fridge according to the package and keep them covered while you work. Clarified butter is also sold as ghee, but you can use 200 g of regular melted butter for a slightly different flavor, keeping an eye on the browning. Pistachios should be shelled and unsalted; walnuts are an easy-to-find substitute at the same weight.

### Method

1. **Make the syrup and cool it.** Boil the sugar and water, add the lemon and cook 12-15 minutes until slightly thickened. Cool completely. **This is the rule that governs the whole recipe: cold syrup on hot baklava.** Hot syrup meeting a hot pastry is absorbed too quickly and makes a soggy, soft pastry.
2. **Lay the bottom.** Brush a 30x40 cm pan (or 26x36 for thicker squares) with butter and lay 8-10 phyllo sheets, gently brushing each sheet with melted butter. Sprinkle a little flour between the bottom sheets.
3. **Fill.** Scatter the ground nuts in an even layer.
4. **Lay the top.** Lay another 10-12 sheets, each brushed with butter. The top layer should be brushed generously.
5. **Cut before baking.** With a sharp knife, cut into squares or diamonds all the way to the bottom of the pan. After baking the pastry is too brittle and will crumble when cut.
6. **Bake** at 170°C (338°F) for about 45-50 minutes, until an even golden-amber color. Relatively low heat over a long time is what creates the crispness.
7. **Pour the syrup.** Take it out of the oven and immediately pour the cold syrup slowly and evenly. You'll hear a crackle — that's the sign the temperatures are right.
8. **Wait 4-6 hours** before serving, for full absorption. Baklava is eaten at room temperature, never from the fridge.

### Nutrition facts and calorie estimate

The recommended serving is one square, about 45 g.

| Component | Per square (45 g) |
|---|---|
| **Calories** | **About 205** |
| Carbohydrates | About 23 g |
| Of which sugar | About 14 g |
| Fat | About 11 g |
| Protein | About 3 g |

This is one of the cases where the number misleads in two opposite ways. On the one hand, 205 calories per square doesn't sound dramatic — until you remember that almost nobody stops at one square. Three squares are about 615 calories and about 42 g of sugar — more than one and a half times the recommended daily limit for added sugar for a woman (about 25 g), and beyond the limit for a man (about 36 g). On the other hand, the traditional Turkish serving really is one or two small squares alongside bitter Turkish [coffee](/en/food/coffee/), not a plateful.

The only ingredient with real nutritional value is the nuts: [pistachios](/en/food/pistachios/) provide monounsaturated fat, [magnesium](/en/food/supplements/magnesium/) and [polyphenols](/en/food/polyphenols/). They are also the expensive ingredient, which is exactly why industrial versions skimp on them — a good sign of good baklava is a thick layer of nuts, not a thick layer of syrup.

For perspective on the role of added sugar in the diet, see [the sugar industry's tactics](/en/food/sugar-industry-tactics/).

### Variations and tips

* **A slightly lighter version:** Make the syrup from 300 g of sugar instead of 400, and pour a little less of it. The baklava will stay crisp and drop to about 175 calories per square.
* **[Walnuts](/en/food/walnuts/) instead of pistachios:** Common in central Anatolia, cheaper, and adds plant omega 3 ([ALA](/en/food/supplements/omega-3/)).
* **Smaller cuts:** In Gaziantep they serve squares of 25-30 g. That's not stinginess — it's the right way to eat a dessert this calorie-dense.
* **Freezing:** Baked baklava keeps in the freezer for about two months and thaws at room temperature without losing its crispness.

### Summary

Baklava is a dessert defined by technique — thin sheets, generous butter, and cold syrup on a hot pastry — not by expensive ingredients. About 205 calories and 14 g of sugar in a single square make it a special-occasion treat rather than an everyday one, exactly as it is eaten in Turkey. A much lighter Turkish dessert, for ending an everyday meal, is [sütlaç](/en/recipes/turkey/sutlac/). More recipes in [Turkish cuisine](/en/recipes/turkey/).

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in baklava?**
Answer: A standard square of about 45 g is about 205 calories with about 14 g of sugar. Large squares in bakeries reach 70-80 g, meaning about 320-360 calories per piece.

**Question: Why is cold syrup poured over hot baklava?**
Answer: The temperature difference allows controlled absorption without the sheets going soft. Hot syrup on a hot pastry is absorbed too quickly and makes the baklava soggy and soft, whereas cold syrup is absorbed slowly and leaves the layers crisp.

**Question: What's the difference between Turkish and Greek baklava?**
Answer: The Turkish one is sweetened with sugar syrup only and is usually filled with pistachios, without spices. The Greek one is usually sweetened with honey, filled with walnuts and spiced with cinnamon and cloves. The difference is immediately noticeable in taste and aroma.

**Question: Can baklava be made without butter?**
Answer: You can use vegetable oil, but it's the compromise most noticeable in the result: clarified butter gives the flavor and the characteristic dry crispness. Calorie-wise the difference is negligible — oil and butter are very close in energy per gram.

**Question: How long does baklava keep?**
Answer: In an airtight container at room temperature it keeps 7-10 days. Don't put it in the fridge — the cold crystallizes the syrup and makes the sheets hard and dry. For long storage, freeze it baked.
