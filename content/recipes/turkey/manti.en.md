## Manti (Mantı)

**In Turkish: *Mantı*, usually written manti in English.** In Hebrew it is written "מנתי" or "מנטי". These are tiny meat dumplings considered the oldest Turkish dish to have survived: it arrived with the Turkic tribes from Central Asia, and it's a cousin of Tibetan momo, Russian pelmeni and Georgian khinkali.

The pride of Kayseri manti, the most famous version, is the size of the dumplings: so small that 40 of them are supposed to fit on a single spoon. According to tradition, a prospective bride was tested on her ability to make them small enough. In a modern home kitchen you can settle for reasonably sized dumplings and still get the dish. The cooked manti is topped with three layers: [yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/) with [garlic](/en/food/garlic/), red butter with paprika, and dried mint and sumac on top.

**Prep time:** 60-75 minutes · **Cooking time:** 12 minutes · **Total:** about an hour and a half · **Yield:** 4 servings · **Difficulty:** High

### From China to Kayseri: the dumpling's migration

The name *mantı* is etymologically related to the Chinese *mantou*, a hint at the route the dish traveled: meat-filled dough dumplings migrated west from East Asia along with the nomadic tribes, leaving behind along the way Tibetan momo, Russian pelmeni, Georgian khinkali and Turkish manti. At each stop two things changed — the size of the dumpling and the sauce — but the idea stayed the same: a thin wrapper of flour around a little meat, a method that turns a small amount of meat into a filling meal.

In Kayseri, the capital of manti, the dish is still made in large quantities and dried: uncooked dumplings are spread on trays and dried completely, then stored for months in a jar and cooked as needed. Dried *kuru mantı* is sold packaged in markets like pasta, and it's essentially a traditional form of convenience food.

### Ingredients (for 4 servings)

**For the dough:**
* 300 g flour
* 1 [egg](/en/food/eggs/)
* 120-140 ml water
* 1/2 teaspoon salt

**For the filling:**
* 250 g [ground meat](/en/food/red-meat/) (lamb or beef, 15% fat)
* 1 small [onion](/en/food/onion/), grated and squeezed of its liquid
* A handful of chopped parsley
* Salt and black pepper to taste, 1/2 teaspoon cumin and 1/2 teaspoon pul biber or the substitute below

**For the yogurt sauce:**
* 400 g plain [yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/)
* 3 [garlic](/en/food/garlic/) cloves, crushed with salt

**For the paprika butter:**
* 50 g butter (or 3 tablespoons [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/))
* 1 teaspoon [tomato](/en/food/tomato/) paste, 1 teaspoon paprika
* Dried mint and sumac for sprinkling

### Choosing ingredients and substitutes

Regular white flour and ground meat from the butcher work. Instead of half a teaspoon of pul biber, the Turkish pepper flakes, use a quarter teaspoon of sweet paprika and a pinch of chili to taste. Local plain yogurt works for the sauce; if it's very thick, thin it with a spoonful or two of the cooking water once it has cooled slightly.

### Method

1. **Knead a stiff dough.** Mix the flour, egg, salt and water and knead 10 minutes into a firm, elastic dough, stiffer than pasta dough. Wrap and rest 30 minutes — the rest is what allows thin rolling.
2. **Make the filling.** Mix the meat with the squeezed onion, parsley and spices. Squeezing the onion matters: liquid in the filling wets the dough and opens the dumplings during cooking.
3. **Roll out and cut into squares.** Roll the dough into a very thin sheet (1-2 mm) and cut into 2.5-3 cm squares. For a quick version — 4 cm squares.
4. **Fill.** Place a [pea](/en/food/peas/)-sized amount in the center of each square, lift the four corners up and pinch between them into a closed bundle.
5. **Cook.** Bring salted water to a boil and cook the dumplings 8-12 minutes until they float and soften. You can also bake them 10 minutes at 200°C (392°F) before boiling — that's the custom in Kayseri, and it adds a toasty flavor.
6. **Prepare the toppings.** Whisk the yogurt with the garlic. Melt the butter, and add the tomato paste and paprika off the heat.
7. **Assemble.** Drain the dumplings, pour the garlic yogurt over them, then the paprika butter, and sprinkle with dried mint and sumac. Serve immediately.

The calorie breakdown shows where the weight lies: the dough about 290 calories, the meat about 165, the butter about 90 and the yogurt about 60. Two decisions change the number more than anything else — swapping the butter for [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) (a small calorie saving, a big improvement in fat profile) and choosing 0% yogurt instead of 3% (a saving of about 30 calories per serving).

Nutritionally it's a fairly balanced dish for a dough-and-meat dish: 25 g of protein per serving, a good share of it from the yogurt. The downside is the white flour and the amount of saturated fat from the butter and meat.

### Variations and tips

* **Vegan manti:** Fill with cooked [lentils](/en/food/lentil/) and fried onion, and replace the yogurt with [soy](/en/food/soy-milk/) yogurt. This version is common in Turkey during fasting periods.
* **Freezing:** Arrange the uncooked dumplings on a tray in a single layer, freeze, then transfer to a bag. Cook straight from frozen, adding 2-3 minutes.
* **Saving time:** Larger squares (4 cm) cut the work in half. Tradition prefers small; the home kitchen prefers getting it done.
* **The cooking water:** Save a spoonful of cooking water and stir it into the yogurt — that way it doesn't split on contact with the hot dumplings.

### Summary

Manti is the Turkish dish that demands the most patience and the fewest ingredients: flour, egg, meat and yogurt. About 610 calories per serving with 25 g of protein, and the yogurt layer is what makes it balanced rather than heavy. ### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in manti?**
Answer: A serving of about 220 g of dumplings with yogurt sauce and paprika butter is about 610 calories, with about 25 g of protein. Using 0% yogurt and olive oil instead of butter brings the serving down to about 550 calories.

**Question: Why do the dumplings open during cooking?**
Answer: Three common reasons — a filling that's too moist (squeeze the onion), corners that weren't sealed firmly, and water boiling too hard. Cook at a gentle boil, not a rolling one, and seal the dumplings by pressing firmly between the corners.

**Question: Can manti be made ahead?**
Answer: Yes, and that's the usual way. Freeze the uncooked dumplings in a single layer on a tray until hard, then pack them in a bag. They keep about 3 months and are cooked straight from frozen.

**Question: What's the difference between manti and ravioli or pelmeni?**
Answer: Size and sauce. Kayseri manti is especially tiny — several dozen dumplings per serving — and it's served in garlic yogurt with paprika butter instead of tomato sauce or butter alone. The dough itself is very similar to fresh pasta dough.

**Question: How many dumplings are in one serving?**
Answer: In the traditional Kayseri version, where each dumpling is less than a centimeter and a half across, a serving is 40-60 dumplings. In the home version in this recipe, with 2.5-3 cm squares, a serving of about 200 g is roughly 20-25 dumplings.
