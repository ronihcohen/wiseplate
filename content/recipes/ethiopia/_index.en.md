The Ethiopian cuisine known in Israel arrived mainly with the Beta Israel communities from the Gondar and Tigray regions, but Ethiopia is large and multilingual and has no single uniform cuisine. [Doro wat](/en/recipes/ethiopia/doro-wat/) and [dabo](/en/recipes/ethiopia/dabo/) earned a special place on the Shabbat table of Ethiopian Jewish families; [kitfo](/en/recipes/ethiopia/kitfo/) is associated instead with the Gurage people; [shiro](/en/recipes/ethiopia/shiro-wat/) is common in northern Ethiopia and in Eritrea too. These differences are noted on each page instead of attributing every dish to Beta Israel.

The shared base is sour [injera](/en/recipes/ethiopia/injera/), on which stews are placed and eaten with the right hand. [Berbere and awaze](/en/recipes/ethiopia/awaze/) provide heat and color — the first is a dry blend and the second a sauce; turmeric leads in the mild alicha stews; garlic, ginger, fenugreek and korarima build depth. In Israel, teff flour, berbere and shiro are sold in Ethiopian shops and spice shops, and the rest of the ingredients are found in any supermarket.

### What defines the flavor and the meal

* **Injera is both plate and cutlery.** Several stews are placed on it, and you tear off a piece to scoop up a bite. The holes, the "eyes," come from proper fermentation and a hot pan — baking powder is not required.
* **Wat is not the name of one dish.** It is a family of stews. Red wat usually includes berbere; alicha is lighter and milder, usually with turmeric.
* **The onion cooks before the fat.** In many wat stews a lot of onion is steamed with almost no fat until it breaks down; only then are the oil and spices added.
* **Christian fasting and Jewish kashrut are not the same thing.** Ethiopian fasting dishes are vegan and therefore sometimes suit kashrut, but they originate in the customs of the Ethiopian church. Beta Israel kept customs of Shabbat, ritual slaughter and separating meat from dairy; don't attribute one tradition to the other.
* **Raw meat is not a Beta Israel tradition.** Raw kitfo is a well-known Gurage dish, but Ethiopian Jews traditionally avoided raw meat. The recipe here is the fully cooked `yebesele` version, with kosher meat.

### A note on calories and serving

The calculation for each stew does not include injera unless stated otherwise; a medium injera adds about 200 calories. Weights are before cooking, and the values were divided by the stated yield. Oil left in the pot is counted in full. An Israeli manufacturer's label takes precedence over the estimate when you use branded berbere, shiro or teff flour. A deviation of 10%–20% is possible because of the cut of meat, the amount of oil and the size of the injera.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Is all Ethiopian food spicy?**
Answer: No. Misir and doro wat are usually spicy, but atkilt, gomen and dabo are mild. You can reduce the [berbere](/en/recipes/ethiopia/awaze/) without giving up garlic, ginger and korarima.

**Question: Where can I buy teff and berbere in Israel?**
Answer: In Ethiopian food shops, spice shops and health food chains; some supermarkets have a dedicated shelf. Check the date and that the package is sealed, because whole teff flour spoils relatively quickly. You can [make awaze from berbere in five minutes](/en/recipes/ethiopia/awaze/).

**Question: Are all the recipes from Ethiopian Jews?**
Answer: No. Doro wat and dabo are well documented on the Ethiopian Jewish table. Other dishes belong to the wider Ethiopian cuisine, and the pages note a regional or religious origin when it is known.

**Question: How do I serve an Ethiopian meal at home?**
Answer: Spread out a large injera, arrange several stews on it without mixing them in advance and serve rolled injeras on the side. Eat with the right hand or with cutlery, whatever suits the diners.
