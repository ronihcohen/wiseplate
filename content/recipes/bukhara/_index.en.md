"Bukharian Jews" is a name for Jewish communities from Central Asia that lived not only in Bukhara but also in Samarkand, Tashkent, the Fergana Valley and areas that today belong to Uzbekistan and Tajikistan. So the cuisine shares rice, doughs, lamb and techniques with its Uzbek and Tajik neighbors, but it developed within Jewish community life, kashrut and Shabbat. [Osh savo](/en/recipes/bukhara/osh-savo/) is a clear example of Jewish adaptation: a pilaf that became a Shabbat hamin (overnight stew).

This page presents versions familiar in Bukharian homes and restaurants in Israel. It doesn't claim that manti, lagman or non belong only to Bukharian Jews; these are broad Central Asian foods that entered the community's repertoire. On the other hand, [bakhsh](/en/recipes/bukhara/bakhsh/), the Shabbat osh traditions and the style of dumplings as preserved in Israel are a clear part of Bukharian Jewish identity.

### What defines the Bukharian table

* **The rice is given structure.** In oshpelo, meat, onion and carrots are cooked as a base and only then is the rice added, without stirring it again and again.
* **Herbs are an ingredient, not a garnish.** In bakhsh, bunches of cilantro and parsley are used until the rice is truly green.
* **One dough doesn't make all the dumplings.** Manti are steamed and large; dushpara are small and cook in soup; goshtgizhda is baked.
* **Lamb fat is a traditional flavor, not a technical requirement.** In Israel you can use fatty beef or oil, and the recipe notes the change in flavor and calories.
* **Fresh cuts through rich.** [Bukharian salad](/en/recipes/bukhara/bukharian-salad/) is finely chopped and served alongside rice and meat to add acidity and freshness.

### A note on the calories

The calculations are based on the weight of the raw ingredients divided by the stated yield. Lamb fat, oil and meat are the big variables. In osh savo all the dried fruit and oil are counted; in the salad all the olive oil is counted. Values for pastries are per piece of the defined size, not for an unweighed "pastry." A deviation of 10%–20% is possible depending on the cut of meat and the amount of fat left in the pot.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Is Bukharian cuisine simply Uzbek cuisine?**
Answer: Not exactly. It grew up within an Uzbek-Tajik space and shares many dishes with it, but the customs of kashrut, Shabbat and community history created a distinct Jewish repertoire.

**Question: Do you have to use lamb?**
Answer: No. Beef shoulder, available in Israel, suits most of the recipes. A little lamb fat adds a traditional flavor, but you can use a neutral oil.

**Question: What is the difference between manti and dushpara?**
Answer: Manti are large and cooked by steaming. Dushpara are much smaller and cook inside a soup.

**Question: Which dishes can be made ahead?**
Answer: Osh savo is meant for long cooking; oshpelo and bakhsh are best the same day. Manti and dushpara can be frozen uncooked, and non and goshtgizhda freeze well after baking.
