## Miso Soup (味噌汁)

**In Japanese: *misoshiru* (味噌汁), literally "miso soup."** In Japan it is not a starter but a fixed part of the meal — served alongside [rice](/en/food/rice/), at breakfast too, and usually in a small bowl of 150-200 ml. In Hebrew it is simply called "מרק מיסו".

The soup is built from just two components: **dashi**, a Japanese sea stock, and **miso**, a fermented soybean paste. Everything else — [tofu](/en/food/tofu/), wakame seaweed, scallions — is *gu*, the solids, and they change with the season. Two technical rules decide whether the soup comes out right, and both are about temperature.

**Prep time:** 10 minutes (plus 30 minutes soaking the kombu) · **Cook time:** 15 minutes · **Total:** about 25 minutes · **Yield:** 4 small bowls · **Difficulty:** easy

### The two rules every miso soup rests on

**Kombu does not boil.** The kombu seaweed comes out of the water the moment small bubbles appear around the edges, at about 90-95°C (194-203°F). Beyond that it releases slimy compounds and a bitter taste, and the stock is spoiled. Katsuobushi (dried bonito flakes) should not boil either — the recommended steeping temperature is about 80°C (176°F), and boiling draws bitterness out of it.

**Miso is not cooked.** This is the more important of the two rules. Miso is dissolved at the end, with the heat off or very low, and never boiled. Boiling does two things: it breaks down the delicate aroma of fermentation, and it kills the lactic acid bacteria and enzymes that unpasteurized miso contains. More on the [miso](/en/food/miso/) page.

The practical way to dissolve it is in a small vessel: put the miso in a ladle, pour a little of the hot soup into it, dissolve it with a fork until there are no lumps, and only then pour it back into the pot.

### Ingredients (for 4 small bowls)

**For the dashi (about 1 liter):**
* 1 liter water
* 10 grams [kombu seaweed](/en/food/seaweed/) (a piece about 10×10 cm)
* 15 grams katsuobushi — dried bonito flakes

**For the soup:**
* 4 tablespoons (about 70 grams) [miso](/en/food/miso/) paste — white (*shiro*) for a mild, sweet taste, red (*aka*) for a stronger, saltier taste
* 200 grams soft or silken [tofu](/en/food/tofu/), or regular tofu for a firmer texture, in 1.5 cm cubes
* 5 grams dried [wakame seaweed](/en/food/seaweed/)
* 2 scallions, thinly sliced

### Choosing ingredients and substitutes

Miso, kombu, wakame and bonito flakes are sold in Israel in Asian grocery stores, and some in health food stores. If you have no kombu and bonito, replace all of the dashi with a liter of mild, unsalted vegetable stock and start from step 5; the taste will be different. You can swap the silken tofu for 200 grams of regular tofu, which will be firmer, and leave out the wakame. There is no substitute for the miso itself that gives the same soup; do not replace it with the same amount of soy sauce.

### Method

1. **Soak the kombu.** Place the kombu in **cold** water and soak for at least 30 minutes (overnight in the fridge also works). Do not wash off the white powder on it — it is mannitol, which means flavor, not dirt.
2. **Heat slowly and remove.** Heat over medium-low heat. **The moment small bubbles appear around the edges — take out the kombu.** If it boils, the stock will come out bitter and slimy.
3. **Add the katsuobushi.** Lower the temperature with about 100 ml of cold water, add the flakes and leave for 3-5 minutes without stirring, until they sink.
4. **Strain.** Pass through a fine sieve. **Do not press the flakes** — pressing draws bitterness out of them and clouds the stock.
5. **Return to the heat and add the solids.** Return the dashi to the pot, heat to just below a boil and add the tofu cubes. Cook for a minute or two over low heat.
6. **Rehydrate the wakame.** Add the dry seaweed straight to the pot — it swells 8-10 times within a minute, so 5 grams is a lot.
7. **Dissolve the miso and that's it.** Turn off the heat. Take a ladle, put the miso in it, add a little hot soup, dissolve with a fork until there are no lumps, and return it to the pot. **From here on, do not boil.**
8. **Serve immediately** with sliced green [onion](/en/food/onion/) on top. Miso soup that stands tends to separate into layers — stir gently before ladling.

### Nutrition facts and calorie estimate

The recommended serving is a small bowl of about 200 ml, a quarter of the recipe.

| Nutrient | Per small bowl (200 ml) |
|---|---|
| **Calories** | **about 80** |
| Protein | about 6 grams |
| Carbohydrates | about 6 grams |
| Fat | about 3.5 grams |
| **Sodium** | **about 700-900 mg** |

This is the lowest-calorie soup in the category, and at the same time one of the highest in [sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/) relative to serving size. One tablespoon of miso (about 18 grams) contains roughly 600-650 mg of sodium, so a single small bowl provides about a third to 40% of the recommended daily limit (2,300 mg) in under a hundred calories.

That is the trade-off worth knowing before calling miso soup a "diet soup." Anyone watching their sodium intake can use 3 tablespoons of miso instead of 4 and increase the amount of dashi — the flavor stays, and the sodium drops to about 550 mg per bowl.

On the other hand, miso is a fermented food that provides lactic acid bacteria — as long as it is unpasteurized and has not been boiled. More on the [miso](/en/food/miso/) page and the [kefir](/en/food/kefir/) page. The [tofu](/en/food/tofu/) supplies most of the protein, and the [seaweed](/en/food/seaweed/) provides [iodine](/en/food/supplements/iodine/) — plenty of it, and sometimes too much: kombu is one of the most concentrated sources of iodine in nature, so daily consumption of strong homemade dashi is not recommended for people with thyroid problems.

### Variations and tips

* **Vegan dashi (*kombu dashi*):** leave out the katsuobushi and use kombu only, optionally with 2-3 dried shiitake [mushrooms](/en/food/mushrooms/). The stock is more delicate and completely vegan — and so is the whole soup, which is free of [milk](/en/food/milk/) and [eggs](/en/food/eggs/) anyway.
* **Quick dashi:** ready-made *dashi no moto* sachets save 40 minutes, but add monosodium glutamate and extra sodium. The difference in taste is smaller than people tend to think; the difference in sodium is not.
* **Seasonal solids:** thinly sliced daikon radish, spinach, fresh shiitake mushrooms, Japanese sweet potato, or an egg beaten into the soup. The Japanese rule is two to three solids at most per bowl.
* **Types of miso:** *shiro* (white) is fermented for a short time, sweet and mild; *aka* (red) is fermented for a long time, strong and much saltier. With the red version, use a smaller amount.
* **Making ahead:** **the dashi** can be made ahead and kept for 3 days in the fridge, or frozen. The finished soup is best made fresh — reheating miso spoils its aroma, which is why in Japan it is made for each meal.

### Summary

Miso soup is two ingredients and two temperature decisions: don't boil the kombu, and don't boil the miso. About 80 calories per small bowl with 6 grams of protein make it the lightest soup here — as long as you remember that it also carries about 700-900 mg of sodium in that same bowl. ### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in miso soup?**
Answer: About 80 per 200 ml bowl with tofu and seaweed, including about 6 grams of protein. It is the lowest-calorie soup in the category — but also one of the richest in sodium relative to the serving, with about 700-900 mg per bowl.

**Question: Why shouldn't miso be boiled?**
Answer: For two reasons. Boiling breaks down the volatile compounds formed during fermentation and flattens the taste, and it also kills the lactic acid bacteria and enzymes in unpasteurized miso. Dissolve the miso in a ladle with a little hot soup, return it to the pot, and turn off the heat.

**Question: What is dashi, and can you make miso soup without it?**
Answer: Dashi is a Japanese stock made from kombu seaweed and dried bonito flakes, and it gives the soup its layer of umami. You can make miso soup with water alone, but the result is much flatter. A good alternative is dashi made from kombu and dried shiitake mushrooms — completely vegan.

**Question: Is miso soup healthy?**
Answer: It is low in calories, provides protein from the tofu and the soy, and unpasteurized miso is a fermented food. The only significant caveat is sodium: one small bowl carries about a third of the recommended daily limit. Anyone watching it can cut down to three tablespoons of miso instead of four.

**Question: What is the difference between white and red miso?**
Answer: Fermentation time. *shiro* (white) is fermented for weeks to months, and is sweet, light and mild. *aka* (red) is fermented for months to years, and is dark, strong and saltier. In a recipe with red miso, use about 25% less.

**Question: How much wakame seaweed do you add to the soup?**
Answer: Very little — 5 grams dry is enough for four small bowls. Wakame swells 8-10 times in liquid within a minute, and a heaped spoonful of dry seaweed will fill the pot. There is also a nutritional reason for restraint: [seaweed](/en/food/seaweed/) is very rich in [iodine](/en/food/supplements/iodine/), and too much of it burdens the thyroid gland.
