## Chelo Kabab Koobideh

**In Persian: *چلو کباب کوبیده*, literally "rice and pounded kebab."** In Hebrew it is written "צ'לו כבאב כובידה" and also appears as "כובידה", "קובידה" or "צ'לו קבב". *Chelo* is Persian steamed rice, and *koobideh* means "pounded" — a name that describes the technique: the meat is pounded to a sticky, uniform texture, not coarsely ground as in Turkish [Adana kebab](/en/recipes/turkey/adana-kebab/).

This is Iran's national dish, and equally the dish that is easiest to ruin. Only two ingredients form the base of the mixture — ground meat and onion — and everything depends on the ratio between them and on how the onion is handled. Onion that has not been squeezed of its juices will make the kebab fall off the skewer within seconds, which is why everyone who has tried making koobideh at home has failed at least once.

**Prep time:** 30 minutes · **Resting time:** an hour (meanwhile: soaking the rice) · **Rice cooking time:** 50 minutes · **Grilling time:** 8 minutes · **Yield:** 4 servings · **Difficulty:** medium-high

### Why rice and kebab, of all things, became a national dish

Until the 19th century, rice in Iran was a food of the wealthy classes, and kebab was the food of markets and roadside inns. The combination of the two — *chelo kabab* — was born in Tehran at the end of the Qajar period, when the city's restaurants began serving kebab on a bed of rice instead of in bread. This combination became a dish eaten by every layer of society, and today it is served in exactly the same way in upscale restaurants and at street stalls.

The way the dish is served is almost completely fixed: one or two skewers on a mound of white rice, a pat of butter melting into the rice, a [tomato](/en/food/tomato/) grilled over the same coals, sumac in a small bowl, raw red onion, and to drink, *doogh* — a diluted, salted yogurt drink, a relative of Turkish ayran. The sumac is not decoration: its acidity is meant to cut through the lamb fat.

### Ingredients (for 4 servings — 8 skewers)

**For the kebab:**
* 700 grams ground [lamb](/en/food/lamb/) or beef, 20% fat (lean meat will crumble)
* 2 large [onions](/en/food/onion/)
* 1 [egg](/en/food/eggs/) yolk — not accepted in every household, but very helpful for first-timers
* 1 teaspoon salt, 1/2 teaspoon black pepper, 1/2 teaspoon [turmeric](/en/food/turmeric/)

**For the rice (chelo):**
* 3 cups basmati [rice](/en/food/rice/)
* 3 tablespoons salt (for the soaking and cooking water)
* 3 tablespoons butter or oil
* A pinch of saffron, dissolved in 3 tablespoons hot water

**To serve:** 4 tomatoes, sumac, red onion, butter, [lemon](/en/food/lemon/).

### Choosing ingredients and substitutes

Ask the butcher for ground meat with about 20% fat; there is no need for a special Persian cut. Regular basmati is fine for chelo. Saffron is sold in spice shops in small packages; you can skip it and serve white rice. Turmeric adds a different color and is no substitute for the flavor of saffron.

### Method

1. **Squeeze the onion — the step that decides everything.** Grate the onion on a fine grater, transfer to a sieve or cheesecloth and squeeze hard until no liquid remains. What is left is about 150 grams of dry onion pulp — about half the weight of the original onion. The liquid is discarded.
2. **Pound the meat.** Mix the meat with the onion pulp, the yolk and the spices, and knead by hand for a full 10 minutes — until the mixture is sticky and clings to your palm when you turn it over. This is not kneading for meatballs; the goal is to stretch the meat proteins.
3. **Chill for an hour.** A cold mixture sticks to the skewer; a room-temperature one does not.
4. **Soak and cook the rice.** Rinse the rice until the water runs clear, soak in salted water for at least an hour, cook in boiling salted water for only 5-7 minutes until the grain is soft on the outside and still firm in the center, and drain.
5. **Steam for tahdig.** Return to the pot with 2-3 tablespoons of oil and a quarter cup of water, form a mound of rice, wrap the lid in a towel and steam for 40 minutes over minimal heat. The result: rice separate grain by grain with a golden crust at the bottom (*tahdig*).
6. **Shape the skewers.** Wet your hands and spread about 110 grams of the mixture on each flat, wide skewer, pressing with four fingers to create the characteristic ridges.
7. **Grill fast.** Over blazing coals, 3-4 minutes per side, turning continuously. Koobideh cooks very quickly, and ground meat left on the fire one more minute dries out.
8. **Serve.** Mix a spoonful of rice with the saffron water and scatter over the mound, add butter, the skewers and the grilled tomato, and sprinkle with sumac.

### Nutrition facts and calorie estimate

The recommended serving is one skewer (about 120 grams of grilled meat) with 180 grams of cooked rice.

| Nutrient | Per serving (skewer + rice) |
|---|---|
| **Calories** | **about 550** |
| Protein | about 28 g |
| Carbohydrates | about 48 g |
| Fat | about 27 g |
| of which saturated fat | about 11 g |
| Iron | about 3 mg |

The calorie breakdown shows that the dish splits almost in half: the meat about 300 calories and the rice with butter about 250. At a restaurant the serving is much bigger — two skewers on a full mound of rice reach 900-1,000 calories, and with the fried *tahdig* and a glass of doogh it goes higher still.

The strong side is the protein and the heme [iron](/en/food/iron/). The weak side is twofold: white [rice](/en/food/rice/), with a high glycemic load and little fiber, and saturated fat from the lamb and the butter. The two accompaniments that come with the dish — raw red onion and grilled tomato — are what bring fiber and [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) into it, so they are worth eating rather than leaving on the plate.

### Variations and tips

* **Lighter version:** ground beef with 12%-15% fat with a yolk as a stabilizer, and brown rice instead of white. The serving drops to about 450 calories and doubles the fiber.
* **Without a grill:** an oven tray with a rack, top broiler at 250°C (480°F), 5 minutes per side. There is no smoky flavor, but the texture holds.
* **On leftover rice:** cooked rice kept at room temperature is one of the more dangerous foods in the kitchen — see [the danger in reheated rice](/en/food/reheated_rice_dangers/). Cool it quickly and refrigerate within an hour.
* **Tahdig:** for an extra crust, line the bottom of the pot with potato slices or yogurt mixed with saffron before loading in the rice.

### Summary

Chelo kabab koobideh is a rare case of a national dish built from only two ingredients, with all its difficulty lying in the technique: squeezing the onion, long kneading and chilling. About 550 calories per serving of skewer and rice, with 28 grams of protein and one weak side — the white rice. A Persian dish that balances it well is [mast-o-khiar](/en/recipes/iran/mast-o-khiar/), and a Turkish dish parallel in concept is [Adana kebab](/en/recipes/turkey/adana-kebab/). ### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in chelo kabab koobideh?**
Answer: A serving of one skewer (about 120 grams) with 180 grams of rice is about 550 calories. A restaurant serving with two skewers and a full mound of rice reaches 900-1,000 calories, before the tahdig and the drink.

**Question: Why does koobideh fall off the skewer?**
Answer: Almost always because of the onion juice. Grate the onion and squeeze it dry before mixing, knead the meat for a full 10 minutes to stretch the proteins, and chill the mixture for an hour in the refrigerator. A flat, wide skewer rather than a round one is another requirement.

**Question: What is the difference between koobideh and Turkish Adana kebab?**
Answer: Koobideh is pounded and kneaded to a sticky, uniform texture and seasoned with very few spices — onion, salt, pepper and turmeric. [Adana kebab](/en/recipes/turkey/adana-kebab/) is chopped with a knife to a coarse texture, contains lamb tail fat and hot isot pepper, and contains no onion at all.

**Question: What is tahdig and how do you get it?**
Answer: *Tahdig* is the golden rice crust that forms at the bottom of the pot during steaming, and it is considered the most sought-after part of the dish. You get it by long steaming over a low flame in a pot with a layer of oil at the bottom, and sometimes with the help of a layer of potatoes, lavash bread or yogurt beneath the rice.

**Question: Can you use beef instead of lamb?**
Answer: Yes, and it is very common in Iran too. What matters is not the type of meat but the fat percentage: below 15% fat the mixture crumbles and dries out. With lean beef, add an egg yolk as a stabilizer, and don't skip the long kneading.
