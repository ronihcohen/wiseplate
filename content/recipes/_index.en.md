A recipe is, in the end, a nutrition table written in a different language. The exact same dish can range from 250 to 600 calories depending on the amount of oil, the serving size and whether it is fried or baked — and most recipe sites simply don't say so. This section presents recipes from different cuisines using ingredients familiar in Israel, substitutes for less available ingredients, and a nutritional estimate per serving. The substitutes are listed in each recipe, along with the expected change in flavor or texture.

### How we calculate calories per serving

* **The serving is defined before calculating.** A "serving" is a concrete number — a 300 ml bowl, a 150-gram skewer, a 45-gram square. Without such a definition, any calorie number is meaningless.
* **Oil is the biggest variable.** A tablespoon of [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) is about 120 calories. A recipe that absorbs four tablespoons in frying is completely different from the same recipe baked in the oven, even if the ingredient list is identical.
* **The numbers are an estimate, not a measurement.** The fat percentage of the meat, the brand, the substitutes and the amount of sauce can change the result considerably. The estimates here are meant to give the right order of magnitude for a decision, not a laboratory value.

### What's on every recipe page

Every page is built in the same order, so you can jump straight to the relevant part:

1. **What the dish is and where it comes from** — the original name, its common Hebrew transliterations, and what sets it apart from a similar dish it gets confused with.
2. **Times and quantity** — prep time, cooking time, number of servings and difficulty, in one line at the top of the recipe.
3. **Ingredients for a defined number of servings** — and usually also substitutes for ingredients that are hard to find in Israel.
4. **The method**, highlighting the step that determines the result. Almost every recipe has one such step, and it isn't always the step that looks most complicated.
5. **A nutrition table per serving** — calories and macronutrients, alongside an explanation of where the calories come from and what changes them.
6. **Variations** — including lighter versions, and how many calories they really save.
7. **Questions and answers** about the common mishaps with that dish.

### Soups

**[Soups](/en/recipes/soups/)** — ten classic soups that don't belong to a single cuisine: [chicken soup](/en/recipes/soups/chicken-soup/), [vegetable soup](/en/recipes/soups/vegetable-soup/), [split pea soup](/en/recipes/soups/split-pea-soup/), [pumpkin soup](/en/recipes/soups/pumpkin-soup/), [mushroom soup](/en/recipes/soups/mushroom-soup/), [French onion soup](/en/recipes/soups/french-onion-soup/), [minestrone](/en/recipes/soups/minestrone/), [tomato soup](/en/recipes/soups/tomato-soup/), [miso soup](/en/recipes/soups/miso-soup/) and [tom yum](/en/recipes/soups/tom-yum-soup/).

This section is organized by type of dish rather than by origin, and it shows clearly how meaningless the word "soup" is in calorie terms on its own: [miso soup](/en/recipes/soups/miso-soup/) is about 80 calories a bowl, and [French onion soup](/en/recipes/soups/french-onion-soup/) with its bread and cheese topping is about 480 — six times as much, in the same serving bowl. More soups also appear within the cuisines themselves: [Turkish lentil soup](/en/recipes/turkey/mercimek-corbasi/), Moroccan [harira](/en/recipes/morocco/harira/), Iranian [ash reshteh](/en/recipes/iran/ash-reshteh/) and Iraqi [kubba hamusta](/en/recipes/iraq/kubba-hamusta/).

The first three cuisines share borders and centuries of mutual influence, yet they are built completely differently: Turkish around bread, the grill and meze; Persian around rice and stew; and Iraqi around the sourness of tamarind and bread that served as an eating utensil. The comparison stands out clearly in pairs of parallel dishes — [cacık](/en/recipes/turkey/cacik/) vs. [mast-o-khiar](/en/recipes/iran/mast-o-khiar/), [Adana kebab](/en/recipes/turkey/adana-kebab/) vs. [koobideh](/en/recipes/iran/chelo-kabab-koobideh/), [sütlaç](/en/recipes/turkey/sutlac/) vs. [sholeh zard](/en/recipes/iran/shole-zard/) vs. [kleicha](/en/recipes/iraq/kleicha/), and Iraqi [tashreeb](/en/recipes/iraq/tashreeb/) vs. Iranian [abgoosht](/en/recipes/iran/abgoosht/) — two dishes built on the same idea of bread soaking up broth.

Moroccan cuisine stands at the other end of the same continuum, with two thousand kilometers separating it from the three. It replaces rice and bread with [couscous](/en/recipes/morocco/couscous/), and fresh sourness with salt-preserving — preserved lemon and olives instead of tamarind and dried lime. And yet the same ideas recur: Moroccan [dafina](/en/recipes/morocco/dafina/) and Iraqi [tbit](/en/recipes/iraq/tbit/) are the same Sabbath dish born of the same constraint, and [zaalouk](/en/recipes/morocco/zaalouk/), [mirza ghasemi](/en/recipes/iran/mirza-ghasemi/) and [imam bayildi](/en/recipes/turkey/imam-bayildi/) are three different ways of handling the same eggplant.

Tripolitan cuisine is geographically close to Moroccan but built differently: tomato paste, paprika and caraway create the red base of [chraime](/en/recipes/tripoli/chraime/) and [shorba](/en/recipes/tripoli/shorba/), and the Italian period in Libya left pasta and [nokidess](/en/recipes/tripoli/nokidess/) as an entirely local part of it. In [tbecha bsilk](/en/recipes/tripoli/tbecha-bsilk/) the word "silk" refers to chard, and [arisa](/en/recipes/tripoli/arisa/) is a wheat hamin (Sabbath stew), not a hot spread.

### Three mistakes that keep coming up when reading recipes

* **The recipe's "serving" isn't the serving you eat.** A recipe that says "serves 4" divides the pot into four; in practice most people take a third of the pot. Every calorie calculation starts with the question of how many servings it really made.
* **An ingredient left in the pan is counted in full.** Frying oil poured away at the end, marinade that gets thrown out and cooking water that is drained don't enter the body. Oil absorbed into the food, on the other hand, does — and that is exactly the difference between [roasted eggplant and fried eggplant](/en/recipes/turkey/imam-bayildi/).
* **The "garnish" sides are sometimes half the dish.** Bread, [yogurt](/en/food/greek-yogurt/), rice and sauce alongside the dish can add more calories than the dish itself. Most pages here also note what comes on the plate alongside the dish and how much it adds.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How do I calculate how many calories are in a dish I made?**
Answer: Add up the calories of all the ingredients in the pot by their raw weight, and divide by the number of servings it made. The two things that skew the calculation most are frying oil absorbed into the food and the actual serving size, which is almost always bigger than the serving the recipe talks about.

**Question: Does cooking change the nutritional value of food?**
Answer: Yes, in both directions. Heat destroys some vitamin C and the water-soluble B vitamins, but it also releases other compounds — the lycopene in [tomatoes](/en/food/tomato/) becomes more available with cooking, and legumes and grains lose some of the phytic acid that blocks mineral absorption when cooked.

**Question: What is the calorie difference between frying and baking?**
Answer: Food deep-fried in oil usually absorbs 8%-20% of its weight in oil, meaning an extra 70-180 calories per 100 grams. The same dish in the oven with a tablespoon of oil gets by with about 120 extra calories for the whole tray.

**Question: Is a traditional, oily dish necessarily unhealthy?**
Answer: No. Many traditional dishes are built around legumes, vegetables, [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/) and yogurt, and their fat profile is actually good. What matters is frequency and amount — [baklava](/en/recipes/turkey/baklava/) is a dessert for an occasion, [lentil soup](/en/recipes/turkey/mercimek-corbasi/) is an everyday meal.

**Question: What is the difference between Turkish and Persian cuisine?**
Answer: The structure of the meal. In [Turkish cuisine](/en/recipes/turkey/) the center is bread, grilled dishes and starters, and in [Persian cuisine](/en/recipes/iran/) it is rice and stew that cooks for hours. The balance is different too: Persian leans toward sweet-and-sour using dried lime and pomegranate molasses, and Turkish toward rich-and-tangy using yogurt, sumac and lemon.

**Question: Where are the soup recipes?**
Answer: In the [soups](/en/recipes/soups/) section, which is organized by type of dish rather than origin — chicken soup, vegetable soup, split pea, pumpkin, mushroom, French onion, minestrone, tomato, miso and tom yum. Soups identified with a particular cuisine appear within it, for example [Turkish lentil soup](/en/recipes/turkey/mercimek-corbasi/) and Moroccan [harira](/en/recipes/morocco/harira/).

**Question: How is Moroccan cuisine different from the other three cuisines on the site?**
Answer: In three ways. The base is [couscous](/en/recipes/morocco/couscous/) — rolled semolina — not rice or bread; the sourness comes from salt-preserving (preserved lemon, olives) rather than tamarind or dried lime; and it is the only one of the four that sprinkles sugar and cinnamon over a main course, as in [pastilla](/en/recipes/morocco/pastilla/). Full details in [Moroccan cuisine](/en/recipes/morocco/).

Answer: In [Tripolitan cuisine](/en/recipes/tripoli/), with ten recipes of the Jews of Libya adapted to ingredients available in Israel and a calorie estimate for a defined serving.
