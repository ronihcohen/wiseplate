In Israel people say "Tripolitan" (in Hebrew, "טריפוליטאי") about almost the entire cuisine of Libyan Jews, even when the family came from Benghazi, Homs or another town. Tripoli was the largest community and the name became a catch-all term. It is a Mediterranean and North African cuisine with a clear Italian layer: [couscous](/en/recipes/tripoli/couscous/), spicy meat stews and fish on one side; pasta and nokidess on the other. The base that recurs in almost every pot is [tomato](/en/food/tomato/) paste fried in oil with garlic, paprika and caraway until it darkens and loses its raw taste.

This page focuses on the versions preserved by Libyan Jews in Israel. All the ingredients are available in ordinary Israeli supermarket chains; for barley flour, harissa and ground caraway, a health food shelf or a spice shop is enough. Dishes that require intestine casings or special offal, like traditional usbana, are not on the list, because the goal here is a recipe you can actually make in a home Israeli kitchen.

### What defines Tripolitan flavor

* **Fried tomato paste, not just diluted.** Frying it in oil for two to three minutes caramelizes the sugars and removes the metallic taste. This is the step shared by [chraime](/en/recipes/tripoli/chraime/), [shorba](/en/recipes/tripoli/shorba/) and [arisa](/en/recipes/tripoli/arisa/).
* **Caraway instead of cumin.** The two seeds look similar but taste different. In Libyan chraime, ground caraway is the signature; cumin alone gives a different sauce.
* **Heat built in layers.** Hot paprika, shatta chili and harissa are not all required together. Start with a little and adjust at the end, because the liquid reduces and the heat concentrates.
* **A useful Italian influence.** Nokidess are relatives of gnocchi, and the small pasta in shorba is not a random modern addition. Italian rule in Libya left pasta as a completely local staple.
* **Fish and meat are not mixed.** The recipes here are built according to Libyan Jewish cuisine and kashrut: chraime is a separate pot; the meat stews contain no dairy products.

### A note on the calories

The calculation is done from the weight of the raw ingredients before cooking, divided by the stated number of servings. For frying oil, only the estimated amount absorbed is counted; in soups and stews, all the oil remaining in the pot is counted. That is why [mafrum](/en/recipes/tripoli/mafrum/) shows a clear range for frying, and [bazin](/en/recipes/tripoli/bazin/) is defined as a large, festive serving and not as a weightless "ball." A change in the fat percentage of the meat and the amount of oil can shift the result by 10%–20%.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: Are Tripolitan and Libyan the same thing?**
Answer: Tripoli is the capital of Libya, but in Israel "Tripolitan" has become a catch-all name for the cuisine of Libyan Jews. There are family and regional differences, so there is no single version of each dish.

**Question: Is all Tripolitan food spicy?**
Answer: No. Chraime and shorba are usually spicy, but couscous, nokidess and bazin can be well seasoned without burning. You can reduce the shatta and harissa without harming the structure of the recipe.

**Question: What is the "silk" in tbecha bsilk?**
Answer: Leaf beet — Swiss chard — and not the purple beet root. Both the leaves and the tender part of the stems are used, and they are cooked until very dark.

**Question: Which dishes are good for making ahead?**
Answer: Mafrum, tbecha bsilk, chraime and arisa improve after a night in the fridge. Nokidess are best eaten on the day they are made, and makroudh keeps in an airtight box for about two weeks.
