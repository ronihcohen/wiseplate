Iraqi cuisine grew up between two rivers, and that explains its two most prominent traits. The ancient agriculture of the Euphrates and Tigris basin gave it [rice](/en/food/rice/), wheat, [dates](/en/food/dates/) and freshwater fish; and its location on the trade routes between India and the Mediterranean brought in cardamom, [turmeric](/en/food/turmeric/), tamarind and pickled [mango](/en/food/mango/) — ingredients that barely appear among its neighbors to the west.

The result is a cuisine resting on three anchors: **sourness** — tamarind, dried lemon and fresh lemon in almost every stew; **baharat** — an aromatic, relatively sweet spice blend based on allspice, [cinnamon](/en/food/cinnamon/), cardamom and cloves; and **bread**, which in Iraq is not a side but a utensil — the [samoon](/en/recipes/iraq/samoon/) torn into the soup and used to soak up the sauce.

Many of the dishes here are known to Israelis not through Iraq but through Iraqi-Israeli cooking: [kubba hamusta](/en/recipes/iraq/kubba-hamusta/), [amba](/en/recipes/iraq/amba/) and [tbit](/en/recipes/iraq/tbit/) arrived with the great immigration from Iraq in the 1950s and blended into the local cuisine until their origins were almost forgotten.

### What characterizes Iraqi cuisine nutritionally

* **Sourness in every dish — and not just for flavor.** Tamarind and lemon appear in [bamia](/en/recipes/iraq/bamia/), [dolma](/en/recipes/iraq/dolma/) and [kubba hamusta](/en/recipes/iraq/kubba-hamusta/). Beyond balance, the [vitamin C](/en/food/supplements/vitamin-c/) in lemon improves the absorption of plant [iron](/en/food/iron/) from the legumes and herbs in the same bowl.
* **Legumes alongside the meat, not instead of it.** White beans in [tashreeb](/en/recipes/iraq/tashreeb/), [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/) in stews — a method that lowers the amount of meat per serving and raises the fiber. Tashreeb reaches 10 grams of fiber per serving.
* **White rice and white bread are the backdrop for almost everything.** This is the main drawback, and it recurs in [Persian cuisine](/en/recipes/iran/) too. A full Iraqi meal usually includes both rice and bread.
* **The fish.** [Masgouf](/en/recipes/iraq/masgouf/) is the dish with the best protein-to-calorie ratio here — 50 grams of protein in about 470 calories, with [omega-3](/en/food/supplements/omega-3/) and almost no carbohydrates.
* **[Dates](/en/food/dates/) instead of white sugar.** [Kleicha](/en/recipes/iraq/kleicha/) is sweetened mostly with fruit — almost all the sugar in it comes from the dates rather than white sugar, unlike [baklava](/en/recipes/turkey/baklava/) and [sholeh zard](/en/recipes/iran/shole-zard/).
* **Sodium.** [Amba](/en/recipes/iraq/amba/), [samoon](/en/recipes/iraq/samoon/) and pickles — each one on its own is reasonable, and together an Iraqi meal easily reaches a high amount of [sodium](/en/food/supplements/sodium/).

### Six ingredients that unlock this cuisine

| Ingredient | What it is | Reasonable substitute |
|---|---|---|
| Tamarind | A tart concentrate from the pods of the tamarind tree | [Lemon](/en/food/lemon/) juice + a teaspoon of silan (date syrup) |
| Iraqi baharat | An aromatic blend: allspice, cinnamon, cardamom, cloves, cumin | Commercial baharat in the same amount, adjusted to taste |
| Dried lemon | A lemon dried in the sun; deep acidity and a little bitterness | Lemon juice at the end of cooking |
| Cardamom | The hallmark of [kleicha](/en/recipes/iraq/kleicha/) and of Iraqi coffee | No real substitute |
| Fenugreek | The ingredient that gives [amba](/en/recipes/iraq/amba/) its signature aroma | No substitute — better to skip it |
| Fine semolina | The shell of [kubba](/en/recipes/iraq/kubba-hamusta/); absorbs water and stretches without tearing | Flour does not replace it — the dough will shrink |

### Where to start

The easiest way in is [bamia](/en/recipes/iraq/bamia/) or [tashreeb](/en/recipes/iraq/tashreeb/) — one-pot stews that demand time, not skill. [Masgouf](/en/recipes/iraq/masgouf/) is remarkably easy in the oven version, and [amba](/en/recipes/iraq/amba/) in the version here takes about a quarter of an hour of work plus about 20 minutes of cooking, followed by chilling. The dishes that really take practice are [kubba hamusta](/en/recipes/iraq/kubba-hamusta/), because of stretching the semolina shell, and [dolma](/en/recipes/iraq/dolma/), because of the hour of stuffing and the flip at the end.

Alongside this cuisine it is worth looking at its two neighbors on the site — [Turkish cuisine](/en/recipes/turkey/) and [Persian cuisine](/en/recipes/iran/) — which share many ingredients with it and arrange them completely differently. And [Moroccan cuisine](/en/recipes/morocco/), far from all of them, offers the sharpest comparison: Moroccan [dafina](/en/recipes/morocco/dafina/) and Iraqi [tbit](/en/recipes/iraq/tbit/) are exactly the same Sabbath dish, born of the same constraint in two communities that never met.

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: What is the national dish of Iraq?**
Answer: [Masgouf](/en/recipes/iraq/masgouf/) — a river fish split open and grilled upright facing an open fire. It is identified with the banks of the Tigris in Baghdad more than any other dish, although dishes like [dolma](/en/recipes/iraq/dolma/) and [quzi](/en/recipes/iraq/quzi/) are more common in homes.

**Question: What is the difference between Iraqi, Turkish and Persian cuisine?**
Answer: Iraqi cuisine relies on the sourness of tamarind and dried lemon, on the aromatic baharat blend, and on bread as an eating utensil. [Persian](/en/recipes/iran/) cuisine is built around rice and stew with a sweet-and-sour balance, and [Turkish](/en/recipes/turkey/) around bread, the grill and starters. Many shared ingredients, a different meal structure.

**Question: Where did amba come from?**
Answer: From India. Iraqi Jewish merchants who traded between Baghdad and Bombay in the 19th century encountered mango pickling there and brought it to Iraq, and the name *āmbā* — mango in Marathi — stuck. In the 1950s it reached Israel with the immigration from Iraq. The details are in [the amba recipe](/en/recipes/iraq/amba/).

**Question: Which Iraqi dishes suit vegetarians and vegans?**
Answer: [Amba](/en/recipes/iraq/amba/) is fully vegan, [samoon](/en/recipes/iraq/samoon/) is pareve if you leave out the milk powder, and [kleicha](/en/recipes/iraq/kleicha/) can be made vegan with oil and water and without the egg wash. [Dolma](/en/recipes/iraq/dolma/), [bamia](/en/recipes/iraq/bamia/) and [tashreeb](/en/recipes/iraq/tashreeb/) come in traditional meatless versions in which legumes take its place.

**Question: What is Iraqi baharat and how is it different from other baharat?**
Answer: A blend based on allspice, cinnamon, cardamom, cloves, cumin and coriander. It is sweeter and more aromatic than the Levantine blends and less spicy than them, which is why Iraqi stews get a "sweetish" scent even when they contain no sweetener at all.
