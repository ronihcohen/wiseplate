## Iraqi Dolma

**In Iraqi Arabic: *دولمة*, from the Turkish *dolma* — "stuffed."** In Hebrew it is written "דולמה" and also "דולמא". Almost every cuisine in the region has its own dolma, but the Iraqi version stands apart from all of them in three ways: the number of vegetable types in a single pot, the sweet-and-sour sauce, sometimes made from tamarind, and the way it is served — the whole pot is flipped onto a platter like a cake.

While in [Turkey](/en/recipes/turkey/) dolma is usually a single kind of vegetable, and in Iran it is mostly grape leaves, Iraqi dolma is a whole platter dish: grape leaves or chard, [eggplants](/en/food/eggplant/), [zucchini](/en/food/zucchini/), [peppers](/en/food/red-pepper/), [tomatoes](/en/food/tomato/) and onion — all in the same pot, all with the same filling, and all soaking up the same sauce.

**Prep time:** 1 hour · **Cook time:** 1½ hours · **Total:** about 2½ hours · **Yield:** 8 servings · **Difficulty:** high

### Why the pot gets flipped, and why it's not just for show

Iraqi dolma is cooked in a pot arranged in layers: a base of sliced [onion](/en/food/onion/) or potatoes that prevents scorching, and above it the stuffed vegetables in a fixed order — the heavy ones at the bottom, the delicate ones on top. A heavy plate is placed on top to press everything down, and the dish cooks covered for an hour and a half. At the end, the whole pot is turned over onto a platter.

This order has a physical logic. The bottom vegetables absorb most of the sauce and cook in a concentrated liquid, while the top ones cook mainly in steam. Flipping brings the soaked layer to the top, redistributing the liquid across the platter. The plate pressing down from above keeps the filling from floating out of the vegetables as the rice swells.

### Ingredients (for 8 servings)

**For the filling:**
* 2 cups short-grain or basmati [rice](/en/food/rice/), rinsed and soaked for 30 minutes
* 400 grams ground [lamb](/en/food/lamb/) or beef
* 1 finely chopped [onion](/en/food/onion/), a handful of parsley, a handful of dill
* 2 tablespoons [tomato](/en/food/tomato/) paste
* 1 teaspoon Iraqi baharat, 1 teaspoon [turmeric](/en/food/turmeric/), salt and pepper
* 3 tablespoons [olive oil](/en/food/olive-oil/)

**For the vegetables:**
* 2 small [eggplants](/en/food/eggplant/), 2 [zucchini](/en/food/zucchini/), 2 [peppers](/en/food/red-pepper/), 3 [tomatoes](/en/food/tomato/), 1 large onion, 15-20 grape or chard leaves

**For the sauce:**
* 3 tablespoons [lemon](/en/food/lemon/) juice + 1 teaspoon silan (date syrup), as an easy-to-find substitute for tamarind
* 1 tablespoon tomato paste, 1 tablespoon oil, 500 ml hot water, salt

### Choosing ingredients and substitutes

You can buy grape leaves in a jar; rinse off the brine and taste before salting the filling. Fresh chard is an easy-to-find alternative: remove the thick stem and blanch the leaves for about a minute until pliable. For the sauce, use three tablespoons of lemon juice and a teaspoon of silan; this is a substitute for tamarind, not the same flavor. A teaspoon of regular baharat works in place of a dedicated Iraqi blend.

### Method

1. **Prepare the vegetables.** Halve the eggplants and zucchini lengthwise and hollow them out with a corer, leaving a wall of about half a centimeter. Cut a "cap" off the tomatoes and peppers and hollow them out. Boil the onion for 5 minutes and separate it into layers. Blanch the grape leaves for 30 seconds.
2. **Make the filling.** Mix all the filling ingredients. **The rice stays raw** — it will cook inside the vegetables.
3. **Fill to two-thirds.** This is the one rule you must not break: the rice swells, and a full stuffing will burst the vegetable. Roll the leaves tightly, but follow the same rule.
4. **Line the pot.** Arrange slices of onion or potato in the bottom of a wide pot, and on top of them the stuffed vegetables — eggplants and zucchini at the bottom, peppers and tomatoes in the middle, stuffed leaves on top.
5. **Pour in the sauce and press down.** Mix the sauce ingredients and pour to about two-thirds of the height of the vegetables — depending on the width of the pot, you may need 700-800 ml of water rather than 500. Place a heavy inverted plate on top.
6. **Cook.** Bring to a boil, lower to very low heat, and cook covered for 75-90 minutes until the rice is tender and the liquid is almost absorbed.
7. **Flip.** Let rest for 10 minutes, remove the plate, place a large platter over the pot and turn it over in one motion.

### Nutrition facts and calorie estimate

The recommended serving is about 300 grams — about 5-6 stuffed pieces of different kinds.

| Component | Per serving (300 grams) |
|---|---|
| **Calories** | **about 420** |
| Protein | about 14 grams |
| Carbohydrates | about 51 grams |
| Of which fiber | about 5 grams |
| Fat | about 19 grams |

This dish demonstrates a principle that recurs in all three cuisines on the site: when the vegetable is the vessel rather than the side, the dish changes. Here 400 grams of meat is divided among eight diners — about 50 grams per serving — and vegetables and rice fill the rest of the volume. The result is about 5 grams of fiber per serving, a high figure for a meat-and-rice dish, and a fairly moderate amount of red meat (see [red meat](/en/food/red-meat/)).

The weak points are the oil and the white rice. Three tablespoons of oil in the filling and another tablespoon in the sauce come to about 490 calories spread across the servings, and the rice adds carbohydrate without fiber. Two adjustments work well: replacing half the rice with bulgur or [quinoa](/en/food/quinoa/), and cutting the oil in the filling to one tablespoon — the vegetables release enough liquid.

### Variations and tips

* **Vegan version:** skip the meat and add cooked [chickpeas](/en/food/chickpeas/), pine nuts or walnuts, and raisins to the filling. This version is traditional and is also eaten cold.
* **The hard vegetables to stuff:** tomatoes tend to fall apart. Choose firm tomatoes and place them in the center of the pot, supported on all sides.
* **Tamarind:** it is what makes dolma Iraqi. An accepted substitute is lemon juice with a teaspoon of silan, which gives a similar sweet-sour balance.
* **Make ahead:** you can stuff the vegetables a day ahead, keep them in the fridge, and cook the next day.

### Summary

Iraqi dolma is a platter dish: six kinds of vegetables, one filling, one sweet-and-sour sauce, and a pot that gets flipped at the end. About 420 calories per serving with 5 grams of fiber and a moderate amount of meat. It takes an hour of hands-on work, which is why it is a dish for entertaining rather than an everyday one. A much quicker Iraqi dish with a similar sweet-and-sour sauce is [bamia](/en/recipes/iraq/bamia/). More recipes in [Iraqi cuisine](/en/recipes/iraq/).

### Frequently asked questions

**Question: How many calories are in Iraqi dolma?**
Answer: A serving of about 300 grams, meaning 5-6 stuffed pieces, is about 420 calories with about 14 grams of protein and 5 grams of fiber. Cutting the oil in the filling to one tablespoon removes about 40 calories per serving.

**Question: What is the difference between Iraqi dolma and Turkish or Greek dolma?**
Answer: The Iraqi version combines six kinds of vegetables in the same pot and is served flipped onto a platter, and its sauce gets its sweet-sour taste from tamarind. The Turkish and Greek versions are usually based on a single vegetable — mainly grape leaves — and seasoned with lemon and olive oil, without the tamarind.

**Question: Why do the vegetables burst during cooking?**
Answer: Overfilling. The rice in the filling is raw and swells to about two and a half times its volume, so each vegetable is filled only to two-thirds. A second reason is heat that is too high, causing a rolling boil that moves the vegetables around in the pot.

**Question: Can you make dolma without meat?**
Answer: Yes, and it is a fully traditional version. Replace the meat with cooked chickpeas, nuts and raisins, and slightly increase the amount of herbs. The vegetarian version is also eaten cold, at room temperature, like Turkish [zeytinyağlı](/en/recipes/turkey/imam-bayildi/) dishes.

**Question: What do you do if the pot doesn't flip out neatly?**
Answer: There are usually two reasons — the dish wasn't given 10 minutes of rest before flipping, or the bottom wasn't lined. A lining of onion or potato slices prevents sticking, and the rest lets the liquid absorb and the dish set.
